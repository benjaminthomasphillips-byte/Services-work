# Prompt for an independent commission (for other AI systems)

Paste everything below the line into a deep-research capable assistant. It is written to be self-contained. It deliberately gives no conclusions or preferred frameworks, so the result is independent.

---

## Your brief

I invest in and help run services businesses. I want an independent, rigorous piece of work on one question:

**How should a market-leading AI-native services business be designed and built, starting in 2027 with the knowledge, tools and institutions that exist today, and built to lead through 2035 and beyond?**

I have done my own thinking on this. I am deliberately not sharing it, because I want your independent view. Do not assume any particular answer. Work it out.

### The questions I want answered

1. **The operating model.** What is the ideal operating model for such a firm? Cover:
   - the processes it runs;
   - the infrastructure and technology it needs, including the back office;
   - the team and roles, and how people and AI agents are organised together;
   - how decisions are made;
   - how quality is assured.
2. **Turning work into something AI can do.** Do you need to list everything the firm does (say, its 200 services), turn each into a product, find the edge cases and automate as much as possible? Or is there a better way in?
   - It has been suggested that automating any task requires four things: state or context an agent can read; a written playbook for the task; a way to write (to act in systems); and a verifier. Is that right, sufficient, or wrong?
3. **Organising principles.** What rules and principles should the firm follow, for both people and AI?
4. **Continual self-improvement.** What would a services firm that keeps improving itself through AI actually look like? Recursive self-improvement is one framing.
   - Tools that monitor work, map processes and find bottlenecks have been criticised as static, as have one-off projects such as "let's automate billing".
   - What does a firm that improves constantly need?
   - Should the AI have "full" context: all calls, emails and meetings?
   - What would happen as it compounds, and what could go wrong?
5. **Commoditisation.** Does AI-driven self-improvement turn ordinary services work into a commodity? If so, what is left that is scarce and valuable?
6. **Building it.** How would you actually build this from 2027? Who needs to believe in it ("be AI-pilled") and who does not?
7. **Variation.** How does the design change across different kinds of services business? I want real work on this, not a paragraph. See "Types of business and work" below.
8. **Incumbents.** How would an existing, messy incumbent, with many systems, tacit knowledge and an established partnership or ownership, get to the design you propose? What does that take, and what has to be thrown away?
9. **Where to build or invest.** What does all of this teach about the most promising areas to build or invest in?

### Types of business and work

The right design almost certainly differs by type of business and type of work. Build a map of services along the dimensions that matter, then design for each region of it. The dimensions should include, at least:

- **Client size and sophistication:**
  - consumers and households;
  - small businesses;
  - the middle market;
  - large enterprises with their own in-house teams and AI.
- **Rhythm of the work:**
  - routine and recurring;
  - continuous;
  - ad hoc, episodic or once in a lifetime;
  - project-based.
- **Nature of the judgement:**
  - work with a right or wrong answer that can be checked;
  - work where the answer can only be known late, or never;
  - judgement-based, contested or strategic work.
- **What is produced:**
  - information or a decision;
  - money moved or legal status changed;
  - a physical change in the world;
  - a human act such as care or comfort.
- **The parties:**
  - one client whose interests are aligned with the firm;
  - several parties;
  - an opponent on the other side;
  - a payer who is not the person served.
- **Rules and place:**
  - regulated or not;
  - local or global;
  - one jurisdiction or many.
- **Any other dimension your research shows matters more.**

Then:

- Say which few dimensions actually drive the design, and which make little difference.
- Give a distinct design, not a variation in wording, for each important region of the map: for example, enterprise against middle market against small business, and routine against ad hoc, and checkable against judgement-based.
- Work through at least eight concrete services chosen to span the map as widely as possible, and say why you chose them. Include some that are likely to break your design.
- For each, show:
  - what the firm sells;
  - where its people and agents sit;
  - how it is organised;
  - how it makes money;
  - what is different from the general design.

### Mistakes to avoid

Work of this kind tends to go wrong in predictable ways. Guard against each of these:

1. **Being backward-looking.** Research describes what firms did with the tools of the last few years. The firm you design has to win with the tools of 2027 and lead in 2035, so do not let today's examples set the limits of the design. Use the research as raw material, then look forward:
   - what capabilities, costs and behaviours are likely by 2030 and 2035;
   - what that makes possible that nobody has built yet;
   - what a firm would need to be ready for it.

   Feel free to infer and conjecture about the future, as long as each step is grounded in logic and labelled as conjecture.
2. **Being timid and evidence-bound.** Treating missing evidence as a reason to say nothing produces a cautious, committee-ready plan instead of a design. Where evidence runs out, reason forward.
3. **The opposite: bold calls with nothing behind them.** Sharp forecasts made in one pass, without mechanism, base rates or counter-arguments, are as useless as timidity. Check your calls before you rely on them.
4. **Automating today's work instead of redesigning it.** Listing what a firm does today and asking which tasks AI can do keeps the old shape of the work. Also ask what the service would look like if designed from the client's outcome with today's tools, and how that changes the firm's boundaries. Consider several levels of ambition, and say which wins and why.
5. **Narrowing too early.** Do not seize on one idea (one business model, one pricing approach, one sector) and spend the work elaborating it. Explore the space first, and choose later, with reasons.
6. **Letting today's constraints set the design.** Treating current regulation, insurance or institutional habits as the starting point shrinks the design before it has been explored. Design first, then apply the constraints as a stress test, and say which ones change the design and how.
7. **Starting from incumbents' point of view.** Asking only established practitioners produces "the incumbent wins; sell to us". Balance the perspectives you consult: builders of new firms, investors of different kinds, practitioners, clients, regulators and people who study organisations.
8. **Designing only the inside of the firm, or only the strategy.** An operating model without decisions on ownership, revenue, risk and distribution is incomplete. A strategy without an operating model is not a design. Cover both.
9. **Marking your own work.** If you score your design against your own criteria, you will flatter it. Use independent critique: separate reviewers, or tests set before the design was written, and report where the design failed them.
10. **Losing the original question.** Re-read this brief before you finish, and check you have answered every question in it.

### Research first, and make it current

Research is the starting point, not the limit (see the first mistake above). Before designing, commission or carry out independent research so the design rests on the latest available information. The field moves fast, so prioritise the most recent material and give a date for everything you cite. In particular:

- **Build a library of real cases.** What firms have actually done with AI in services work:
  - AI-native services firms built from scratch;
  - AI-enabled roll-ups and holding companies that buy and transform services firms;
  - incumbents redesigning themselves;
  - software and platform companies moving into services, or services into software.

  For each case, separate:
  - the company's own claim;
  - any independently observed result;
  - your interpretation.

  Record who is speaking and what they gain: an operator describing their own firm, a vendor, an investor with a position, a journalist, a regulator or an academic.
- **Look for failures, reversals and abandoned efforts, not only successes.** Successes are over-reported.
- **Research the economics:**
  - who has kept the savings from AI, and who has passed them to clients;
  - pricing changes;
  - filed results of listed services firms;
  - valuations.
- **Research the people side:**
  - what has happened to roles, junior hiring, training routes, pay and incentives;
  - how adoption was led, and what resistance looked like.
- **Research the rules:**
  - what regulators, professional bodies and insurers now say about AI-produced work, supervision, sign-off and liability;
  - what the law says about recording and monitoring work;
  - who may own regulated firms;
  - pending reforms with dates.
- **Research the technology and infrastructure in production:**
  - how firms run agents;
  - how they check agents' work;
  - how they keep improving them;
  - what it costs;
  - what is built and what is bought.
- **Research the theory.** Recent economics and organisation research on firm boundaries, coordination and organisation design when AI agents do much of the work, plus relevant older traditions (lean, safety science, cybernetics).
- **Go beyond English-language and US sources** where useful. Include Europe, the UK, Japan, China and elsewhere.

Prefer primary sources: filings, regulators' own pages, firms' own engineering or operating write-ups, research papers, job advertisements (which reveal real organisation charts), court and tribunal records. Use commentary mainly to find primary material.

If you can run several researchers or agents in parallel, do so, and say how you organised them. If you cannot access something (paywalls, sign-in walls), say so by name. Never summarise what you have not read.

### Standard of reasoning

Evidence in this field is thin and moves fast, and the design is for 2027 to 2035, so forward-looking inference and well-grounded conjecture are expected, not merely allowed. Use logic where data runs out. But every claim the design depends on should state:

- the claim itself;
- the mechanism: why it would happen, and who gains;
- what is visible today, and how fast comparable shifts have gone historically;
- the strongest counter-argument;
- what follows for the design, and whether the design survives if the claim is wrong.

Label each substantive claim as one of:
- **seen:** exists somewhere today, with a source;
- **reasoned:** follows from logic;
- **conjecture:** plausible towards 2035.

Say how confident you are and why. Do not invent statistics. If a number is an estimate, say so. Separate direction from timing and magnitude.

Test your design hard:
- Apply it to several concrete services that differ from one another as much as possible, and say which you chose and why.
- Seek out the strongest objections, including from practitioners in those services.
- Show where the design breaks, not just where it holds.
- Consider a range of plausible 2035 landscapes, not one forecast, and say what the design must do in each.

Avoid generic consulting frameworks and boilerplate. I value specific, decision-changing observations over comprehensive lists.

### What to hand back

A single report, in plain English, structured as:

1. **The answer in brief.** What you conclude, your weakest premise, and what would change your view.
2. **Research summary.** What you found, with dated citations and a note on how much weight each strand can bear.
3. **The operating model.** The ideal design, in enough detail to act on:
   - organisation, roles, people and agents;
   - principles;
   - processes;
   - technology and back office;
   - how it improves itself;
   - economics.
4. **How it is built,** from 2027 to 2035, including how a firm reaches clients and grows.
5. **The map of services, and a design for each region,** with at least eight worked examples, and which dimensions drive the design most.
6. **The incumbent retrofit.**
7. **Where to build or invest.**
8. **Challenges, counter-arguments and open questions,** including where your own design is weakest.
9. **Sources:** what you read in full, in part, and could not access.

Take whatever length the substance needs, but put the most important conclusions first.
