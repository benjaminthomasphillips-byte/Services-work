# Design C: operator under mandate

Stage 4 of the run. Written 23 November 2026. One designer, working alone; the other two designs were not read. Nothing was written to Notion.

**Stance.** Own one firm as the operating platform. Run work for other firms under a mandate, on their systems, under their name and signature, without buying them. This is the hybrid A003 proposes and nobody has tested (R-LIB-STRUCTURE-04). I argue it as its best advocate would, and say at the end where it is weakest.

**Read.** The brief, the ledger and both inventories in full. Evidence rows opened for detail: R-LIB-STRUCTURE-03/04/21, R-LIB-ECONOMICS-22/30/31/32/33, R-LIB-IMPROVE-TECH-34, R-WEB-OWNERSHIP-09/27, R-WEB-ROLLUPS-12/32, R-WEB-COMMERCIAL-18. Other rows are cited from the ledger and were not re-checked.

**Tags.** [source says] = a row or inventory source states it. [inferred] = my reasoning. "Design choice" = a parameter I set; it is not evidence. Sources S1 to S21 are in inventory A; S-B-01 to S-B-17 in inventory B.

**How sure.** Sure of the legal frame. Fairly sure of the diagnosis: no result exists, debt kills, the signature stays local. Not sure the mandate earns more than an outsourcer's margin. No case shows a mandate working or failing on this kind of work. The whole design is a proposal.

## The design in one page

1. **The group owns one firm per trade, outright, and buys no others at the start.** The first is a UK accountancy practice like reference firm A. It is the laboratory, the licensed chassis and the place the group carries full liability on its own methods.
2. **It sells one thing to other firms: prepared work, delivered to their review queue.** The unit is a whole case (a bookkeeping month, a VAT quarter, a set of accounts ready for review). The other firm keeps its clients, prices, name, sign-off and filing. The group never files, pays or sends in another firm's name.
3. **It is paid per unit, at a price below the firm's own measured cost.** No fixed project fee, because under a fixed fee the client keeps the saving (R-LIB-ECONOMICS-22). No gain-share on a disputed baseline.
4. **The freed hours at the owned firm are the capacity that serves the mandates.** This is what makes the parts fit. Nobody at the owned firm is cut; their hours are sold to firms that cannot hire.
5. **Each mandate carries a right to buy a stake later, at a formula fixed on pre-mandate earnings.** The mandate is two years of paid diligence on the work itself. The group buys only what it has seen from the inside.
6. **Capital goes into capability, not goodwill.** One purchase, no acquisition debt.

Why this and not a roll-up. [source says] No AI roll-up has published an audited result (ledger finding 1). Debt and over-priced buying are where roll-ups die (R-WEB-OWNERSHIP-19; R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-28). Accountancy entry is crowded: 46% of surveyed UK mid-tier firms are PE-backed (R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-26). The licensed person stays accountable whoever owns the firm (R-WEB-LAW-16). [inferred] If the gain is unproven, the price is high and the signature does not move, the cheapest way to find out is to own one firm and rent your way into the rest.

## Part 1. Purpose and where it plays

**Decision.** The group exists to make prepared professional work cheaper and better checked than a small firm can make it alone, and to sell that to firms that are short of people. It starts in UK accountancy compliance work (bookkeeping, VAT, payroll changes, accounts and tax preparation). A second trade (residential property management) follows only when a second platform firm is owned. It will not do audit, advice sold on judgement, or work on any system whose vendor refuses a write path. It will not rely on raising client prices. Horizon: permanent or long-dated capital, with no planned exit date.

**Reason.**
- [source says] 48% of UK accountants say lack of capacity holds back growth (inventory A, S18; a vendor survey, search summary). New statutory work arrived in April 2026 (S17). A capacity-bound firm is where a saving can become sold capacity (R-LIB-ECONOMICS-09; a rival buyer's claim).
- [source says] Most of firm A's income is fixed fee with no tender (inventory A, section 1). A saving stays with the firm until it reprices, so there is something to share.
- [source says] Price rises at consolidated local firms ended in regulation (R-WEB-OWNERSHIP-25). The mandate has no pricing power over end clients, so the temptation is absent.
- [source says] A sale process made Constellation's managers cut long-term spending (R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-11). A five-year mandate cannot be honoured by an owner who must sell in four.

**The bet.** That a firm which owns one practice, and so lives with its own output through renewal seasons, can prepare work for other practices better than an outsourcer can, for long enough to earn back a small capital outlay. [inferred] It does not bet on a moat.

**Evidence.** Rules and filings for the diagnosis. None for the bet. Dwelly plans the same line and has not started it (R-WEB-ROLLUPS-12).

**Scale.** One: one owned practice, no mandates until its own methods hold. Five: one owned, four mandates, all UK accountancy, one of them possibly the money work of a property manager (see "Applied"). Thirty: two or three owned platform firms, one per trade; the rest mandates, some converted to minority stakes.

## Part 2. Ownership, capital and legal form

**Decision.**

| Entity | Owns or does | Owned by |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Holding company | Capital, the stakes, the options | The sponsor, with staff and partner units |
| Services company | Platform, playbooks, evaluation sets, engineers, the method | Holding company, 100% |
| Platform firm (the owned practice) | Clients, practitioners, delivery pods, the permissions (institute membership, money-laundering supervision, HMRC agent status, insurance). Signs mandate contracts as subcontractor. | Holding company in the majority; partners keep a real minority. Percentage open; disclosed designs run from 40 to 60% bought (R-WEB-OWNERSHIP-17) to a 50/50 split (R-WEB-OWNERSHIP-27). |
| Audit entity | The twelve audits | Its responsible individual; not the group (R-WEB-LAW-29/30). Ring-fenced, served under a services agreement, or handed on. |
| Mandate firms | Everything they own today | Their own partners. The group holds a contract and an option. |

- **Why own a firm at all.** [inferred] A practice holds a stack of permissions, not one signature (inventory A, section 3). A company with none of them cannot lawfully or credibly prepare tax work for others. Subcontracting between practices is ordinary; the platform firm is that subcontractor. And Chroma's objection is that a deployment firm has only ability, while an owner has access, authority and time (R-LIB-STRUCTURE-21). Owning one firm supplies all four once.
- **Charging.** The services company charges the platform firm for technology and method under a services agreement. [source says] This is the documented route: CBIZ booked $651.2m of such fees in 2025 (R-WEB-OWNERSHIP-09). The platform firm pays the same unit rates as a mandate firm. Design choice: one price list, so the owned firm cannot be subsidised.
- **Debt.** None on the platform firm. None sized on AI savings. Sellers are paid in cash at completion and in group units; no bank debt at the first firm. [source says] The failures on file are debt-funded (R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-28; R-WEB-OWNERSHIP-31/32/33).
- **Sellers.** Stay for at least three years. No earn-out on the firm's own hours or revenue, because that rewards steady hours in the years a redesign cuts them (ledger Part 2, the researcher's inference). Deferred pay is tied to client retention and held in group units for about four years (R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-11).
- **The mandate contract.** Design choices: five-year term; either side may leave after eighteen months; scope is named cases; unit prices with a stated step-down at each anniversary; a right for the group to buy a stake at a formula on pre-mandate earnings, usable only after two annual cycles. On exit the firm keeps its client rule files and a frozen copy of the playbooks it used.
- **Technology ownership.** The services company owns shared playbooks and evaluation sets. Each firm owns its client data and its client-specific rules. [source says] A method can be owned and licensed (R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-06).

**Evidence.** Strong on the structure (R-WEB-LAW-32; R-WEB-OWNERSHIP-09). None on mandate terms, option pricing or what sellers accept. The ICAEW proposal that investor-controlled firms lose the "Chartered Accountants" title is open until 2027 (R-WEB-LAW-31); it would hit the platform firm and no mandate firm.

**Open.** How much a transition year can carry (R-LIB-STRUCTURE-08). Prices paid: only brokers' marketing at 0.8 to 1.3 times recurring fees for small practices (inventory A, S19). Whether a UK service transfer of this kind moves staff to the provider by law [inferred, general knowledge of the transfer regulations; not checked in this run]. Take advice before the first contract.

**Scale.** One: three entities plus the audit ring-fence. Five: the same, four contracts. Thirty: a platform firm per trade, each with its own permissions; minority stakes held through the holding company; approval needed for stakes above 10% in law firms and for any FCA-authorised firm (R-WEB-LAW-26/33).

## Part 3. Governance and decision rights

**Decision.** Rights follow liability. Whoever answers to a regulator or a client can stop the work. The centre can stop its own agents anywhere. It can never order a mandate firm; it can only decline to serve.

| Decision | Centre (services company) | Platform firm (owned) | Mandate firm | Agent | Who can stop it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Capital, buying a firm, exercising an option | Holding board decides | Advises | Sells or not | Never | Holding board |
| Appointing and paying the firm's head | Decides | n/a | Its partners | Never | n/a |
| Taking on or dropping an end client | No say | Firm's partner | Firm's partner | Never | The firm's partner |
| End-client price | No say | Firm's partner | Firm's partner | Never | The firm's partner |
| Signing or filing work | Never | Named signatory | Named signatory | Never | The signatory |
| Sending anything that commits the firm | Never for a mandate firm | Named owner approves | Named owner approves | Only templated, non-committing messages inside written limits | The firm's workflow owner |
| Preparing a unit of work | Pod lead | Pod lead | Receives it | Yes, inside limits | Pod lead; signatory for their own clients |
| Client-specific rules | Proposes | Workflow owner approves | Workflow owner approves | Proposes | The firm |
| Shared playbook or check | Release board approves | One vote | Consulted; may stay on the old version one quarter | Proposes; cannot read or edit checks | Head of quality |
| Agent register, limits, budgets | Head of quality sets | Sponsor per agent | May refuse any agent on its work | Never | Head of quality; any signatory for their work |
| Accepting a mandate, or a case into scope | Centre and platform firm both must agree | Its partner (it carries the subcontract liability) | Its managing partner | Never | Either side |
| Serious incident | Head of quality leads | Signatory notifies client and insurer | Its signatory notifies its client and insurer | Halts and reports | n/a |
| Staff recording | Sets the group rule | Consults staff | Its own decision | Never | Any employee, for their own work, by the pause control |

- The head of quality reports to the holding board, not the chief executive. [source says] The FCA found decisions about regulated firms taken by unregulated boards (R-WEB-OWNERSHIP-20).
- Board: sponsor, chief executive, the platform firm's managing partner, one independent with a professional licence. Risk and client acceptance never go to the holding board.
- [source says] Constellation's one hard power is replacing the manager (R-WEB-GOVERNANCE-01). [inferred] In a mandate the group lacks even that. Its only lever is the contract and the right to walk away. That is a real weakness, named in the last section.

**Evidence.** The liability side is rule (R-WEB-LAW-16/18). The table is a design choice with no AI-era precedent (ledger Part 3).

**Scale.** One: the table's first two columns. Five: a mandate council meets quarterly, one seat per firm. Thirty: a head per trade holds the centre's column; Leonard ran out of span at about thirty units (R-WEB-GOVERNANCE-07).

## Part 4. The acquisition engine, which here is mostly a mandate engine

**Decision.** Two pipelines, one team. Buying is rare; signing mandates is the repeatable function.

| Stage | Buying a platform firm | Signing a mandate |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sourcing | One firm per trade. Capacity-bound, modern ledgers, a head who wants this. | Capacity-bound firms; firms owned by other consolidators; firms that will never sell. Referred by the platform firm's partners. |
| Screen | Light inventory from the practice-management export; the task test; write path; insurer's view | The same sheet, on the cases in scope only |
| Diligence on the work | Two to four weeks on the floor before closing (Chroma's plan, R-LIB-STRUCTURE-20). Sample past files; time today's reviews; count exceptions by cause. | A paid baseline month: unit counts, minutes per unit, review minutes, error and rework found on a sample |
| Price | Three scenarios, rivals adopting AI in the base (R-LIB-ECONOMICS-32). Pay for none of the improvement. | Unit price below measured cost; step-down fixed; option formula fixed |
| Integration | Finance, reporting and data first, agents later (ledger Part 4 assumption) | One case live in shadow, then a second. Nothing migrated. |
| Measures | Client and staff retention; months to first live case | Months to first accepted unit; review minutes per unit; cost of this launch against the first |

**Reason.**
- [source says] Integration is a staffed function at Dwelly, about five businesses per launch manager (R-WEB-ROLLUPS-08/09). I copy the ratio as a design choice.
- [source says] US Office Products bought 230 firms without shared systems and failed (R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-33). The mandate cannot outrun its system: a firm that is not served is not counted.
- [inferred] The mandate is the diligence A003 says nobody practises. After two annual cycles the group knows the target's exception rates, review quality and client behaviour. A buyer in an auction knows none of these.
- [source says] The number to ask for is the cost of the latest firm against the first; nobody has published it (ledger Part 4). The mandate makes it a monthly figure, because each launch has its own contract and its own cost code.

**Evidence.** Dwelly's advertisements for structure, not results. None for mandate sales cycles.

**Scale.** One: no engine; the chief executive does it. Five: a mandate lead and one launch manager. Thirty: six launch managers, a post-mortem a year after each launch (R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-09).

## Part 5. Clients and the commercial side

There are two kinds of client. Keep them apart.

**End clients** (the small businesses and households).

- The local firm owns the relationship, the brand and the price, in both owned and mandate firms. The group has no brand an end client sees.
- Told in the engagement letter, before work: that AI is used, on which work, that a named subcontractor prepares work, and that their material is not used to train models (R-WEB-COMMERCIAL-26; R-WEB-LAW-12; inventory A, S9). For a regulated surveying firm: in writing and in advance, with redress and an opt-out (inventory B, S-B-04). So every case must still run the old way for a client who refuses.
- Prices held. New pricing is tested on new services first, such as the quarterly digital-tax work (ledger Part 5 assumption).
- If the end client has the same tools: [source says] one in five has checked their accountant with AI (R-WEB-COMMERCIAL-25; a US vendor survey). [inferred] The firm answers by selling the check and the filing status, not the typing.

**Firm clients** (the mandate firms).

- What is sold: review-ready units on their systems, at a unit price, with a published acceptance standard. Units and pricing by step have precedent (R-WEB-COMMERCIAL-21).
- Who sells: the platform firm's partners, to their peers. [source says] Agents "cannot reliably generate demand" (R-LIB-GOVERNANCE-14).
- No cross-selling to a mandate firm's clients, ever, in the contract. [source says] Paying for steered clients is treated as a conflict (R-WEB-OWNERSHIP-21). [inferred] A mandate firm that fears for its clients will not sign.
- What happens when the firm has the same tools: it leaves, as AT&T did with its panel (R-LIB-ECONOMICS-15). The contract expects this: step-down prices, an exit after eighteen months, and the option as the group's hedge.

**Evidence.** Rules for disclosure. None on what small firms will pay per unit, or whether partners can sell this to peers.

**Scale.** One: end clients only. Five: four firm clients, each sold by a partner. Thirty: a small commercial team under the mandate lead; the price list is public to members.

## Part 6. Delivery (Q1 and Q2)

**Q1. Decision: a light full inventory as a screen, then whole cases, never 200 products.**

- [source says] No case began from a full inventory and reported (ledger Q1). Every case started narrow. Both inventories found the light list cheap and worth doing, because it showed that a few shared gaps block most lines (inventory A, section 4; inventory B, section 4).
- **Unit.** Four units for four jobs: the *line* for screening; the *case* for building and for selling (a bookkeeping month, a repair from report to paid invoice); the *step* for limits and checks; the *decision* for sign-off. [source says] The firm has "perhaps a dozen real cases"; the row is the wrong unit to build (inventory B, section 4).
- **Recorded per case:** trigger; inputs and where they live; playbook version; client rule file; limits; owner; exception classes with counts; the check and who runs it; undo; test set; unit cost.
- **Exceptions** are classed by cause: missing input, blocked tool, policy, judgement (R-LIB-DELIVERY-12/13). Each class has a route and a named receiver.
- **Kept true by the work.** Each new exception class and each change in law amends a row. The whole sheet is re-scored at each model release.
- **"Product"** means a case with a fixed procedure, a unit price and an outside reader. [source says] It fits about ten of 123 lines at firm B (inventory B, section 4). [inferred] In this design the product is sold to the firm, not to the end client. That is where a unit price belongs: the firm buys parts; its client buys a bundle.

**Q2. Verdict on Ben's four: necessary, not sufficient** (ledger section 3). The test I would apply has thirteen items. Ten are the inventories' own. Two were added by inventory A. One is specific to this stance.

| # | Item | Pass means |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Readable state | Inputs sit in a system the agent may read |
| 2 | Written playbook | Procedure and exception routes written by the people who do the work (R-WEB-SELFIMPROVE-26) |
| 3 | A way to write | A typed, permitted write path |
| 4 | Independent verifier | A check the maker cannot reach: an outside balance, separate code, or a person who judges before seeing the agent's reasoning (R-WEB-SELFIMPROVE-24; R-WEB-GOVERNANCE-21) |
| 5 | Trigger | A clear start, with complete inputs (R-LIB-DELIVERY-05) |
| 6 | Authority and limits | Stated in money, contact and scope; enforced by the system |
| 7 | Named owner | A person has agreed to own the agent's version, not merely the task today (inventory A's correction) |
| 8 | Undo | Reversible, or gated before the irreversible step |
| 9 | Test set | Real past cases, held where the agent cannot edit them |
| 10 | Volume | Enough units to pay for build and upkeep. No threshold exists; judged. |
| 11 | Inputs arrive in time | The client or third party is not the critical path, or the case includes the chase |
| 12 | Outside body permits the write | For example HMRC allows its interfaces, not automation of its web pages (inventory A, S10) |
| 13 | Third-party operation permitted | The firm's client terms, insurer and system vendor allow a subcontractor to prepare the work |

**Scoring.** Y, P or N per item. Any N on 3, 4, 8, 12 or 13 blocks handover. Status is one of four: hand over now; hand over with a designed check; assist only; leave with people. A firm's score is three numbers: share of hours by status; the histogram of binding items; and the number of things that must be built once. For firm B that last number is about four (inventory B, section 3.1).

**The mandate boundary: prepare to review.** Under a mandate the centre stops at the firm's review queue. [source says] HMRC agents may act only through their own agent services account (inventory A, S10). [source says] The accounting roll-up's own delivered unit is "a return prepared for a local practitioner to review" (R-LIB-DELIVERY-19). So the boundary costs little and removes the hardest liability questions.

**The check is the product.** [source says] Item 4 is met on 14 of 87 lines at firm A and binds half the firm's hours (inventory A, section 3). Human checkers defer (R-WEB-STACK-27; R-WEB-PEOPLE-17). So each case ships with: automated tie-outs by separate code; a reviewer's screen that shows the tie-outs and asks for a judgement before showing the agent's reasoning; and a timer on review. Review minutes per unit are measured from day one.

**Evidence.** The order (screen, then cases) rests on two desk inventories and an absence. The check design is untested; no remedy for deference has been tested (ledger Q3).

**Scale.** One: three cases deep. Five: the same cases at each firm, with a client rule file per firm. Thirty: perhaps a dozen cases per trade; the count of cases grows slowly, the count of firms faster.

| Size (design choices) | 1 firm | 5 firms | 30 firms |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Centre, excluding pods | about 5 | about 12 to 15 | 40 to 60, with a head per trade |

**Roles.** New: practice engineer (R-WEB-STACK-10), pod lead, launch manager, head of quality. Changed: bookkeeper to operator; manager to workflow owner; trainee route. Gone: none in year one. [source says] CBA and Ford cut before measuring and re-hired (R-LIB-FAILURES-01/03).

**What the people whose know-how is captured receive.** [source says] No pay design for this exists anywhere in the evidence (ledger finding 10). What follows is judgement.

1. **A job pledge, funded by the mandates.** No redundancy caused by automation until one full annual cycle is measured. Headcount falls only by attrition and avoided hires (R-WEB-PEOPLE-01/02 show that is how it happens anyway). Freed hours are sold to mandate firms, so the pledge is paid for by revenue, not by hope.
2. **Paid authorship.** Playbook writing counts as chargeable time (R-WEB-PEOPLE-21). Each playbook and client rule file carries its authors' names.
3. **A reuse award.** When a playbook passes the release gate and is used at a second firm, its authors receive group units, held about four years (R-WEB-PEOPLE-20 for rewarding reuse; R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-11 for holding). Mandate-firm authors receive it too, as a fee credit to their firm that the contract requires be passed to the named people.
4. **A route up.** Operator to pod lead to workflow owner or practice engineer. [source says] PwC US has an engineer track to partner (R-WEB-PEOPLE-24).
5. **Never usage pay.** [source says] Usage targets drove cost at Uber and fear at Proton (R-LIB-FAILURES-05; R-LIB-PEOPLE-14).

**Juniors.** Training is an explicit cost line. [source says] Trainees learn on the lines handed over first; training is 2,300 hours at firm A (inventory A, N2). Route: do the task by hand, then check the machine (R-WEB-PEOPLE-15/16; no results published). [source says] Half of juniors added nothing as checkers in one study (R-WEB-PEOPLE-17), so a trainee's sign-off is tested against known-answer files before it counts. [inferred] Trainees who work on four firms' files see more variety than trainees at one.

**Leading the change.** Staff are consulted before any capture. They hold a pause control. [source says] Imposed recording met revolt at Meta (R-WEB-PEOPLE-28/29). They are told in writing what saved time is for; two-thirds of users elsewhere are not (R-WEB-PEOPLE-33).

**Evidence.** Negative cases are strong. The positive design is judgement throughout.

## Part 8. Agents as part of the organisation, and the principles (Q3)

**Decision.**

- Every agent has its own identity, a named sponsor, system-enforced limits and a register entry (R-WEB-GOVERNANCE-25/27/29). No agent borrows a person's login. [source says] Software that collects HMRC sign-in details breaches HMRC's terms (inventory A, S10).
- One case agent per case, built as a maintained workflow with AI steps. Several agents only for parallel reading (R-LIB-GOVERNANCE-20). [source says] Role agents failed at Sierra (R-LIB-GOVERNANCE-17).
- Separation by firm and by client is enforced at the gateway. An agent serving firm X cannot read firm Y. This matters more here than in any other design, because the centre serves firms that compete.
- Budget: a monthly token and money cap per case per firm, set by the head of quality. At the cap the agent stops and the pod lead decides. [source says] No working example exists (ledger Part 8).
- Review: each agent is reviewed quarterly on first-pass acceptance and recurring corrections. Retired when its case is retired or a plain rule will do (R-LIB-DELIVERY-34).

**The principles, on one page.**

| # | Principle | Comes from |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | A named person answers for every piece of work. The agent's sponsor answers for the agent. | R-WEB-LAW-16/18; R-WEB-GOVERNANCE-27 |
| 2 | Whoever answers for it can stop it, at once, without asking. | Ledger Part 3 assumption |
| 3 | Alone: reversible, internal work inside written limits. With approval: anything outward that commits, anything filed, paid or signed. Never: client acceptance, price, payment release, changes to bank details, reports of suspicion. | R-WEB-LAW-17/19; R-LIB-GOVERNANCE-13; inventory B, A17 and B7 |
| 4 | Approval by consequence, not by step. Sampling for routine reversible work; every output reviewed where the profession's guidance says so. | R-WEB-LAW-19; S-B-04; S9 |
| 5 | The maker never checks its own work, and cannot read or change the check. | R-WEB-SELFIMPROVE-24/25; R-LIB-GOVERNANCE-19 |
| 6 | A reviewer judges before seeing the agent's argument. Review time is measured, not assumed. | R-WEB-GOVERNANCE-21; R-WEB-STACK-27 |
| 7 | Record what the work produces, for a stated purpose. No blanket capture. Decisions are made in writing. Anyone may pause recording of their own work. | R-WEB-LAW-01/02; R-LIB-GOVERNANCE-31/32; R-WEB-PEOPLE-29 |
| 8 | A firm's client material stays in that firm's store and trains nothing. | R-WEB-LAW-12/15 |
| 9 | Anyone, person or agent, may propose a change. A named owner approves it. A shared change passes the release gate. Every change can be rolled back. | R-LIB-GOVERNANCE-12/23; R-WEB-SELFIMPROVE-02 |
| 10 | When a person and an agent disagree, the person decides, and writes one line saying why. That line is a classed correction. | R-LIB-DELIVERY-18; ledger Q3 unknown |
| 11 | Measure outcomes: accepted units, review minutes, repeat corrections, cash. Never usage. | R-LIB-GOVERNANCE-27; R-LIB-FAILURES-05 |
| 12 | Say what AI does, to clients and staff, before it does it. | R-WEB-COMMERCIAL-26; S-B-04 |

**The two disputes.**

- *Step approval against limits.* I take the middle (principles 3 and 4). No case compares the two on the same work (R-LIB-GOVERNANCE-15). The mandate adds a simple line: across the firm boundary, everything is step-approved, because the firm's reviewer accepts each unit. Inside the pod, limits and sampling apply. Insurers may yet ask for review of all outputs (R-WEB-LAW-25); the tax bodies' guidance already reads that way for tax deliverables (S9). So for tax, every unit is reviewed and the gain must come from faster, better-supported review.
- *Record everything against its costs.* I side with O'Connor on capture and with Blomfield on the loop. The record is the workflow's own trace, the reviewer's classed corrections and written decisions. Not screens, not keystrokes. [source says] Records people must enter die (R-LIB-GOVERNANCE-31).

**Evidence.** Convergent practice for identity and register; no outcome evidence (ledger Part 8).

**Scale.** One: perhaps ten agents, one register page. Five: the same agents, one instance per firm. Thirty: hundreds of instances; review by exception; a quarterly audit of the approvers (R-WEB-GOVERNANCE-26).

## Part 9. The management system

**Decision.** Few measures, fixed rhythm, decisions in writing. Agents write the reports; people read exceptions.

**Eight measures** (the number is Danaher's, R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-02/04):

1. First-pass acceptance: units the reviewer accepts without correction.
2. Review minutes per unit.
3. Recurrence: the same correction seen again (R-WEB-SELFIMPROVE-17).
4. Escalations per case, by cause.
5. Unit cost (people and tokens) against unit price.
6. Capacity to P&L: fees per head at each firm, with a named use for each freed hour (R-LIB-ECONOMICS-30).
7. End-client retention and complaints.
8. Staff turnover, reviewers first.

| When | What | Who |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Weekly | Pod exception review, thirty minutes: new exception classes, stuck cases. Service note to each mandate owner: units delivered, accepted, late, review minutes. Platform firm: weekly P&L and cash note (R-LIB-PEOPLE-20). | Pod leads; launch managers; managing partner |
| Monthly | Operating review, half a day, the eight measures by case and firm. Release board: playbook and check changes. Risk: agent register, incidents, budget caps hit. Monthly close, by a rented product. | Chief executive; head of quality; workflow owners |
| Quarterly | Mandate council. Re-score the inventory and re-test the register of failed uses at each model release (R-LIB-FAILURES-28/29). Price and scope review per contract. Insurer and regulator calendar. Capital: build, buy or exercise. | Board; mandate lead |
| Yearly | Plan. A post-mortem a year after each launch (R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-09). Staff unit awards. | Board |

**Escalation.** Pod lead, then workflow owner, then the firm's signatory. Anything touching a client or a regulator goes to the head of quality the same day. Two firms that disagree on a procedure each keep their client rule file; the shared playbook changes only by the release board. A mandate firm may stay on the old version for one quarter.

**Attention.** [source says] At the one firm that measured it, meeting time rose 20% and standing instructions grew to 8,500 (R-WEB-GOVERNANCE-15/16). Rules: no standing report without a named reader; any measure beyond the eight needs one removed.

**Evidence.** Precedents for rhythm. Nothing on routines inside an AI group (ledger Part 9).

**Scale.** One: the weekly and monthly rows only. Five: add the council. Thirty: the monthly review splits by trade.

## Part 10. The improvement system (Q4)

**Decision.** A gated loop per case. Modest gains expected. The mandate makes the loop commercial: every correction comes from a paying reviewer.

| Loop | What it needs | Proposes | Tests | Approves | Undo |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Models improve | A fixed, human-written test set per case; a release-by-release re-score | The vendor | The test set, in shadow | Head of quality | Pin the previous model version |
| 2. Playbooks, checks and tools improve | Classed corrections (error, preference, stale value); real-case regression sets | Reviewers, operators, the trace-reading agent | Regression set; then one firm, staged | Workflow owner for client rules; release board for shared playbooks | Version rollback, rehearsed quarterly |
| 3. The process improves | An incident review; the yearly post-mortems | Head of quality | The eight measures | Board | Revert the rule |

- [source says] Every production loop is human-gated (ledger Part 10). Gains are small: about a point in a month at Intercom (R-WEB-SELFIMPROVE-03). Agent-written playbooks made results worse (/26). A keep-best gate gave steady gains; ungated edits swung (/15).
- [source says] Corrections are noisy (R-LIB-DELIVERY-18; R-WEB-STACK-21). So a correction is not usable until the reviewer picks its class. That is one click on the review screen and it is a term of the contract.
- **How to know it works**, other than by counting changes: recurrence falls; review minutes per unit fall; first-pass acceptance rises; and the launch cost of the latest firm is below the first.
- **Why it might last.** [source says] Danaher's loop was carried by line executives who teach and are rated on it (R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-03). Here pod leads and workflow owners own it, and the reuse award pays them for it. [source says] No sound failure rate for such programmes exists (R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-34).
- **Ben's "static" criticism.** Fair for what is evidenced (ledger section 3). [inferred] The mandate answers it by construction: there is no map to go stale and no project to end. The group is paid per unit for as long as it serves, so a playbook that stops improving loses money as the price steps down.
- **Full context.** It would need every channel. [source says] One build caught 11 notes against 101 calendar entries (R-LIB-IMPROVE-TECH-25). The law forbids blanket monitoring (R-WEB-LAW-01/02/03). Where a firm cannot record: interviews, reviewer corrections and written decisions.
- **What travels between firms.** Procedure, checks and exception classes travel. Client files and client rules do not. [source says] Whether client work may improve a firm's own playbooks is unsettled and argued by analogy (ledger section 5, gap 4). [inferred] Using firm X's client work to improve what firm Y receives is harder still. Until advice says otherwise, cross-firm test sets are built from consented or synthetic cases. This limits the loop's main supposed advantage. It is open.

**Evidence.** Software and vendor accounts. No measured loop at any professional firm (ledger Q4).

**Scale.** One: loop 2 on three cases. Five: corrections from five sets of reviewers; the release board meets monthly. Thirty: a release owner per trade; staged release across firms.

## Part 12. Back office and shared services

**Decision.** Rent it, prove the method on it, charge it, and do not call it an advantage.

- First builds at the platform firm are back-office lines: recurring billing, payment reminders, signature chasing (inventory A, order 1). They prove permissions, logging and the owner role before client work.

[inferred] In year one the mandate firm's costs rise unless it has work it is turning away or vacancies it cannot fill. So the mandate is sold only to such firms. That narrows the market and is the right narrowing.

**The centre.** Income: unit fees and the services charge. Cost: engineers, practice engineers, quality, launch. [source says] Build costs on file: six weeks per tax domain; 1.5 engineers over months for permissions; engineers on site two years at Long Lake (R-WEB-STACK-04; R-LIB-IMPROVE-TECH-30; R-LIB-STRUCTURE-16). The cost of the centre and its charge are unknown for every group (ledger Part 14).

**Unit economics.** Per unit: price, less operator minutes, less a share of the pod lead, less tokens, less connector and platform upkeep. [source says] Where automation worked, the check was two-thirds of what remained (R-LIB-DELIVERY-29). Under the mandate that check is done by the firm, at the firm's cost. [inferred] This is the stance's best economic feature and its trap: the centre's margin looks good because the costliest step is someone else's. If review minutes do not fall, the firm gains nothing and leaves.

**The honest base case.** [source says] Listed outsourcers with wide AI use earn 10 to 15% (R-WEB-COMMERCIAL-02/07/09), and critics say outsourcers are "not trusted to innovate, not rewarded for innovating, and not allowed to innovate" (R-WEB-ROLLUPS-32). Mandate income should be planned at an outsourcer's margin. The upside is the options and the platform firm's own earnings.

**Capital allocation.** One purchase. Two years of centre cost. No debt. Later capital goes to options on firms already served, priced on A003's rule: pay for part of a credible improvement, never all of it (R-LIB-ECONOMICS-32).

**Avoid the five double-counts** (R-LIB-ECONOMICS-31). The one most tempting here: counting a freed hour at the platform firm both as a saving and as mandate capacity. It is one or the other.

**Scale.** One: cash out. Five: the question is whether four mandates cover the centre; unknown. Thirty: [source says] "a cost gain that lasts three renewal cycles … still returns its cost" (R-LIB-STRUCTURE-06). With little capital in, the hurdle is low.

## Part 15. How it changes over time, and whether anything durable is left (Q5)

| Stage | What exists |
| --- | --- |
| Founding | The pair at the top, capital for one firm and two years, an insurer's answer in writing |
| After the first firm (year one) | Three cases live at the platform firm; the check and review queue built; one friendly mandate in shadow |
| Five firms (years two to three) | Four mandates; launch cost per firm measured; first reuse awards paid; first option decision |
| Steady state | A platform firm per trade; mandates as the main channel; stakes in the firms worth owning; a head per trade near thirty units (R-WEB-GOVERNANCE-07) |

**As models improve and prices fall.** [source says] Scaffolding is rebuilt with each generation (R-LIB-PEOPLE-23). Single-task capture and release tooling are already rentable (R-WEB-MAPPING-25; R-WEB-SELFIMPROVE-35). The register of failed uses is re-tested at each release (L019). When a rival cuts price by a third, the step-down clause has already conceded part of it; the rest is met by unit cost or the contract is lost.

**Q5. Does the work become a commodity?** For the routine, visible tier, yes, at the pace clients can compare and renew (R-WEB-COMMERCIAL-05/12). [inferred] The mandate puts the group on the commodity side of the table by choice. Prepared units are exactly what gets cheaper. So the question is sharper here than in any other design.

**Who keeps the gain.** At first, the mandate firm and the group split it, because small-business end clients have no procurement function (inventory A, section 1). Later the mandate firm takes more through the step-down. Later still, end clients take it at repricing. [source says] Firm A's protection is thinner than "licensed" suggests: only audit is reserved (inventory A, section 3). Firm B's is thinner still (inventory B, section 3.4).

**What stays scarce, and can this group own it?**

| Scarce thing | Can the group own it? |
| --- | --- |
| End-client relationships and trust | Only at the platform firm, and at firms where an option is exercised |
| Permissions: agent status, supervision, insurance | At the platform firm. Rented, in effect, at mandate firms. |
| A tested, independent check per case | Yes, for a time. Nobody sells one (ledger Q9). Quick to copy once seen (R-LIB-ECONOMICS-33). |
| The habit of change | Perhaps (R-LIB-ECONOMICS-40). Unproven. |
| Standing with other firms' partners | Yes. Slow to build, slow to copy. |

**If nothing durable is left beyond a few years.** Then this is the right vehicle, because it has the least capital at risk and no goodwill to write down. [source says] Concentrix wrote off $1.05bn (R-WEB-COMMERCIAL-09). The strategy is: earn through the window on little capital; use the mandates to choose which few firms are worth owning for their clients and permissions; own those; let the rest go.

## Q8. How the design changes by type of business

| Dimension | Effect on a mandate design |
| --- | --- |
| **Licence and ownership rules** | Drives it. Where outside ownership is barred (audit, German law), the mandate is the only route. Where nothing is reserved, the firm is easier to buy and the mandate matters less. |
| **Pricing basis and who pays** | Drives it. Fixed fee to small payers: the firm keeps a saving and can share it. Hourly or enterprise: the end client claims it at renewal (R-WEB-COMMERCIAL-15/18) and nothing is left to split. |
| **Capacity- or demand-bound** | Drives it. A capacity-bound firm buys units. A demand-bound firm has no use for them. |
| **Checkability, and when** | Drives the check and the boundary. An outside balance allows sampling. Advice has no such check and stays out of scope. |
| **Shared systems across firms** | Added by this run; it matters more here than in any other design. Few common suites: mandates scale. Bespoke or closed systems: they do not. |
| Regulated or not, beyond ownership | Sets disclosure and review duty (S9; S-B-04) |
| Physical work | Stays with the firm. The mandate takes the paperwork round it. |
| Third-party money (client accounts) | Narrows scope to preparation only |
| Small-business or enterprise | Enterprise firms can build this themselves (AT&T). Sell to small firms. |
| Advice or coordination | Advice is out. Coordination fits if state is readable. |
| Global or local | Little effect by itself. Country matters through employment and ownership law. |
| Commodity or high-touch | Little effect by itself; it is the sum of the rows above. |

The four that drive it: ownership rules, pricing basis, capacity, and shared systems.

## Fit: do the parts belong together?

**Incentives reward what the improvement system needs.**

- The loop needs classed corrections, practitioner-written playbooks and honest exceptions (Part 10). Authors are paid for playbooks reused elsewhere, in units held for years. Reviewers are measured on review minutes and recurrence, not on agreement with the agent.
- The loop needs people not to hide what they know. [source says] A third say they do, from fear (R-WEB-PEOPLE-30). The job pledge removes the reason, and the mandates fund the pledge.
- The centre is paid per accepted unit at a falling price. It earns only if the playbooks keep improving. A fixed fee or a licence would reward the first build and nothing after.
- Sellers are paid on retention and in group units, not on their firm's hours.
- **Where the fit is loose.** A mandate firm's partners gain when their reviewers are fast. Fast review is also how deference shows up. The eight measures include first-pass acceptance, which the centre wants high. Both parties are rewarded for a reviewer who nods. The only counterweights are the head of quality's sampled re-review and seeded known-error files. I think that is enough. I cannot show it.

**Decision rights match liability.**

- The signatory answers to the regulator and can stop any agent on their work (Parts 3 and 13).
- The platform firm answers for prepared units and has a veto on which mandates and cases it accepts.
- The services company answers for the platform and holds the kill switch and the release gate.
- No unregulated board decides a regulated matter.
- **Where the fit is loose.** The group is liable for unit quality but cannot make a mandate firm's reviewers review well. Its remedy is to leave.

**The economics pay for the centre drawn.**

- At one firm they do not. A centre of about five is carried by capital for at least a year. This is stated, not hidden.
- At five firms the centre is paid by the services charge and unit margins. Whether four mandates cover twelve to fifteen people is unknown, and at an outsourcer's margin it is doubtful. The design's defence is that the centre is small, the capital is small, and each launch carries its own cost code so the answer arrives early.
- At thirty the centre is drawn at 40 to 60, in line with the only anchors (R-LIB-STRUCTURE-11/26). It is paid by unit fees across trades and by earnings from firms where options were exercised.
- **Where the fit is loose.** The reuse awards, the job pledge and the training line are all costs in the years the centre is not yet paid for.

## Where this stance is weakest

1. **It may be outsourcing with a better story.** UK practices already buy prepared work from outsourcing suppliers (inventory A, S18 names one). Those suppliers can rent the same models. Listed outsourcers earn 10 to 15% (R-WEB-COMMERCIAL-02/07/09). The group's claimed difference is that it owns a practice and designs the check. Nothing in the evidence shows that difference is worth a price.
2. **The gain is made in the review, and the review belongs to someone else.** If reviewers defer, quality falls under the firm's signature. If they do not, review minutes may not fall and the firm gains nothing. The group controls neither.
3. **No authority.** A mandate has "access and ability but perhaps not authority or time" (R-LIB-STRUCTURE-21, the library's own note). I gave it time by contract. I could not give it authority.
4. **The firm can learn and leave.** It keeps its rule files and has watched the method for eighteen months. The option is a hedge, not a lock.
5. **The cross-firm loop is legally narrow.** If client work cannot inform what another firm receives, each mandate improves mostly from its own corrections, and the centre's scale advantage shrinks to shared code.
6. **Other firms' systems.** One vendor refusal ends a case (R-LIB-IMPROVE-TECH-34).

The weakest of these is the first, because the others are risks inside the model and the first is whether there is a model.

**Evidence that would make me abandon it.**

- A baseline month at three real practices shows the unit price needed to cover pod and platform cost is above the firm's own cost per unit, or above an offshore supplier's price.
- At the platform firm, after one annual cycle, review minutes per unit have not fallen on any case.
- Insurers decline to cover subcontracted, AI-prepared work, or price it so that the unit price fails.
- Two of the first four mandate firms leave at eighteen months and carry on alone.
- Advice confirms that staff transfer by law to the provider on every mandate, at a cost that turns the mandate into an acquisition of people.
- An owned roll-up publishes an audited margin gain with a baseline. Then the price of ownership is justified and the mandate is the timid choice.

**The type of business it suits worst.**

- Demand-bound firms with enterprise clients on hourly rates: no capacity to sell, and the end client takes the saving.
- Work that is physical, or that turns on third-party money or a relationship: most of reference firm B. A mandate can take B's ledgers. It cannot take its repairs, boards or site visits.
- Firms on closed or bespoke systems.
- Advice sold on judgement, where there is no unit and no outside check.

For those, ownership and deep knowledge of one firm is the better stance, and this design says so.
