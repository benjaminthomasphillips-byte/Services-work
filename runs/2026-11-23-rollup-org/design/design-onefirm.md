# Design of the whole organisation: one firm at a time

Stage 4 of the run. Written 23 November 2026. One designer, working alone, without sight of the other two designs. Nothing was written to Notion.

**Stance.** One firm at a time. Know each business deeply. Grow it and improve it by ordinary means before automating it. Share little beyond method, capital and a short set of principles. This is the position Chroma argues (N061, N052, N051) and the practice of the lasting decentralised acquirers (Constellation, Berkshire, Halma). I argue it as its best advocate would, and say at the end where it is weakest.

**Read.** The brief, the ledger and both inventories in full. Evidence rows opened for the Chroma position (LIB-ECONOMICS 09 to 12, 30, 31, 40; LIB-GOVERNANCE 03 to 05, 33; LIB-STRUCTURE 19 to 21, 30; LIB-PEOPLE 20) and for the decentralised acquirers (WEB-GOVERNANCE 01 to 11; WEB-PRECEDENTS 07 to 18). Other rows are cited from the ledger and were not re-opened.

**Tags.** [source says] = a ledger row or inventory source states it. [inferred] = my reasoning. "Design choice" = a decision of mine with no evidence behind the particular setting. Figures taken from the inventories are model assumptions and are marked as such.

**How sure, overall.** Fairly sure of the legal form, the debt rule and the placing of accountability: they rest on rules and on recorded failures. Moderately sure of the governance shape: it copies groups that lasted, but those groups bought firms that did not need rebuilding (R-WEB-GOVERNANCE-10). Not sure of the economics: no AI roll-up of any design has published a result (ledger section 4, finding 1), and the advocate of this stance has completed no deals (R-LIB-STRUCTURE-20).

## The argument in one page

1. [source says] Nobody has shown that an owner keeps an AI saving. Every results figure from the platform roll-ups is self-reported and several change between tellings (R-WEB-ROLLUPS-04/14/17/22; R-LIB-STRUCTURE-18).
2. [source says] The tools are rentable. Task capture, self-rewriting memory, release tooling and back-office products are sold to anyone (R-WEB-MAPPING-25; R-WEB-SELFIMPROVE-13/35; R-WEB-STACK-23/28). "Every buyer can automate invoicing" (R-LIB-ECONOMICS-11).
3. [inferred] So a design should not pay for an AI gain in the purchase price, should not borrow against one, and should not build a centre that only pays if one arrives. It should own firms that are good businesses without it.
4. [source says] Both inventories find the same thing: the firm does not need many builds. It needs a few missing things built once, and they are specific to the firm and its clients: an independent check, written rules per client, readable inputs, authority limits (inventory A section 3; inventory B section 3.1).
5. [inferred] Those things are held by the people who do the work and can only be written by them. [source says] Playbooks written by people helped; playbooks agents wrote for themselves made results worse (R-WEB-SELFIMPROVE-26). A working tool went unused until months of in-person work (R-LIB-PEOPLE-15). The work is local, so the organisation should be.
6. [source says] Where an owner might keep a saving, it is where the firm is short of capacity, not customers (R-LIB-ECONOMICS-09; a hypothesis). Both reference firms look short of capacity on thin evidence (inventory A section 1; inventory B section 1, block side only). So the gain to aim at is growth at the same price, not cost out.
7. [source says] What lasted among serial acquirers was autonomy, a small shared method, long-held equity and a long horizon (ledger finding 10).

The design that follows is a small permanent holding company that buys one well-run, capacity-short firm at a time, leaves it its name, its systems and its decisions, and puts one person and one method into it for a year or more.

## Part 1. Purpose and where it plays (with Q8)

**What it is for.** To be a good long-term owner of local and regional services firms, and to help each one serve more clients with the people it has. AI is one of the means. It is not the purpose and not the name on the door.

**The bet.** [inferred] That in the next several years the scarce things are a firm's client relationships, its permissions to practise, its experienced people and its willingness to change how it works; and that the last of these is won firm by firm (R-LIB-ECONOMICS-40 argues the same; a fund's thesis, no results).

**Where it plays.** Firms that meet all of these:

| Screen | Why | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Short of capacity, not of clients | A saving becomes volume at the same price | R-LIB-ECONOMICS-09 (hypothesis) |
| Fixed or recurring fees, small clients without procurement | A saving stays until renewal or until the client asks | R-WEB-COMMERCIAL-04/15/18; inventory A section 1 |
| A stack of permissions or named accountabilities the client cannot easily copy | Thin protection, but some | Inventory A section 3; inventory B section 3.4 |
| A head who wants to stay and wants to change the work | Autonomy only works with the right person | R-WEB-GOVERNANCE-01; R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-14 |
| Work already cut into job types in a system of record | The diligence inventory is cheap | Inventory A section 4; inventory B section 4 |

UK first. Germany and the Netherlands later, if at all: works councils hold a veto over monitoring tools and Germany bars investors from law firms (R-WEB-LAW-09/10/28).

**What it will not do.** Buy at auction. Put debt on a regulated entity. Buy faster than it can know the firm. Count price rises as improvement: that route ended in regulation for vets and funerals (R-WEB-OWNERSHIP-25; R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-23/24/25) and in a refund order for TransDigm (R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-18, summary only). Own audit. Migrate firms on to one system. Set usage targets.

**Time horizon.** Permanent capital, with no planned exit. [source says] When Constellation's investors put it up for sale, managers cut acquisitions and long-term spending (R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-11). Leonard's view is that autonomy needs "an unusually trusting culture and a long investment horizon" (R-WEB-GOVERNANCE-02; he calls it a hunch). A fund with a three-to-seven-year clock (R-WEB-OWNERSHIP-06) does not fit this stance. If Ben's capital has such a clock, this design is the wrong one and he should take one of the others. Ben's horizon is unknown (ledger Part 1).

**Q8. How the design changes by type of business.**

| Dimension | What it changes in this design | Weight |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Capacity- or demand-constrained | Whether to buy at all. Demand-constrained firms pass the saving to clients (R-LIB-ECONOMICS-09) | Drives |
| Licensed or not | Legal form, who signs, who may own (Part 2). Note the licence is thinner than it sounds at both reference firms | Drives |
| Pricing basis and who pays | How long a saving is kept. Hourly passes it on; fixed holds it to renewal; payer-is-not-buyer (block management) delays the question further | Drives |
| How checkable the outcome is, and how soon | The control: sampled review where an outside check exists (a bank balance, a registrar), step approval where it does not | Drives |
| Whether the work is physical | How much stays with people (22% of hours at firm B against 15% at firm A, on model hours) | Matters |
| Small-business or enterprise clients | Enterprise buyers take the saving through procurement. Stay with small clients | Matters, as a screen |
| Country | Through employment and ownership law only | Matters at entry |
| How alike the firms' work is | The more alike, the more a shared platform would carry between firms, and the weaker this stance | Matters; cuts against me |
| Global or local | No evidence it matters by itself (ledger Q8, unknown) | Little |
| Advice or coordination | Shows up through checkability and physical work, not separately | Little |
| Commodity or high-touch | Shows up through pricing basis and who pays | Little |

The four that drive the design are the ledger's four (Q8 assumption). I add a fifth that matters to the choice between stances: how alike the firms' work is.

**Decision.** A permanent-capital holding company buying capacity-short local services firms in the UK, one at a time, for growth at held prices.
**Reason.** It is the only purpose that does not depend on an unproven saving.
**Evidence.** R-LIB-ECONOMICS-09/11/40; R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-11/12; R-WEB-OWNERSHIP-25. All hypotheses or operators' accounts. No measured case.
**One, five, thirty.** One firm: a single trade, chosen for Ben's access. Five: at most two trades, so that firm heads have peers to learn from (R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-13). Thirty: three or four trades, grouped five to seven firms to a group partner (Part 3). [inferred] At one or two purchases a year, thirty firms is a long way off. This design accepts that.

## Part 2. Ownership, capital and legal form

**Structure.**

```
Holding company (permanent capital; Ben and long-term investors)
│
├── Group services company
│     employs the centre; owns the method, templates and group contracts
│
├── Firm 1 Ltd  (own name, own board, own bank accounts, own systems)
│     majority: holding company        minority: the firm's head and senior staff
│     └── Firm 1 Audit Ltd, if any: controlled by qualified persons, NOT owned by the group
│
├── Firm 2 Ltd  ...
```

- **Each firm stays a separate legal entity under its own board**, as at Halma (R-WEB-GOVERNANCE-11). The group partner sits on that board.
- **Audit is left out.** UK audit firms must be controlled by qualified persons (R-WEB-LAW-29/30). At firm A audit is 12 opinions and under 5% of hours (inventory A section 3). It stays in an entity controlled by the responsible individual, or the book is referred to another firm. The group does not own it and takes no fee from it. [inferred] This also avoids the independence conflicts that cost CBIZ client work (R-WEB-OWNERSHIP-11).
- **Non-audit accountancy has no ownership bar**, but an investor-controlled firm may lose the "Chartered Accountants" title under an ICAEW proposal, results due 2027 (R-WEB-LAW-31). Open. It bears on firm A's marketing and should be priced as a risk.
- **Property management:** no ownership rule was searched (ledger Part 2, unknown). Open.
- **Seller terms.** The holding company buys a majority. The head and the senior people who are staying keep or buy a minority in their own firm, not in the group. [source says] Disclosed designs range from buying 40 to 60% of earnings (Focus, R-WEB-OWNERSHIP-17) to half the practice's equity (Pets at Home, /27). The percentage here is open and set deal by deal. Payment: cash at completion plus deferred consideration paid over about three years, conditional on client retention and on the seller staying. **No earn-out on hours, revenue per head or the firm's short-term profit**: those reward keeping the work as it is in the years a redesign would change it (ledger Part 2, contradictions; the researcher's inference).
- **Debt.** None on any firm. No guarantee by a firm of holding-company borrowing (the FCA's finding, R-WEB-OWNERSHIP-19). Little or none at the holding company, and none sized on AI savings. "A bad year should stay a bad year, not become a crisis" (R-LIB-STRUCTURE-20). The failures on file are debt failures (R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-28; R-WEB-OWNERSHIP-31/32/33).
- **How technology is owned and charged.** Each firm owns its own playbooks, test sets and client rules. They are that firm's property and were written by its people. The group services company owns only the method and templates, and gives them to firms free. Anything further the centre does for a firm is billed at cost and the firm may refuse it (R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-10). There is no platform fee.

**Decision.** Holding company; firms as separate entities with local minority owners; audit outside; no debt at firm level; no platform charge.
**Reason.** It matches the control rules, keeps the head an owner, and removes the failure mode on file.
**Evidence.** R-WEB-LAW-29/30/31; R-WEB-OWNERSHIP-17/19/27; R-WEB-GOVERNANCE-11; R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-10/28. No evidence on the right minority percentage or on UK multiples beyond brokers' marketing (inventory A, S19).
**One, five, thirty.** The same at every scale. At thirty, intermediate group companies by trade may be added for the group partners; firms remain separate.

## Part 3. Governance and decision rights

The rule is Constellation's: the layer above "can nurture, goad and suggest, but they can't order", and its one hard power is replacing the firm's head (R-WEB-GOVERNANCE-01). I add Berkshire's audit (R-WEB-GOVERNANCE-10) and a short list of non-negotiables, because an AI-era firm can hurt clients in ways Berkshire's subsidiaries could not.

**Decision-rights table.**

| Decision | Centre | Firm (head and board) | Signatory or workflow owner | Agent | Who can stop it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Buying a firm; price; structure | Decides | Head of an existing firm may propose a tuck-in | – | – | Holding board |
| Appointing, paying, replacing the firm's head | Decides | Consulted | – | – | Holding board |
| Dividends and capital between firms | Decides | Proposes reinvestment | – | – | Holding board |
| Debt, guarantees | Decides: none | May not | – | – | Centre |
| The firm's plan, prices, hiring, pay below the head | Advises | Decides | – | – | Firm board |
| Client acceptance and disengagement | No role | Sets policy | Decides each case (MLRO or principal where a rule names one) | May prepare; never decides | Signatory |
| Sign-off of client work; filings; references | No role | Sets review policy | Decides and answers for it | Drafts; never signs | Signatory |
| Client money: mandates, payment release | Audits | Dual authorisation by principals | – | Never | Any principal |
| Which work to hand to an agent, and in what order | Supplies the test; advises | Decides | Owner decides for their workflow | – | Owner; head |
| Systems of record and tools | Advises; holds a short list of vetted suppliers | Decides | – | – | Firm head |
| An agent's limits, tools and budget | Sets the floor (the non-negotiables) | Approves | Owner sets within the floor | Acts within them | Owner, at once; any signatory on their own work; head |
| A change to a playbook, check or test set | No role | – | Owner approves; a second person where the output is signed or outward | May propose; may not read or edit the checks | Owner; rollback by owner |
| The shared method and principles page | Owns; changes once a year after consulting heads | May adopt more, not less, on the non-negotiables | – | – | Centre |
| Security or data-protection breach response | May order a stop | Runs the response | – | – | Centre audit, firm head |
| What clients are told about AI and ownership | Sets the floor: disclose both | Words it | – | – | Firm head |

**The non-negotiables (the only orders the centre gives).** No debt or guarantees. Client money rules as the regulator sets them. Monthly accounts and the small measure set on time and honest. Disclosure of ownership and AI use to clients. Every agent on the firm's register with a named owner. No client material in an open tool. No recording of staff without the stated-purpose process (Part 10). The centre may audit any of these without notice.

**Board, risk and client acceptance.** Each firm's board: the head, one or two senior staff owners, the group partner. It meets monthly. Risk and client acceptance are decided in the firm by the people the rules name. [source says] The FCA found decisions about regulated firms being taken by unregulated boards (R-WEB-OWNERSHIP-20); this table keeps them out of the centre.

**Why the centre holds no release gate.** The ledger's assumption for this part gives the centre "the release gate for shared procedures". This design has almost no shared procedures, so it has no such gate. Each firm's "bespoke" process (R-LIB-IMPROVE-TECH-36) is changed by the person who answers for it.

**Decision.** Autonomy by default; a short list of orders; hard powers limited to capital, the head, audit and the stop on security grounds.
**Reason.** Decision rights sit where liability sits (Part 13), and "no PM can personally know the customers, products, employees, and competitors sufficiently well" (R-WEB-GOVERNANCE-01).
**Evidence.** R-WEB-GOVERNANCE-01/10/11; R-WEB-LAW-16/18; R-WEB-OWNERSHIP-20. Operators' own accounts from software and conglomerates. No AI-era precedent (ledger Part 3).
**One, five, thirty.** One: Ben or his partner sits on the firm's board. Five: one group partner chairs all five boards (Halma's span is five to seven, R-WEB-GOVERNANCE-11). Thirty: five or six group partners, each with five to seven firms and power to do tuck-ins; Leonard ran out of personal span at about thirty units and had to push monitoring, coaching and deals down a level (R-WEB-GOVERNANCE-07).

## Part 4. The acquisition engine (with Q6: how you build it)

**Sourcing.** Direct approaches to owners, no auctions (R-LIB-STRUCTURE-20). [source says] The accountancy market is crowded: 46% of surveyed UK mid-tier firms are PE-backed (R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-26). The pitch against those buyers is: your name stays, your people stay, no debt, no platform migration, no exit. Whether that wins deals at a fair price is untested.

**The sequence.**

| Stage | What is done | Who | Output |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| First meeting | A free half-day with the owner (R-LIB-STRUCTURE-20) | Deal partner | Is the head staying? Is the firm short of capacity? |
| Diligence on the business | Accounts, client list, concentration, staff turnover, client money, insurance history, regulatory record | Deal partner; outside advisers | Price on the firm as it stands |
| Diligence on the work | Weeks on the floor. A light full inventory, about 100 to 120 lines, from the system's own job types and counts, scored on the task test (Part 6). Ask the system vendor in writing whether an outside agent may write to it (R-LIB-IMPROVE-TECH-34). Ask the firm's insurer what it requires for AI-assisted work (R-WEB-LAW-25) | The operator who will become the resident | The scoring sheet; the binding-item table; three candidate cases; a list of lines not to touch |
| Pricing | Three scenarios, none with an AI saving in the price (R-LIB-STRUCTURE-10; R-LIB-ECONOMICS-32). Assumptions tested against base rates from earlier deals once there are any (R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-09) | Deal partner | Offer |
| Day one | Staff told in person, by the head, with the group partner present: who owns the firm, what does not change (name, jobs, systems), what will be asked of them. Clients written to: ownership disclosed. Nothing technical | Head; group partner | – |
| First hundred days | Finance first: monthly accounts, a weekly P&L and cash note, the small measure set. Client-money audit. The resident moves in. Baselines taken on the three cases: handling time, review time, error and rework found by sampling, clients per head. One back-office workflow handed over as a rehearsal. Engagement terms amended for AI disclosure | Resident; firm's managers | Baselines; first workflow owner named; a 100-day plan the head wrote (Danaher's sequence, R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-02) |
| Year one | Three whole cases redesigned: remove steps, write the client rules, build the check, then hand over what passes. A capacity-to-P&L line for each (V006; R-LIB-ECONOMICS-30). Freed hours sold as new clients | Workflow owners; resident; one engineer | Three cases live or stopped, with reasons |
| Month twelve | Post-acquisition review against the diligence assumptions (R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-09). The next purchase is not signed until this is done | Centre; head | Base rates updated; method amended |

**Carried from firm to firm.** The method (task test, loop design, principles page, capacity-to-P&L line), the diligence sheet, the base rates, the vetted supplier list, and the people who have done it before. **Rebuilt each time.** The inventory, the client rules, the playbooks, the checks, the test sets, the adoption. [source says] That is also what the platform roll-ups rebuild: mapping, data clean-up, adoption (R-LIB-STRUCTURE-03). The number A003 asks for, the cost of the latest firm against the first, will fall more slowly here than a platform claims. I expect it to fall through people's experience, not shared code. No services group has published that number (ledger Part 4, unknown).

**Integration as a function.** There is no integration team because there is no integration. There is a resident: one operator who lives in the firm for 12 to 18 months (Chroma's own figure, R-LIB-STRUCTURE-20; a plan, not a result). [source says] In-person work is what got a working tool used (R-LIB-PEOPLE-15); engineers stayed on site about two years at Long Lake (R-LIB-STRUCTURE-16).

**Q6. Who needs to be "AI-pilled".**

| Who | Needed? | Why | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Sponsor | Patience, not belief | Must fund a year or more before results | R-WEB-ROLLUPS-15; R-LIB-STRUCTURE-16 |
| Firm's head | Yes: must want the change and grant authority | The centre cannot order it | R-WEB-GOVERNANCE-01; R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-03 |
| Workflow owners | Yes: one named person per case | Owner conviction alone failed at Ciridae's client (N016, late 2025); a product owner restarted a stalled rollout (B14, Pluris, weak) | R-LIB-PEOPLE-15/16 |
| Whole staff | No. They need a reason, a say and a share | Mandate bought entry and fear at Proton; imposed capture drew revolt at Meta | R-LIB-PEOPLE-14; R-WEB-PEOPLE-28 |
| Clients | They need to be told, not converted | Guidance; RICS standard | R-WEB-COMMERCIAL-26; inventory B S-B-04 |

The hard case for this stance is N015: a convinced, resourced owner got poor results after a year (R-LIB-FAILURES-08; 2025 models; the owner's own account). I take it as a warning that conviction and ownership are not enough without a method, and as a dated result to re-test.

**Decision.** One purchase at a time, gated by a twelve-month review; diligence on the floor with a light inventory; a resident, not an integration team.
**Reason.** Pace and poor integration are the named causes of roll-up failure (R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-31), and the knowledge needed is local.
**Evidence.** R-LIB-STRUCTURE-20 (a plan); R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-02/09; R-LIB-PEOPLE-15. No measured comparison of this against a platform approach.
**One, five, thirty.** One: the deal partner and the resident are most of the centre. Five: two residents, so at most two firms in their first year at once. Thirty: residents are former workflow owners and managers from earlier firms; group partners source their own tuck-ins (R-WEB-GOVERNANCE-03).

## Part 5. Clients and the commercial side (with Q5)

- **What is sold.** What the firm sells now, at the prices it charges now. New pricing is tried on new services first (ledger Part 5 assumption).
- **Who owns the client.** The firm and the named person who serves them. The centre holds no client relationships and sets no cross-selling targets. [source says] Central sales targets reached clinicians in vet groups (R-WEB-OWNERSHIP-28), and paying sellers to steer clients is treated as a conflict (R-WEB-OWNERSHIP-21). Firms may refer to each other if they choose.
- **Brand.** The firm's own name, with the group named plainly on letterhead, website and engagement terms. Quiet group ownership under a local name is what the vets remedy acted on (R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-24).
- **Selling.** Growth is the point of the design, and agents "cannot reliably generate demand" (R-LIB-GOVERNANCE-14). So the freed hours of partners and managers go to selling, and each capacity-to-P&L line names who will sell the capacity and to whom (R-LIB-ECONOMICS-30). "Freed capacity is not profit until someone sells it" (R-LIB-FAILURES-16).
- **What clients are told.** In the engagement terms, from the first renewal after purchase: that AI tools are used, on what kind of work, that a named person reviews and answers for it, that client material is not used to train any model, and how to object. [source says] Tax guidance suggests it (R-WEB-COMMERCIAL-26); the RICS standard requires it "in writing and in advance" with an opt-out stated (inventory B, S-B-04); the SRA and ABA require authority before client material is used for learning (R-WEB-LAW-12/15). The firm must therefore be able to serve a client the old way.
- **When the client has the same tools.** [source says] One in five accounting clients has used AI to check their accountant (R-WEB-COMMERCIAL-25; a US vendor survey). A buyer forced an audit fee down 14% (R-WEB-COMMERCIAL-01). The firm's answer is to offer it first: share the tooling with the client where it helps (a records portal, a live ledger), and hold the accountable part. If a client asks for a lower fee on evidence, the head decides. The centre does not.

**Q5. Does ordinary work become a commodity, and what is left?**

[source says] Where the client could see the cost fall and do the work itself, price or volume fell: translation, moderation, IT run work (R-WEB-COMMERCIAL-05/07/12). Where neither holds, prices rose: US law (R-WEB-COMMERCIAL-13/14). Nothing is measured for either reference firm (ledger Q5, unknown).

[inferred] For these two firms:

- The routine tier (bookkeeping, payroll, demands, reminders) is already half done by rented software and will be a commodity at the pace clients compare and renew.
- The protection is thinner than "licensed" suggests. At firm A only the audit opinion is reserved by law; the rest rests on agent status, insurance, title and trust (inventory A section 3). At firm B there is no reserved activity at all (inventory B section 3.4, not checked against a primary source). Chroma's "last signature" (R-LIB-ECONOMICS-12) is weaker here than its authors claim. This is an inventory finding against my own stance's source, and I accept it.
- What is left is local and human: the trust of an owner-manager or a volunteer board, the client-money machinery, knowing which of a client's transfers are drawings, a contractor who turns up. A roll-up can own these only by keeping the people who hold them. That is the case for firm-level equity and autonomy.
- What stops a client doing it in-house: for small clients, they do not want to. AT&T could insource because it has 38 people and holds the knowledge itself (R-LIB-ECONOMICS-15/16). A plumber with four staff and a resident management company with three volunteer directors are not AT&T. [inferred] The nearer threat is a cheaper rival, not the client.

**If nothing durable is left beyond a few years' head start.** Then this is the design that loses least. It paid for the firm as it stood, borrowed nothing, built no platform to write off, and owns a business with recurring clients. The gain was an option that cost one resident and one engineer per firm. [source says] "A cost gain that lasts three renewal cycles … still returns its cost" (R-LIB-STRUCTURE-06). The strategy in that world is to be the local firm that passed the saving on first, as volume and service, and kept its clients.

**Decision.** Firms keep name, clients and pricing; ownership and AI use disclosed; growth through sold capacity; no central selling.
**Reason.** The rules point to disclosure; the regulatory record punishes concealment and central sales pressure.
**Evidence.** R-WEB-COMMERCIAL-04/25/26; R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-24; R-WEB-OWNERSHIP-21/28. No evidence on what small UK clients do when told (ledger Part 5, unknown).
**One, five, thirty.** Unchanged. At thirty the group name may carry some weight with sellers; it should carry none with clients.

## Part 6. Delivery (Q1 and Q2)

### Q1. How the work is found

Ben's hypothesis was a full list of 200 things, turned into products and automated. The evidence goes against that as a first move and as an aim (ledger section 3). Both inventories agree, and add that a light list is cheap and worth having in these two trades.

**This stance puts one step in front.** Before asking whether an agent can do a task, ask whether the task should exist. [source says] Implementers redesign first (R-LIB-DELIVERY-03). Some gains needed no agent: explicit rules, a classifier, a check moved to the originator (R-LIB-DELIVERY-34; Pluris, unchecked). In one example the exception held the cost: 25 minutes of work, 11 days elapsed (R-LIB-DELIVERY-02; an illustrative composite). Inventory A finds about 2,700 model hours of chasing clients for records; inventory B finds 28 of 123 lines waiting on an outside party. Those are operating problems before they are automation problems.

**The order.**

1. **In diligence: the light inventory.** One row per job type from the system of record. Columns as in the two inventories: unit and volume, who does it, how it is checked, how priced, where inputs live, commonest exceptions, test score, status, binding item. Its uses are to screen, to find the few shared missing things, to find the lines not to touch, and to pick where to start. It is not a build plan.
2. **After completion: three whole cases, chosen by the firm's head.** The unit to build is the case, not the row: a repair from report to paid invoice; a client-year from records request to filing. "The table has 123 rows; the firm has perhaps a dozen real cases" (inventory B section 4).
3. **Per case, in this order:** remove steps; write the rules per client; fix the inputs at source; build the independent check; then hand over what passes the test.
4. **The list grows from the floor.** It is kept true by the work, not by a mapping exercise. Each new exception class and each change in law amends a row. The practice manager owns the list. It is re-scored at each major model release and at each statutory change. [source says] Nothing in any source shows a vendor's "living" map doing this (R-WEB-MAPPING-04 against the ledger's Q1 contradictions).

**Units.** Service: what the client buys. Case: one instance from trigger to output, the unit that is built and run. Task: a row in the inventory, the unit that is scored. Decision: a point where someone commits the firm, the unit that is gated.

**Exceptions** are classed by cause, as Dwelly reports doing (R-LIB-DELIVERY-12/13): missing information; blocked tool or write path; policy or authority; judgement. I add a fifth from the inventories: waiting on the client or a third party. Counts per class are the first thing measured in the hundred days. None exist today (both inventories).

**Productising.** It means a fixed procedure, a unit price and an outside reader who can tell if it is right. [source says] It worked where rules fix the procedure, with the rest handed out (R-LIB-DELIVERY-30). At firm B that is about ten of 123 lines: sales packs, notices, section 20, company secretarial, tenancy documents (inventory B section 4). At firm A most compliance work is already a fixed-fee product in effect. It does not apply to advice, relationships, or a fixed fee covering a bundle the client does not buy in parts.

### Q2. What a task needs: the test as I would apply it

Ben's four are necessary and not sufficient (ledger section 3; confidence medium-high). A quoting workflow with all four scored 92.8% on synthetic tests and 22.3% in production because real requests were incomplete (R-LIB-DELIVERY-05; implementer's figures; late 2025 to January 2026 models). A tool that beat its owner went unused (R-LIB-FAILURES-09).

**Three gate questions, asked first.**

- G1. Is the step needed? Would a plain rule, or moving the check to the originator, do?
- G2. If it worked, who gets the freed hours, what will they do, and which line of the P&L moves? If this cannot be written down, stop (R-LIB-ECONOMICS-30).
- G3. Has a named person agreed to own the agent's version? (Inventory A found the owner item sorts nothing when scored as "someone owns this today".)

**Twelve items, scored Y, P or N.**

| # | Item | Scored Y only if |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Readable state | Every input is in a system, not in email, phone or memory |
| 2 | Written playbook | The firm's own rules per client and the exception routes are written by the people who do the work |
| 3 | A way to write | A supported interface exists and the outside body permits it |
| 4 | Independent verifier | Something the maker cannot reach checks the result: a bank balance, a registrar, a recomputation by separate code, a reviewer who judges before seeing the agent's reasoning |
| 5 | Trigger with complete inputs | The work starts on a clear event and the inputs usually arrive whole |
| 6 | Authority and limits | Money, contact and scope limits are written and enforced by the system |
| 7 | Exception route | Each exception class has a named person and a time to respond |
| 8 | Undo | The act can be reversed, or it is gated by a person |
| 9 | Test set from real traffic | Past real cases, including the messy ones, are held as a fixed set |
| 10 | Volume and value | Enough to pay for build and upkeep. No threshold exists (ledger Q1); judged, then checked at the review |
| 11 | Client supplies inputs in time | Or the workflow includes the chase |
| 12 | Corrections are classed | A reviewer's change is recorded as error, preference or stale value (R-LIB-DELIVERY-18; R-WEB-STACK-21) |

Items 11 and the "outside body permits" clause in item 3 are the two the inventories found missing (inventory A section 4). HMRC's ban on automating its web pages is the example (inventory A, S10).

**Status rule**, as the inventories use it: hand over now; hand over with a designed check; assist only; leave with people.

**Scoring a firm.** Three tables, all from the diligence sheet: hours by status; lines and hours by binding item; the lines to leave alone and why. Then read off the few things to build once. For firm B that was four (inventory B section 3.1). The score "ranks; it does not measure" (inventory B section 4).

**Checked and signed.** The signed check stays local and human. Review time per unit is measured from the first week, because where automation worked the check was most of what was left (R-LIB-DELIVERY-29; R-WEB-MAPPING-06) and because faster preparation moves the queue to the reviewers (inventory A section 3). Reviewers are tested before they are relied on: half of 523 juniors added nothing to the agent alone (R-WEB-PEOPLE-17; a firm's own study).

**Decision.** Light inventory in diligence; redesign before automation; three whole cases; the twelve-item test with three gates.
**Reason.** Every case started narrow; both inventories show the shared gaps only appear on a full list; the cost in these trades is days.
**Evidence.** R-LIB-DELIVERY-03/05/12/34; R-WEB-SELFIMPROVE-26; both inventories. No case began with a full inventory and reported (ledger Q1). No task has been run with and without one item (R-LIB-DELIVERY-37).
**One, five, thirty.** Each firm keeps its own list and its own playbooks. At five, firms in the same trade may compare sheets if they choose. At thirty the centre holds base rates: how often each item binds, by trade.

**Pay, equity and incentives, including for the people whose know-how is captured.** No pay design for captured know-how exists anywhere in the evidence (ledger finding 10). What follows is judgement.

| Who | What they receive | Why this and not something else |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Firm's head | A minority stake in their own firm; partnership-style share of its profit; deferred consideration tied to client retention | R-LIB-GOVERNANCE-05; R-WEB-OWNERSHIP-17 |
| Managers who become workflow owners | The right to buy into the firm's equity over time; a share of the firm's bonus pool, taken partly in firm or group shares held about four years | R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-11 |

**Evidence.** R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-03/10/11/16; R-LIB-FAILURES-01/03. The positive pay design has no evidence.
**One, five, thirty.** As the table above.

## Part 8. Agents as part of the organisation (with Q3)

- **Where they sit.** In the firm, inside a workflow, under a named owner. The centre runs none of a firm's agents.
- **Shape.** A maintained workflow with AI steps by default; one agent where judgement is needed; several only for parallel reading (A001; R-LIB-GOVERNANCE-20; R-LIB-DELIVERY-03). No agent per job title: Sierra tried it and it failed (R-LIB-GOVERNANCE-17).
- **Identity and authority.** Each agent has its own identity, distinct from its owner's, with tools off until the owner turns them on, and limits the system enforces (R-WEB-GOVERNANCE-25/27/28; R-WEB-STACK-14). It works for one client at a time and cannot read across clients (R-LIB-GOVERNANCE-18).
- **Budget.** A monthly money cap per workflow, set by the owner and approved by the head. At the cap the workflow stops and the work falls to people. No working example of an agent budget exists in the evidence (ledger Part 8, unknown); this is a design choice. Token spend is spiky enough to need it (R-WEB-STACK-32).
- **Register.** The firm keeps it: name, owner, purpose, tools, limits, checks, date last reviewed. The centre's audit reads it. The RICS standard already requires a register and a quarterly risk review at regulated property firms (inventory B, S-B-04).
- **Review and retirement.** Quarterly, by the owner with the head. An agent is retired if its override rate is rising, if the same correction keeps recurring, if nobody has used it for a quarter, or if its owner leaves and no one takes it on.

### Q3. The one-page principles, for people and agents

1. **A named person answers for every piece of work.** An agent never does. If no one will own it, it is not handed over. (R-WEB-LAW-16/18; inventory A, S9)
2. **Three bands of action.** *Alone:* reversible, internal, inside written limits. *With approval at the step:* anything irreversible, outward to a client or third party in the firm's name, signed, filed, or that moves money. *Never:* client acceptance, sign-off, payment release, changes to bank details, anything the law gives to a named person. (R-WEB-LAW-17; R-LIB-GOVERNANCE-13; inventory B, S-B-05)
3. **Checks are independent or they do not count.** The checker is not the maker and cannot be reached by the maker. A human reviewer judges before reading the agent's reasoning. High-volume routine work is checked by random sample, and the sample rate is written down. (R-WEB-SELFIMPROVE-24; R-WEB-GOVERNANCE-21; R-WEB-LAW-19; inventory B, S-B-04)
4. **Record the work, not the worker.** Agents' actions are logged in full. People are not recorded unless a stated purpose has been agreed with them. Decisions are made in writing with a named owner. Client material is used only to serve that client unless the client has agreed otherwise. (R-WEB-LAW-01/02/12; R-LIB-GOVERNANCE-05/32; R-WEB-MAPPING-29)
5. **The people who do the work write the playbook, and the owner changes it.** An agent may propose a change. It may not approve one, and it may not read or edit the tests. Every change can be undone, and the undo has been rehearsed. (R-WEB-SELFIMPROVE-02/15/26; R-WEB-STACK-15)
6. **When a person and an agent disagree, the person decides and the disagreement is logged with its class.** If the same disagreement recurs, the owner changes the playbook or the limit. (R-LIB-GOVERNANCE-13 is the nearest case; R-WEB-SELFIMPROVE-17)
7. **Measure outcomes for clients and the firm, never usage.** Review time, errors found by sample, recurrence of corrections, clients served per person, clients kept. (R-LIB-GOVERNANCE-27; R-LIB-FAILURES-04/05)
8. **Clients and staff are told.** Who owns the firm, where AI is used, how to object. (R-WEB-COMMERCIAL-26; R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-24)
9. **Anyone may stop an agent on their own work, at once, without giving a reason first.** (Design choice; follows from 1.)

Gallagher's one page has lasted since 1984 (R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-22), which is the model for the form.

**The two open disputes.**

- *Step approval against limits and budgets (L014 against N076).* No case compares them on the same work (R-LIB-GOVERNANCE-15). I do not pick a side. I sort by consequence, as principle 2 does. Step approval where the act cannot be recalled; limits and sampling where it can. The law allows sampling for routine delegated work (R-WEB-LAW-19; a case about people) and the RICS standard requires it for volume outputs. The tax bodies' guidance reads as review of each tax deliverable (inventory A, S9), and insurers may ask for review of all outputs (R-WEB-LAW-25). Where the insurer or the professional body says "all", the firm does all. Because each firm decides, firm A and firm B may settle this differently, and should.
- *Record everything against its costs (Blomfield against O'Connor, L021, L022).* Blomfield's case is for a loop with a policy layer and a named human (R-LIB-GOVERNANCE-26). The costs are argued, not measured (R-LIB-GOVERNANCE-28), but the law is clear: UK monitoring must be announced, purposeful and the least intrusive means, and consent is not a sound basis (R-WEB-LAW-01/02/03/04). I come down against blanket recording. The firm records what agents do, what reviewers change and why, and what is decided. It does not record screens, keystrokes or calls by default. A team may switch capture on for a stated task for a stated time.

**Decision.** Agents belong to firms, under named owners, on a register, inside three bands.
**Reason.** Regulators, a standards body and three unrelated operators converge on it (ledger Part 8).
**Evidence.** As cited. No outcome evidence for any of it in a services firm.
**One, five, thirty.** One firm: a handful of workflows and one register. Five: five registers, read by one auditor. Thirty: the same, with the audit team sampling registers on a rota.

## Part 9. The management system

Attention is the scarce thing. [source says] At the one firm that measured it, meeting time rose 20% and agent management rose from 10% to 22% of sessions (R-WEB-GOVERNANCE-15/16; the firm also tripled in size). So the routines are few and fixed, and the centre asks for little.

| When | In the firm | Between firm and centre |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Weekly | A standing agenda per direct report; items stay until resolved or dropped. A weekly P&L and a cash note to the head (R-LIB-PEOPLE-20; one property manager's routine, not AI). Each workflow owner looks at their exception counts and review queue | Nothing required. The resident is in the building |
| Monthly | Monthly accounts. The firm board: the measure set on one page, the capacity-to-P&L lines due that month, any incident. Agents prepare the pack; the practice manager signs that it is right | The group partner attends the board. That is the report |
| Quarterly | Workflow review: each owner presents one case, with its baseline, its corrections by class and what changed. Agent register review. The inventory re-scored if a model or a law changed | Heads of firms in the same trade meet, if they wish (R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-13) |
| Yearly | The firm writes its own plan. The centre does not require a budget in a set form (R-WEB-GOVERNANCE-10) but agrees the head's pay against return on capital and growth (R-WEB-GOVERNANCE-03) | Method review. Post-acquisition review at month twelve for each new firm (R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-09). Audit visit |

**The measure set (eight, the same in every firm).** Danaher cut its measures from 150 to eight (R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-04).

1. Clients, or units, served per person.
2. Revenue per person.
3. Client retention.
4. Staff retention.
5. Review time per unit, on the handed-over cases.
6. Errors found by random sample, and errors that reached a client.
7. Recurrence of the same correction (R-WEB-SELFIMPROVE-17).
8. Cash: lock-up days, or client-money reconciliations on time.

No measure of usage, tokens saved or number of automations.

**Escalation.** A problem in a workflow goes to its owner; a problem with a client to the signatory; a problem the head cannot resolve to the group partner, who may advise and may not order, except on a non-negotiable. An error that reaches a client is reported to the firm board at its next meeting and to the insurer as the policy requires.

**When agents do the reporting.** The pack is cheap to produce, so the risk is more reports, not fewer. Constellation distrusts standing reports and prefers an ad hoc analysis of a real problem (R-WEB-GOVERNANCE-05). The rule here: one page a month; anything else is asked for once, for a reason, and lapses.

**Decision.** A weekly rhythm in the firm; a monthly board; eight measures; no central budget ritual.
**Reason.** The lasting groups ran thin, and the one firm of the reference type on record runs on a weekly agenda and a weekly P&L.
**Evidence.** R-LIB-PEOPLE-20; R-WEB-GOVERNANCE-05/10/15; R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-04. Nothing on routines inside an AI roll-up (ledger Part 9, unknown).
**One, five, thirty.** Identical inside each firm. The centre's load grows only by board seats, which is why the span of five to seven sets the number of group partners.

## Part 10. The improvement system (Q4)

Ben's point is that mapping products and one-off projects are static, and that the organisation must keep improving through AI. The ledger's verdict: right as an aim; what exists is a gated, human-approved loop with modest gains; "via AI" overstates what proposes and approves change today (ledger section 3). This stance agrees and goes one step further. The thing that keeps improving is a firm's people and their habits. The loop is a habit with tooling, and the precedent is kaizen, not software release.

**Three things kept apart.**

| | Models getting better | The firm's playbooks, checks and tools getting better | The improvement process getting better |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Who gets it | Every rival | This firm only, to the extent it is specific to its clients | This firm and, through the method, the group |
| What it needs | A fixed, human-written test set per case, re-run at every model or harness change; a register of uses that failed, re-tested at each release (R-LIB-FAILURES-28/29; nobody is recorded doing it) | Corrections classed at source; a regression set from real cases; a named owner; staged release; rehearsed rollback | A quarterly look at whether the loop is producing fewer repeat corrections; the yearly method review |
| Who proposes | The vendor, by releasing | The people doing the work; a trace-reading agent may suggest (R-WEB-SELFIMPROVE-06) | Workflow owners; residents; the method lead |
| What tests it | The fixed test set, then a shadow run (R-WEB-SELFIMPROVE-10) | The regression set, which the proposer cannot read or edit (R-WEB-SELFIMPROVE-24) | The post-acquisition reviews |
| Who approves | The workflow owner | The workflow owner; a second person where output is signed or outward | The centre, after consulting heads; firms choose whether to keep it (R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-08) |
| How undone | Pin the earlier model version where the vendor allows; fall back to people | Version the playbook; roll back; re-review work done since | Drop it. "If they choose to maintain them, then I'm delighted" |
| Note | Not only upward: a rented model got worse between versions (R-WEB-SELFIMPROVE-30; 2023) and harness changes degraded a live product (/19; 2026) | Measured gains are small: about one point in a month at Intercom; 45.3% to 55.8% under a strict gate (/03/15) | Seen only as people rewriting rules after an incident (/19/20) |

**What "ordinary" improvement comes first.** Remove the step. Write the rule per client. Move the check to where the error starts. Chase the client earlier. Stop doing unbilled work without deciding to. These need no model and their gain does not go to every rival.

**How you would know it works, other than by counting changes.** The same correction stops recurring. Review time per unit falls while the sampled error rate does not rise. The capacity-to-P&L lines written before go-live are met at ninety days. Clients served per person rises. If changes are many and these four do not move, the loop is theatre.

**How often such systems last.** [source says] Danaher's has run since 1986 and outlasted changes of portfolio (R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-01/04; company account). No sound failure rate exists for lean programmes: "70%" and "90%" have no source, and one pooled estimate for quality programmes is 37.7% (R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-34; read through a practitioner's review). The one other group Leonard found that ran hundreds of autonomous firms "caved in to increased centralisation" (R-WEB-GOVERNANCE-02). Every AI loop on record is under three years old (ledger Part 15). [inferred] Why they fail, from these rows: a change of owner or horizon; the centre stops trusting the firms; line managers stop teaching it; the measures become the target.

**Full context.** It would need every input, conversation and decision in a readable store. The law does not allow blanket capture of staff (R-WEB-LAW-01 to 04; EU high-risk duties from 2 December 2027, /08). Clients must authorise use of their material for learning (R-WEB-LAW-12/15). And much of it is not in any channel: 101 calendar entries against 11 recorded notes in one build (R-LIB-IMPROVE-TECH-25; one user). This design does not pursue full context. Where the firm cannot record, people write the decision down, the playbook holds the rule, and the agent is kept to the part of the case it can read.

**Marble and the "static" criticism.** Ben's description is accurate with one correction: capture is employee-switched and team-level, with a "measure" step (R-WEB-MAPPING-01/02/03). His criticism is fair for what is evidenced. Where process mining kept paying, an organisation was built round it (R-WEB-MAPPING-22). A firm may rent a capture product for a stated task. It is a tool for a workflow owner, not the improvement system.

**Decision.** A gated loop per case, owned by the manager who runs the case, inside a weekly and quarterly habit; no central loop.
**Reason.** No unattended loop exists in production; the scarce parts are test sets, rubrics and reviewers (ledger finding 7), and those are local.
**Evidence.** R-WEB-SELFIMPROVE-02/03/15/17/24/26; R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-03/08. Almost all software. No measured loop at any accountancy or property firm (ledger Q4, unknown).
**One, five, thirty.** One: three loops. Five: perhaps fifteen, each local; the method lead compares how they are run, not their contents. Thirty: the method itself is on its third or fourth annual revision. Whether playbooks should then be shared between like firms is the question that would move me toward the platform stance (see the last section).

## Part 12. Back office and shared services (Q7)

- **The firm's back office stays in the firm.** Finance, HR, IT and compliance are small at this size (four support staff of 30 at firm A; six central of 50 at firm B, on the models). Berkshire has no group-wide pay or people system (R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-16).
- **It is the proving ground.** The first handover in the hundred days is a back-office workflow: recurring billing, reminders and signature chasing at firm A (lines L2, B5, E4, M5); post routing and office accounts at firm B (J18, J2). A mistake is cheap, and it tests permissions, logging and the owner role before client work.
- **It is not an advantage.** Chroma's objection stands: any owner can rent it (R-LIB-ECONOMICS-11; R-WEB-STACK-23/28). So the firm rents it and the design claims no return from it beyond the hours.
- **What the centre offers.** What Halma's does: acquisition help, legal and compliance guidance, leadership development, technology advice (R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-14). Billed at cost when it is extra work. A firm may decline and hire its own (R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-10).
- **The centre's own back office** (consolidation, deal files, the registers) is run by the same method, with the controller as owner.
- **Deal integration** as back-office work does not exist here beyond finance reporting.

**Decision.** Leave it local, rent the tools, use it as rehearsal, share only advice.
**Reason.** The lasting decentralised groups did not consolidate it (R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-07/15), though one teacher of roll-ups calls consolidation the core work (R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-32). I side with the former and show the disagreement.
**Evidence.** R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-10/14/16; R-WEB-STACK-27 (month-end 7.5 days faster on one vendor's bookkeeping; pre-agent tools). Nothing on HR, compliance or IT cases (ledger Part 12).
**One, five, thirty.** Unchanged. At thirty, a firm-to-firm market in services may appear (firm A doing the books for the property firms). It is allowed, at arm's length, and not required.

**Unit economics per service.** Not held for either trade (ledger Part 14). The first hundred days produce them: handling time, review time and exceptions per case, for the three cases.

**The bridge**, when there are inputs, is A003's: removable roles or avoided hires, plus contribution from volume the freed capacity serves at a viable price, less model cost, review, exception handling and maintenance; transition spend shown apart; no hour counted twice (R-LIB-ECONOMICS-31). In this design the first term is avoided hires only.

**Transition cost and when cash appears.** Cash goes out for at least a year (R-LIB-STRUCTURE-16; self-reported). [inferred] It comes back, if at all, when the next busy season is met without the hires it would have needed, and when the freed partner time has won clients. For firm A that is the second January after purchase. No conversion of saved hours to profit has been measured anywhere (R-LIB-ECONOMICS-30).

**The centre's P&L.** Income: the group's share of firms' distributable profit, and billed extras. Cost: capped headcount. [source says] Constellation reports head office falling from 3.0% to 0.5% of net revenue (R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-10; software; self-reported). [inferred] On the model figures, firm A's profit before partners' pay is about £0.8m, and less once working partners are paid a salary. One firm cannot pay for a managing partner, a resident and an engineer. The centre at one firm is an investment funded from capital. At five firms of this size it is tight. This is shown, not hidden, in the fit section.

**Capital allocation.** Almost all capital buys firms. There is no platform to build. The spend that is not purchase price is residents and engineers, treated as part of the cost of each deal and reviewed at month twelve.

**The measures that show it is working (L017).** The eight in Part 9, plus two at the centre: return on the capital put into each firm including the resident and engineer, and the cost of the latest firm's first year against the first firm's.

**Decision.** Price firms as they stand; plan a year of lower profit; count only avoided hires and sold capacity; fund the centre from capital until scale.
**Reason.** No measured conversion exists; listed peers with wide AI use still earn 10 to 15% (R-WEB-COMMERCIAL-02/07/09) against roll-up targets of 30 to 40% (R-WEB-ROLLUPS-25).
**Evidence.** Methods and absences. No inputs.
**One, five, thirty.** One: the centre loses money. Five: about break-even is the aim, unproven. Thirty: the centre should be a small share of group profit if the cap holds.

## Part 15. How it changes over time

| Stage | What the organisation looks like |
| --- | --- |
| Founding | Two or three people, capital committed for the long term, a method on a few pages, no firm |
| After the first firm | One board seat, one resident, three cases live or stopped, the first post-acquisition review, the method rewritten once |
| Five firms | A group partner, a controller with audit, residents who came from firm one, base rates beginning to mean something |
| Steady state, about thirty | Five or six group partners with their own capital authority; a new layer was needed at about this size elsewhere (R-WEB-GOVERNANCE-07). Whether agents raise that span is unknown (ledger Part 15) |

**As models improve.** Each firm re-runs its fixed test sets and its register of failed uses at each release. Scaffolding is expected to be thrown away: "every model that comes out makes the things we added for the last one pointless" (R-LIB-PEOPLE-23). The inventory is re-scored; lines move from "assist" to "hand over". What does not move: the organisational failures (R-LIB-PEOPLE-05; R-LIB-FAILURES-22) and the rules on who answers. Because nothing was built centrally, there is little to write off.

**As prices fall.** A rival cuts its fee by a third. The head decides, client by client. The firm has no debt to service and no platform fee to cover, so it can follow where it must. Goodwill in a labour-based service was written down within three years of the shift at Concentrix (R-WEB-COMMERCIAL-09); paying a full price for unreformed earnings is this design's largest balance-sheet risk, and the reason for the no-auction rule.

**Regulatory dates to plan round.** ICAEW title result and US split-structure rules in 2027; UK leasehold reform on 1 April and 1 November 2027; EU high-risk duties on 2 December 2027 (R-WEB-LAW-08/31; R-WEB-OWNERSHIP-08; inventory B, S-B-08).

**Decision.** Stay decentralised on purpose; add a layer near thirty; rebuild scaffolding with each model generation.
**Reason.** Leonard's account of what tempts groups to centralise, and the rentability of the tools.
**Evidence.** R-WEB-GOVERNANCE-02/07; R-LIB-PEOPLE-23; R-WEB-MAPPING-25. One operator's account.
**One, five, thirty.** As the table.

## Fit: do the parts belong together?

**1. Do the incentives (Part 7) reward what the improvement system (Part 10) needs?**

| The loop needs | The incentive |
| --- | --- |
| People to write down what they know | Credited time, named authorship, the undertaking on roles, a flat share of firm profit |
| Owners who keep a playbook true for years | Firm equity bought over time; bonuses held as shares about four years |
| Reviewers who judge independently | Seniors judged on sampled review quality, not throughput |
| Heads who invest through a bad year | A minority stake; no exit clock; no change of bonus plan (R-WEB-GOVERNANCE-03) |
| Nobody gaming activity | No reward for usage, hours or number of changes |

They fit. The weak joint: the undertaking on roles depends on growth. In a firm that turns out to be demand-limited the incentive and the economics pull apart.

**2. Do the decision rights (Part 3) match the liability (Part 13)?** Yes, by construction. Whoever answers to the regulator and the insurer decides client acceptance, sign-off, review policy and the limits on their agents. The centre carries capital risk and decides capital. The one mismatch: the centre can replace the head but cannot order a change, so a head who is competent and unwilling can stall the whole thesis at that firm for a year or two before the centre acts. Constellation accepts the same cost.

**3. Do the economics (Part 14) pay for the centre drawn (Part 7)?** Not at one firm. At one firm the centre is three people funded from capital against a firm whose whole profit before partners' pay is about £0.8m on the model. At five firms of this size, a centre of about eight to ten people including residents and engineers is paid for only if residents and engineers are treated as deal cost and charged to the firms they serve; the standing centre (partners, controller) must then live on a small share of perhaps £12m to £17m of combined revenue (my arithmetic on the inventories' estimates; weak). It works at thirty if the cap holds. So this design needs patient capital that accepts a subscale centre for years, or larger firms than the references. That is a real constraint and I do not think it can be designed away.

**4. Does the pace (Part 4) fit the purpose (Part 1)?** Yes for permanence; no if the gain is a two-year window ("a golden period of a couple of years", R-LIB-ECONOMICS-14; an unsupported forecast). The design's answer is that it does not depend on the window.

**5. Does "little shared" (Parts 11 and 12) fit "method shared" (Parts 6 and 10)?** Mostly. The risk is that a method with no shared tooling is only advice, and advice can be ignored. The audit, the eight measures and the power to replace the head are what make it more than that.

## Where this stance is weakest

1. **Its precedents bought firms that did not need changing.** Buffett's managers "were doing exactly that before we acquired their businesses" (R-WEB-GOVERNANCE-10). Constellation's units were software businesses left to run. Nothing in the precedents combines firm autonomy with rebuilding the firm's work (ledger finding 10). This design asks an autonomous head to rebuild their own firm with one resident's help. The only precedent that did rebuild work, Danaher, mandated its system (R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-02/03). This is the weakest point.
2. **Its advocate has no deals and no results** (R-LIB-STRUCTURE-20; N052: partnerships "in discussion and none signed"), and the one convinced owner on record got poor results in a year (N015; R-LIB-FAILURES-08; 2025 models).
3. **Little compounds between firms.** Each firm rebuilds its inventory, rules, checks and test sets. If playbooks do carry between like firms (Long Lake's founder estimates 80% of infrastructure does, R-LIB-STRUCTURE-15; Shield reuses runbooks, R-LIB-STRUCTURE-25; both self-reported), this design leaves that on the table.
4. **A small firm may not be able to carry the build.** One practice engineer against a 30-person firm's profit is a large cost, and the verifier that both inventories say is the main thing to build is engineering work.
5. **The centre does not pay for itself for years** (fit, point 3).
6. **It is slow.** If rivals do capture a temporary gain and use it to buy the best firms, a one-at-a-time buyer arrives late.
7. **Its protective claim is thinner than its source says.** The licensed signature covers under 5% of hours at firm A and nothing at firm B (both inventories).

## What evidence would make me abandon it

- A platform roll-up publishes an audited before-and-after margin for acquired firms, with a baseline, and shows the cost of its latest firm at a small fraction of its first. Either alone would weaken this stance; both would end it.
- In our own first two firms, the month-twelve reviews show that the second firm's three cases cost as much and took as long as the first's, and that the playbooks written at the first would have worked at the second with small changes. That is direct evidence that sharing pays and I chose not to.
- Two autonomous heads in a row leave the work as it was after eighteen months with a resident in the building.
- Fees for small-business accountancy or block management fall quickly and visibly, so that speed of cost-out matters more than depth of knowledge.
- A regulator or insurer accepts certification of the agent system in place of a named person's review of outputs (R-LIB-GOVERNANCE-06 proposes it; Chroma names it as its own falsifier, R-LIB-ECONOMICS-12). The local signatory then stops being the natural place for decisions.

## Which type of business it suits worst

Firms whose work is the same from one to the next, high in volume, visible to the client by the unit, and short of demand, not capacity. Lettings is the nearest case in this run: Dwelly migrates agencies on to one system and claims two months to do it (R-WEB-ROLLUPS-08/09; self-reported), and firm B's lettings side is demand-limited (inventory B section 1). Further along the same line: outsourced customer contact, translation, IT run work, where price fell with the technology (R-WEB-COMMERCIAL-05/07/12) and scale in tooling is the business. There, knowing each firm deeply buys little, and a shared platform, or building new, is the better design. It also suits badly any firm too small to spare a manager as a workflow owner, and any firm whose head is leaving.

It suits best a firm like the block side of firm B or the compliance core of firm A: short of capacity, paid by fixed fee, held by trust and local knowledge, with client-specific rules that only its own people can write.
