# Design of the whole organisation: the central-platform stance

Stage 4 of the run. Written 23 November 2026. One designer, working alone, no sub-agents. Nothing was written to Notion.

**The stance, argued as its best advocate would.** One operating system and one agent platform across the group. Acquired firms converge on it on a fixed timetable. The centre is strong: it owns the platform, the release gate, the management system and the integration team. This is Long Lake's position, with Dwelly's migration practice and Danaher's mandated system as the nearest precedents.

**What I read.** The brief, the ledger and both inventories in full. I did not reopen the evidence files; every row ID below is cited from the ledger and was not re-checked against its source. Inventory sources are cited as S1 to S21 (firm A) and S-B-01 to S-B-17 (firm B).

**How sure I am, overall.** Sure of the legal skeleton (who is accountable, what may be recorded, where debt kills). Fairly sure of the delivery design, because both inventories point the same way. Not sure of the economics: no AI roll-up has published a result with a baseline (R-WEB-ROLLUPS closing summary; R-LIB-ECONOMICS-01). The central claim of this stance, that the later firm costs much less to convert than the first, has no measured support in services (ledger Part 4, unknown). I argue it anyway and name it as the weakest premise in section 18.

**The case in six lines.**

1. [source says] Both inventories found that a firm does not need a build per line. It needs a few shared things built once: an independent check, written limits, a tested write path, and a way to read state out of inboxes (inventory B 3.1; inventory A section 4, point 1). [inferred] Things built once are a platform. A group that builds them once per firm pays for them thirty times.
2. [source says] The expensive, scarce parts are test sets, rubrics and reviewers, not tooling (ledger finding 7; R-WEB-SELFIMPROVE-26/35). [inferred] A test set from thirty firms' real traffic is better than one firm's, and only a centre can hold it.
3. [source says] The regulator found controls that did not scale and decisions taken in the wrong place (R-WEB-OWNERSHIP-20). An acquirer of 230 firms with no shared systems failed (R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-33). [inferred] One register, one gate and one set of books is the cheapest way to meet that.
4. [source says] A rented model can change under the firm, and prompt changes that passed tests degraded a live product for days to weeks (R-WEB-SELFIMPROVE-19/30). [inferred] Re-testing every workflow at every model release is a job a 30-person firm cannot staff.
5. [source says] The one mandated operating system among the precedents has run since 1986 (R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-01/04; company account).
6. [source says] The autonomous groups that lasted bought firms that "were doing exactly that before" (R-WEB-GOVERNANCE-10). An AI roll-up assumes the firm's work must change. [inferred] Autonomy is the right design when nothing has to change. Here something does.

## Part 1. Purpose and where it plays

The group buys small firms that do codified, recurring, fixed-fee work for small clients. It rebuilds their delivery on one platform and uses the freed capacity to serve more clients with the same people. It is betting that the hard parts of handing work to AI are shared across firms of one type, so the cost per firm falls as firms are added.

**One vertical at a time.** [inferred] A platform is only a platform inside a type of work. Firm A's VAT check is no use to firm B's repair case. The shared layers across verticals are thin: identity, permissions, audit, cost metering, evaluation tooling, finance and the management routines. The thick layers (playbooks, checks, test sets, exception classes) are per vertical. So the group enters one vertical, and enters a second only when the cost of converting the latest firm is measurably below the first.

**Which vertical first.** My stance prefers UK non-audit accountancy. [source says] The work "is set by statute and looks alike from firm to firm" (inventory A); the firm is short of capacity and the law is adding work (S17, S18). Against: 46% of surveyed UK mid-tier firms are already backed by private equity (R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-26), and legal protection is thin outside audit (inventory A section 3). Block management has the better operating evidence (R-LIB-DELIVERY-12, self-reported) and an unknown write path. Low confidence. This is one of the first decisions for Ben.

**What it will not do.** Statutory audit (ownership is reserved, R-WEB-LAW-29/30). Law in countries that bar investors (R-WEB-LAW-28). Raising prices at acquired local firms as the plan (R-WEB-OWNERSHIP-25; R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-23/24). Work for enterprise clients with procurement functions (R-WEB-COMMERCIAL-15/18). Germany and the Netherlands until the group has run a works-council process once (R-WEB-LAW-09/10).

**Horizon.** Long hold, in a company and not a fund. [source says] Fund investors in this sector expect to leave in three to seven years (R-WEB-OWNERSHIP-06). When Constellation was put up for sale, managers cut long-term spending (R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-11). [inferred] A platform is long-term spending by definition. If the capital has a clock, say so to sellers and staff, and expect the platform budget to be the first thing cut in the last two years.

**Q8: how the design changes by type of business.**

| Dimension | What it changes in this design | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Licence needed or not | Legal form; who signs; whether the firm can be owned at all | R-WEB-LAW-26 to 33. Strong |
| Pricing basis and who pays | Whether a saving stays until renewal. Fixed fee holds it; hourly passes it on | R-LIB-ECONOMICS-02/03/04/06; R-WEB-COMMERCIAL-04. Fair |
| How checkable the outcome is, and how soon | Which control applies: sampling, step approval, or leave with people. It also sets how much of the platform's check can be reused | R-LIB-GOVERNANCE-11; both inventories. Fair |
| Capacity or demand constrained | Whether freed hours become revenue. Decides if there is any return | R-LIB-ECONOMICS-09 (a rival buyer's claim); R-LIB-FAILURES-16 |
| How alike the work is from firm to firm | **Added by this stance.** Decides whether a platform pays at all | [inferred]; R-LIB-IMPROVE-TECH-36 says each firm's process is "bespoke" even where the noun is shared |
| Physical work | Caps the share of hours a platform can reach (22% of firm B's hours stay with people, 8,300 of them for presence) | Inventory B 3.3, estimates |
| Client size | Enterprise buyers take the saving | R-WEB-COMMERCIAL-15/18 |
| Country | Employment and ownership law only | R-WEB-LAW-09/10/28 |
| Global or local; advice or coordination | Little by themselves, once the rows above are known | No evidence either way (ledger Q8, unknown) |

The four that drive the design: licence, pricing basis and payer, checkability, and the capacity-or-demand constraint. For this stance a fifth matters as much: likeness of work between firms.

**Decision.** One vertical first, UK non-audit accountancy preferred; long-hold company; no reliance on price rises.
**Reason.** Reuse is the whole case for a centre, and reuse needs like work.
**Evidence.** Rules and filings for what to avoid. Judgement for the choice of vertical. None for the reuse claim.
**At one, five, thirty.** One firm: a single vertical and a centre that is plainly too big for it. Five: still one vertical, one region. Thirty: perhaps two verticals, each with its own playbook library, sharing the thin layers.

## Part 2. Ownership, capital and legal form

**Structure.** [source says] Where control is reserved, the documented structure is a licensed firm served by an investor-owned service company under a services agreement (R-WEB-OWNERSHIP-06/09; R-WEB-LAW-32).

```
Investors + sellers + staff share plan
              |
        HoldCo (group)
        /            \
  PlatformCo        Firm entities (one per acquired firm)
  - employs the centre     - hold the client contracts
  - owns the platform,     - hold memberships, agent status,
    playbook library,        supervised status, PI cover
    test sets, register    - employ fee earners and signatories
  - sells services to      - board: firm head, a group
    firms under a            director, the senior signatory
    written agreement
                      Audit entity (if any): controlled by
                      the responsible individual, outside
                      the group, or the audit book is sold
```

- **Audit.** Left out. Firm A's audit book is about 12 signatures and under 5% of hours (inventory A section 3). It goes to an entity the responsible individual controls, or is sold. [source says] A fund-owned group's audit firm cannot audit the group's other companies (R-WEB-LAW-30) and CBIZ dropped client work for independence conflicts (R-WEB-OWNERSHIP-11).
- **The title.** [source says] ICAEW proposes that investor-controlled firms may not use "Chartered Accountants"; results due 2027 (R-WEB-LAW-31). The design assumes the title may be lost and does not rest the brand on it.
- **Who owns the know-how.** PlatformCo owns the platform, the shared playbooks and the test sets. Each firm keeps its client files and its client-specific rules. [source says] Client data may not train models without explicit authority (R-WEB-LAW-12/15). So shared playbooks carry method, never client material, and the engagement terms say so.
- **How the platform is charged.** Two charges. Shared services at cost, split by headcount. The platform as a price per unit of work (per return, per client-month, per block), set once a year, so each firm head can see the centre's cost and complain if it rises. [source says] Constellation cut head-office cost by billing for extra services (R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-10). The formula's values are open; no group discloses one (ledger Part 2, unknown).
- **Debt.** None on a firm entity. No guarantees by firm entities of HoldCo debt (R-WEB-OWNERSHIP-19). No debt sized on AI savings. Any HoldCo acquisition debt must be serviceable from the firms' earnings as bought, with the centre's cost counted. How much that is, is open (R-LIB-STRUCTURE-08). [source says] CBIZ's interest tripled after one deal (R-WEB-OWNERSHIP-10); RSM Tenon, Slater and Gordon and Ince failed after debt-funded buying (R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-28; R-WEB-OWNERSHIP-31/32/33).

**What sellers keep.** Platform stance: sellers and staff hold **group** shares, not shares in their own firm.

| Element | Design | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cash at completion | A share of price; the share is open | No UK percentages on file (ledger Part 2, unknown) |
| Rolled equity | In HoldCo, held about four years | Constellation's holding rule (R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-11). Group shares reward convergence; firm shares reward defending the firm's own way |
| Deferred payment | One to three years, paid on client fee retention, staff retention and migration milestones met. Not on the firm's profit or hours | [source says] Lasting acquirers tie sellers in for one to three years (R-WEB-OWNERSHIP-22/30; R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-20). [inferred] An earn-out on profit rewards steady hours in the years the redesign cuts hours (ledger Part 2, contradiction) |
| Seller's role | Stays as firm head or senior signatory for the deferred period | Supa lets sellers leave; every lasting acquirer does the opposite (ledger Part 2) |
| Never | Payment for steering clients to group products | Treated as a conflict (R-WEB-OWNERSHIP-21) |

**Decision.** HoldCo, PlatformCo, firm entities; audit out; group equity for sellers and staff; no debt on firms.
**Reason.** It is the only documented structure, and group equity is the only incentive that points at the platform.
**Evidence.** Strong on structure and debt. None on percentages, prices or the fee formula.
**At one, five, thirty.** One: HoldCo, PlatformCo and one firm entity; equity-funded. Five: the unit price is tested against five firm heads who can compare notes. Thirty: PlatformCo could license the system outside the group, as Danaher did (R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-06); a regional sub-holding layer appears.

## Part 3. Governance and decision rights

The rule behind the table: the centre decides what is shared and what is capital. The licensed or accountable person decides what goes to a client under their name, and can stop anything on their work. An agent decides only inside limits a named person has set and the system enforces.

| Decision | Centre | Firm | Agent | Who can stop it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Buying, selling, capital, debt | Decides (HoldCo board) | Firm head consulted | None | HoldCo board |
| Appointing, paying, replacing the firm head | Decides | None | None | HoldCo board |
| Group accounts, chart of accounts, internal audit | Decides | Supplies data | Prepares reports | Group finance director |
| Platform, shared tools, model choice, vendors | Decides | May object in writing; objection goes to the monthly review | None | Platform lead; risk lead on security grounds |
| Release of a **shared** playbook, check or test set | Decides, through the release gate | Workflow owners propose and test | May propose; may not read or edit the checks | Release gate; any signatory may refuse it on their own work |
| A firm's client-specific rules (this client's transfers are drawings) | None | Workflow owner decides | May propose | Workflow owner |
| The agent register: creating, changing, retiring an agent | Decides | Names the sponsor | None | Risk lead; the sponsor |
| An agent's limits (money, contact, write access) | Sets the ceiling | Sponsor sets within the ceiling | Acts inside them | Sponsor; any signatory on their work |
| Accepting or disengaging a client | None, except a conflict veto | Decides (firm head; reporting officer on risk) | Prepares checks | Firm's reporting officer |
| Sign-off of work that leaves under the firm's name | None | Named signatory decides | Prepares; never signs | The signatory |
| Client money: mandates, payment release | Audits | Principal decides, dual authorisation | Never | The principal (S-B-05) |
| Prices and fee changes | Sets the pricing method and floor | Decides per client | Drafts | Firm head |
| Reversible, internal, within-limit acts (coding a transaction, chasing a document) | None | Owner reviews by sample | Decides | Owner; system limits |
| Outward, irreversible or signed acts (filing, payment, a notice, advice) | None | Person approves each one | Prepares | Approver |
| Whether a client's work runs without AI | None | Firm head, on the client's written request | None | The client (S-B-04 requires an opt-out to be stated) |

Three boards. HoldCo board: capital, firm heads, risk appetite. PlatformCo operating committee: the release gate, the register, incidents. Each firm's board: client acceptance, sign-off standards, complaints, with one group director present. [source says] The FCA found decisions about regulated firms taken by unregulated boards (R-WEB-OWNERSHIP-20). So nothing in the "firm decides" column can be overruled by the centre except by replacing the firm head, which is Constellation's one hard power (R-WEB-GOVERNANCE-01).

**Where the two firms disagree about a shared procedure.** The domain lead at the centre decides the shared default. A firm may keep a local variant if its workflow owner accepts the cost of maintaining and testing it, and that cost appears on the firm's P&L. Variants are reviewed each quarter. [inferred] This is how a strong centre avoids both a veto by every firm and a mandate nobody follows. No precedent; Constellation lets firms let a pilot lapse (R-WEB-GOVERNANCE-05), Danaher does not.

**Decision.** The table above.
**Reason.** It follows liability as the rules place it (R-WEB-LAW-16/18/22) and puts shared things with the centre.
**Evidence.** Rules for the firm and signatory columns. No AI-era precedent for the table as a whole (ledger Part 3).
**At one, five, thirty.** One: the three boards are the same six people; write the table down anyway. Five: the release gate needs a standing weekly slot. Thirty: a divisional layer sits between HoldCo and firms; [source says] one operator ran out of span at about thirty units (R-WEB-GOVERNANCE-07) and Halma runs five to seven boards per divisional head (R-WEB-GOVERNANCE-11).

## Part 4. The acquisition engine

A staffed function with three teams: deals, launch (integration) and domain.

**Sourcing and screen.** Firms in the chosen vertical; fixed-fee recurring income; short of capacity; a system of record the platform already reads and writes; a head who will stay; no client above a set share of fees (the share is open).

**Diligence on the work itself.** Four things, in about a week on site:

1. The light inventory. Take the practice-management or property-system export and map it onto the group's standard catalogue for the vertical. Score each line on the task test (Part 6). The output is the hours-by-status table and the binding-item table, as in both inventories. For firm N it is a comparison against the catalogue, not a fresh list.
2. A write-path test on every system of record. [source says] One vendor refused the write path (R-LIB-IMPROVE-TECH-34); HMRC bans automation of its web pages (S10).
3. A sample of past files re-performed to get an error rate. [source says] No error rate was found for any line (inventory A, could not find, item 8). Without one, nobody knows what today's reviews are worth.
4. Questions to the firm's insurer and its professional body on AI-assisted work, in writing, before exchange.

**Price.** Pay a price that works with no AI gain at all. Keep A003's three scenarios (R-LIB-ECONOMICS-32). [source says] Small practices change hands at about 0.8 to 1.3 times recurring fees (S19; brokers' marketing, search summaries only).

**Integration: the sequence.**

| When | What happens | Who |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Diligence | Inventory, write-path test, file sample, insurer question, a plan for telling staff and clients | Deal lead, domain lead, launch manager |
| Day one | Ownership announced to staff and clients, with the group named. The no-cuts rule given in writing. Bank mandates and delegated authorities reset. Nothing changes in client work. No recording of anyone | Firm head, launch manager |
| Days 1 to 30 | Group chart of accounts; weekly P&L and cash; baseline of the eight measures (Part 9), including review time, which nobody measures today. Identity, permissions and audit logging switched on | Launch manager, group finance |
| Days 30 to 100 | Data protection assessment and staff consultation on what will be logged. Back-office workflows live first. Workflow owners named. Test sets assembled from the firm's past files. First client cases run in shadow | Domain lead, embedded engineers, workflow owners |
| Days 100 to 365 | Three whole cases live with their designed checks. AI and ownership clause into engagement terms at each renewal. The firm's own system of record migrated to the group standard after the first peak season, not before. One full demand cycle measured | Workflow owners, launch manager |
| Month 12 | Review of the deal against its assumptions; the measured engineering and adoption cost of this firm set against the first | HoldCo board |

[source says] Dwelly's launch team joins diligence and runs announcements, baselining, migration and accounting, at about five businesses per launch manager (R-WEB-ROLLUPS-08 to 11; advertisements show intent, not results). Danaher asks for a plan inside 100 days (R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-02). Constellation reviews each deal after a year (R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-09). Long Lake's first firms took over a year to show outcomes, with engineers on site about two years (R-LIB-STRUCTURE-13/16; self-reported).

**Pace.** No second firm until the first has run one full cycle on the platform. After that, pace is set by launch managers, not by capital. [source says] Teachers of the method name pace, poor integration, debt and price as the causes of failure (R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-31). Long Lake's ten deals in under five months (R-WEB-ROLLUPS-07) is the part of the Long Lake position I do not adopt.

**The engine's own measures.** Days to first group-format accounts. Client fee retention at 12 months. Regretted staff leavers at 12 months. Engineer-weeks and practitioner-weeks to reach three live cases, firm by firm. The last is the number A003 says to ask for, and the one that proves or kills this stance.

**Decision.** A three-team engine, a fixed entry sequence, finance and data first, agents second, one firm per cycle until the cost curve is seen.
**Reason.** Integration is staffed work and is mostly not AI (ledger finding 9).
**Evidence.** Fair for the function (advertisements and operators' accounts). None for the cost curve.
**At one, five, thirty.** One: the centre is the launch team. Five: one or two launch managers; a written playbook; the catalogue stable. Thirty: launch teams per region; several firms a year; the catalogue is the main diligence tool.

## Part 5. Clients and the commercial side

- **What is sold.** The same services under the same fixed fees. The firm sells its freed capacity: more clients, and the services it turns away today. For firm A that includes the new quarterly reporting work (S17). Productising applies to a minority of lines: fixed procedure, a unit price, an outside reader. Inventory B puts that at about ten of 123 lines (inventory B section 4).
- **Price.** Existing terms held. New pricing tested on new services first. [source says] Pricing models are slow to change for services clients already buy (R-WEB-COMMERCIAL-04). Untested for small-business clients.
- **Who owns the client.** The firm entity holds the contract; a named person at the firm holds the relationship. The centre never contacts a client. [source says] Gallagher has lost brokers "along with their clients" (R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-21).
- **Brand.** The firm's own name, with "part of [group]" on every letterhead and engagement letter. [source says] Quiet group ownership under a local name was regulated against in vets (R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-24). A single brand is a decision for thirty firms, not one.
- **What clients are told.** Ownership on day one. AI use in the engagement terms at the next renewal, with a clause on use of client material and, where the professional rule requires it, how to opt out (R-WEB-COMMERCIAL-26; S9; S-B-04). "Nothing changes" (R-LIB-ECONOMICS-23) is not the line.
- **Cross-selling.** Referral between firms only where the client asks and no one is paid for the steer (R-WEB-OWNERSHIP-21). Not a pillar of the plan.
- **When the client has the same tools.** [source says] One in five accounting clients has used AI to check their accountant (R-WEB-COMMERCIAL-25; a US vendor survey). A buyer forced an audit fee down about 14% (R-WEB-COMMERCIAL-01). [inferred] Small clients will not bring payroll or a service-charge ledger in-house soon, but they will ask for a lower fee at renewal. The plan assumes they get some of it (Part 15).
- **Selling.** [source says] Freed capacity is not profit until someone sells it (R-LIB-FAILURES-16). The centre runs one growth lead and shared lead handling; partners and property managers still close. An agent may book and chase; it does not sell (R-LIB-GOVERNANCE-14).

**Decision.** Local name with open group ownership; disclosure in the terms; hold prices; sell capacity.
**Reason.** Rules point one way on disclosure, and price rises have a regulatory ending.
**Evidence.** Rules, strong. Client reaction to disclosure, none (ledger Part 5, unknown).
**At one, five, thirty.** One: nothing visible but the letterhead. Five: a shared pricing method and shared lead handling. Thirty: enough clients that a regulator may look at local concentration; a group brand question.

## Part 6. Delivery (Q1 and Q2)

### Q1. How the work is found

Ben's hypothesis was a full list of 200 things, turned into products. [source says] The evidence goes against that as a first move: no case began from a full inventory and reported, and every case started narrow (ledger section 3). Both inventories add a qualification: a light list is cheap where the trade is codified, and it found the shared blockers that a narrow start would have missed (inventory A section 4; inventory B section 4).

The platform stance adds one thing: **the list is the group's, not the firm's.** A standard catalogue per vertical, about 90 to 125 lines, with fixed line IDs. Each firm is scored against it.

| Level | Used for | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Line | Scoring and diligence | "VAT return preparation and submission" |
| Case | Building and running. A case crosses lines | A repair from report to paid invoice; a company's year end from records request to filing |
| Domain | Building the check and the test set | One tax domain, about six weeks of engineers and practitioners (R-WEB-STACK-04; one origin) |
| Step or document | Selling, where a unit price fits | A sales pack |

What is recorded per line: trigger, volume, who does it, how it is checked now, price basis, where inputs live, exception classes with counts, the twelve test scores, status, binding item, named owner. Exceptions are classed by cause (missing information, blocked tool, policy, judgement) and by consequence (R-LIB-DELIVERY-12/13/14).

How it stays true: the work updates it. Each new exception class, each change in law and each model release amends a row. The domain lead at the centre owns the catalogue. Rows are re-scored at every model release. Nobody runs a mapping exercise twice.

### Q2. The task test

Ben's four are necessary and not sufficient (ledger finding 6). [source says] A quoting workflow with all four scored 92.8% on tests and 22.3% in production (R-LIB-DELIVERY-05; late 2025 to January 2026 models).

First question, before the test: will plain rules or the firm's existing software do? (R-LIB-DELIVERY-34.) If yes, no agent.

| # | Item | Gate? | Yes means |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Readable state | | Every input is in a system the agent can read |
| 2 | Written playbook with exception classes | | Written by the people who do the work, including this client's rules |
| 3 | A way to write | | A tested, permitted write path |
| 4 | A check independent of the maker | **Gate** | Something the agent cannot reach, and not a person reading the agent's own file |
| 5 | A clear trigger and complete inputs | | |
| 6 | Authority and limits | **Gate** | Stated per client and enforced by the system |
| 7 | A named person who has agreed to own the agent's version, and a route for each exception class | **Gate** | Re-worded from the inventories, where "someone owns this today" sorted nothing |
| 8 | Undo, or a human gate before the irreversible | **Gate** | |
| 9 | A test set from real past cases | | Held where the maker cannot read it |
| 10 | Volume and value enough to pay for build and upkeep | | Scored across the group, not per firm |
| 11 | The client or third party supplies inputs in time | | Added by inventory A |
| 12 | An outside body permits the write path and the method | **Gate** | HMRC, a regulator, an insurer |

Status follows from the scores. Any gate failed: assist only, or leave with people. All gates met and no N: hand over, with sampling. Gates met but 1, 2, 3 or 9 partly met: build the missing thing, then hand over.

**Scoring a firm.** Hours-weighted share by status, and hours by binding item. The second table is the build list. Under this stance item 10 is scored for the group: a line too thin to pay at one firm may pay across ten. [inferred] That is the platform's main effect on the test. It is also why a volume threshold, which nobody has (ledger Q1, unknown), matters less here.

**The check.** [source says] A human sign-off is a weak check unless designed to be independent: analysts followed the agent 86% of the time (R-WEB-GOVERNANCE-21; one vendor-run trial of 50); accountants deferred to software (R-WEB-STACK-27). So: tie-outs and recomputation by separate code; sampled review by someone who is not the maker and who judges before seeing the agent's reasoning; the signatory's review timed and reported. [source says] Where automation worked, the check was most of what remained (R-LIB-DELIVERY-29; R-WEB-MAPPING-06).

**Decision.** A group catalogue scored at diligence; whole cases built, three at a time; the twelve-item test with five gates.
**Reason.** Both inventories found the blockers shared and the row the wrong unit to build.
**Evidence.** Company accounts and two desk exercises. No controlled comparison (R-LIB-DELIVERY-37).
**At one, five, thirty.** One: the catalogue is a draft; three cases. Five: the catalogue is stable and item 10 starts to pass on thinner lines. Thirty: most "designed check" lines have a check; the centre's work shifts to upkeep and re-testing.

### A firm (A, after year one)

```
Firm head (a former partner)                  on the firm board
 |- Signatories (partners)        sign; accept clients; can stop any agent on their work

 Embedded for year one: one engineer and the domain lead, employed by PlatformCo
```

**Roles.** New: workflow owner, reviewer as a defined job, the embedded engineer, the domain lead ([source says] a "legal engineer" writing acceptance criteria is advertised at $190–270K, R-WEB-STACK-10). Changed: bookkeepers and payroll staff move from making to handling exceptions; managers from reading files to owning workflows; the practice manager adds the register. Gone in year one: none, by rule.

**The no-cuts rule.** No role is removed for automation until a full demand cycle has been measured on the new workflow. Staff get this in writing on day one, with the honest second half: the plan needs fewer hours per unit of work, growth is meant to absorb them, and where it does not, leavers are not replaced. [source says] CBA re-hired 45 roles within a month; Ford re-hired about 300 engineers (R-LIB-FAILURES-01/03). Dwelly has a framework for "staff not continuing" (R-WEB-ROLLUPS-10). I would not claim, as Long Lake does, to be "net adding jobs" (R-WEB-ROLLUPS-04/25).

### Incentives, including for people whose know-how is captured

[source says] No pay or equity design for these people exists anywhere in the evidence (ledger finding 10). What follows is judgement.

| Who | What they receive | Paid on | Not paid on |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Firm head and signatories | Group shares held about four years; deferred price | Client fee retention; quality (errors reaching clients; regulator and complaint record); migration milestones | Firm profit in the transition years |
| Workflow owner | A pay step for the role; group share units vesting over four years; a reuse payment when their playbook passes the release gate at another firm | The case's measures: review time, override rate, recurrence of the same correction | Number of playbooks or changes |
| Anyone who writes down know-how that enters a shared playbook | Their name on it; credited time (R-WEB-PEOPLE-21); a share of the reuse payment; first claim on the workflow-owner and reviewer roles | The playbook being adopted and its corrections falling | Usage of AI tools, ever |
| Reviewers and exception handlers | A pay step; training time; share units | Disagreements with the agent that prove right on audit | Throughput alone |
| Everyone | A flat share of group profit once there is one (R-WEB-PEOPLE-23); the no-cuts rule | Group result | |
| Centre staff | Group shares held about four years | Cost of the latest firm against the first; the eight measures across firms | Number of deals |

[source says] Usage rewards drove cost at Uber and fear at Proton (R-LIB-FAILURES-05; R-LIB-PEOPLE-14). Activity measures are gamed once they affect pay (R-LIB-GOVERNANCE-27). A third of workers say they withhold knowledge for fear of replacement (R-WEB-PEOPLE-30; vendor survey, third-hand). [inferred] The reuse payment is the platform's answer to that fear: a central library is the only design in which one firm's know-how earns its author something at another firm. Its size is open.

**Juniors.** Training is firm A's third-largest line at 2,300 model hours, and trainees learn on the lines handed over first (inventory A section 3). The route is rebuilt and funded as a cost on the firm's P&L: exams first; the task by hand on closed past files; then checking the agent against known answers, with each trainee's catch rate measured before they are relied on. [source says] Deloitte UK and KPMG UK describe such routes with no results (R-WEB-PEOPLE-15/16); half of 523 juniors did no better than the agent alone (R-WEB-PEOPLE-17). Intake is held. The centre runs one programme for all firms.

**Culture and belief (Q6).** Conviction is needed in three places: the sponsor, to fund the gap; the firm head, to grant authority; a named owner per workflow. Staff need a reason, a say and a guarantee, not belief (ledger Q6). The centre's domain leads teach in person, as Danaher's line executives do (R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-03). [source says] A working tool went unused despite the owner's instruction until months of in-person work (R-LIB-PEOPLE-15); a mandate with tracking produced fear at Proton (R-LIB-PEOPLE-14). Clients need to be told, not converted.

**Decision.** The charts above; group equity; reuse payments; the no-cuts rule; a funded junior route.
**Reason.** The improvement system needs people to write down what they know and to disagree with the agent. Pay must reward both.
**Evidence.** Failures show what not to do. The positive design has none.
**At one, five, thirty.** One: every workflow owner is known to the chief executive by name. Five: reuse payments start to be paid, which is the first proof the design is real. Thirty: a career path from reviewer to domain lead to divisional head.

## Part 8. Agents as part of the organisation, and the principles (Q3)

- **Where they sit.** An agent belongs to a workflow, not to a role or a department. It answers to one sponsor: the workflow owner at the firm. PlatformCo builds and hosts it. [source says] Sierra tried an agent per role and it failed (R-LIB-GOVERNANCE-17/18).
- **How many.** Default: a maintained workflow in ordinary code with AI steps, and one agent. Several agents only for parallel reading with a checkable result (R-LIB-GOVERNANCE-20/21; A001's conditions). Most of both inventories' "hand over now" lines need no agent at all.
- **Budget.** A monthly spend cap per workflow, set by group finance. At the cap the work falls back to the human queue and the sponsor is told. [source says] Caps per head run from $200 to $2,000 a month in engineering-heavy firms (R-WEB-STACK-31); token spend is spiky (R-WEB-STACK-32). No working money budget for an agent is on file (ledger Part 8, unknown), so this is untested.
- **Review and retirement.** A review before release. A quarterly review with the sponsor. Retired when its override rate or cost per unit fails for two reviews, when a plain rule will do, or when a model release makes it unnecessary. [source says] One operator takes six to eight weeks per agent to release (R-WEB-STACK-19).

### The principles, on one page

| # | Principle | Comes from |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | A named person answers for everything an agent does. An agent never signs | R-WEB-LAW-16/18; S9 |
| 2 | Every agent has one sponsor, its own identity, limits the system enforces, and an entry in the group register | R-WEB-GOVERNANCE-25/27/29; R-WEB-STACK-14 |
| 3 | Alone: reversible, internal acts inside its limits. With approval, each time: anything outward, irreversible, signed, or that moves money. Never: client money release, client acceptance, a report to a regulator, advice given as the firm's | R-WEB-LAW-17/19; R-LIB-GOVERNANCE-13; R-WEB-GOVERNANCE-26; S-B-05 |
| 4 | A check must be out of the maker's reach. A reviewer judges before reading the agent's reasoning, or is not the maker | R-WEB-SELFIMPROVE-24; R-WEB-GOVERNANCE-21; R-LIB-GOVERNANCE-19 |
| 5 | Sample routine work; approve each irreversible step; time every review | R-WEB-LAW-19; S-B-04 |
| 6 | We log what systems and agents do, and decisions made in writing. We do not record people. Everyone can see what is logged about their own work | R-WEB-LAW-01 to 04; R-WEB-MAPPING-29; R-LIB-GOVERNANCE-32 |
| 7 | Client material stays in closed tools, in that client's space, and is never used to train a model or carried into a shared playbook | R-WEB-LAW-12/13/14/15 |
| 8 | Anyone may propose a change. The owner accepts a local change. The release gate accepts a shared one. Whatever proposes cannot touch the tests | R-WEB-SELFIMPROVE-02; R-WEB-STACK-15; R-LIB-GOVERNANCE-23 |
| 9 | Playbooks are written by the people who do the work | R-WEB-SELFIMPROVE-26 |
| 10 | When a person and an agent disagree, the person's decision stands, the disagreement is logged and classed, and it is audited later. Being right against the agent is rewarded | No case; nearest is R-LIB-GOVERNANCE-13 |
| 11 | We measure outcomes: errors reaching clients, review time, the same correction recurring, capacity sold. We never measure or pay for usage | R-LIB-FAILURES-04/05; R-WEB-SELFIMPROVE-17 |
| 12 | Every change can be undone, and the undo has been rehearsed | R-WEB-SELFIMPROVE-19; R-LIB-IMPROVE-TECH-09 |
| 13 | A client may ask for their work to be done without AI, and we can still do it | S-B-04 |

**The two open disputes.**

*Approval at each step against limits and budgets.* Neither, as a rule for all work. Sort acts by consequence, reversibility and how soon the result can be checked: step approval for the outward, irreversible and signed; limits, budgets and sampled review for the rest. [source says] Sampling is accepted for routine delegated work, in a case about people (R-WEB-LAW-19), and required as dip samples by the RICS standard (S-B-04). The one authorised AI law firm takes no step without approval (R-WEB-LAW-17). The tax bodies' guidance reads as review of each tax deliverable (S9), and insurers may ask for review of all outputs (R-WEB-LAW-25). So every filed return at firm A is reviewed by a person until an insurer and the institute say otherwise. No case compares the two rules on the same work (R-LIB-GOVERNANCE-15).

*Record everything against its costs.* Not everything. Capture as a by-product of work, for stated purposes, with decisions written down. [source says] UK monitoring must be announced, purposeful and the least intrusive means; consent is not a sound basis (R-WEB-LAW-01 to 04). Imposed capture at Meta drew resistance and a data exposure (R-WEB-MAPPING-29). Records people must enter die (R-LIB-GOVERNANCE-31). An employee who refuses to be recorded has nothing to refuse: there is no screen or keystroke capture, and system logs are announced and assessed first. [inferred] The cost: the platform learns nothing from calls, which is where firm B's state lives.

**Decision.** Agents belong to workflows, one sponsor each, one central register; the thirteen principles.
**Reason.** Regulators, a standards body and unrelated operators converge on it.
**Evidence.** Convergent practice; no outcome evidence (ledger Part 8).
**At one, five, thirty.** One: a register of perhaps a dozen entries. Five: the same agents run at each firm with firm-level limits; the register is how the centre knows what is running. Thirty: register and release gate are a full-time function; [inferred] this is where an unmapped shared endpoint (R-LIB-GOVERNANCE-22) would be found or missed.

## Part 9. The management system

Platform stance: a fixed system, the same at every firm, on the Danaher model and not the Berkshire one. [source says] Danaher cut its measures from 150 to eight and holds a monthly eight-hour review (R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-02/04). Agents do not reduce management time: at the one firm that measured it, meeting time rose 20% (R-WEB-GOVERNANCE-15/16; the firm also tripled in size). Attention is the budget.

**The eight measures, per firm and per case.**

1. Client fee retention.
2. Errors reaching a client, and complaints.
3. Review time per unit, by reviewer.
4. Override rate: how often a person changes the agent's output.
5. Recurrence: how often the same class of correction comes back.
6. Capacity to P&L: hours freed, and what became of each (sold, an avoided hire, a role not replaced, or nothing).
7. Regretted leavers.
8. Cash: lock-up, and platform and token cost per unit of work.

| Rhythm | Routine | Who | Agents' part |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Weekly | Firm flash: P&L, cash, the eight measures, open exceptions older than a set age. One page. Thirty minutes | Firm head, workflow owners | Prepare the page; flag what moved |
| Weekly | Release gate: shared changes proposed, tested, approved or refused | Platform lead, domain lead, risk lead | Propose changes; run regression sets |
| Weekly | Each workflow owner reads a sample of their case's exceptions | Workflow owner | Class the exceptions |
| Monthly | Close, on one chart of accounts | Group finance | Prepare; a person signs |
| Monthly | Operating review per firm, half a day, at the firm, on the eight measures and one problem worked through | Chief executive or divisional head, firm head, owners | Supply data; nothing else |
| Monthly | Register review: agents added, changed, retired; spend against caps | Risk lead, sponsors | |
| Quarterly | Firm board: client acceptance, complaints, sign-off standards, insurer and regulator matters | Firm board | |
| Quarterly | Review of the improvement system itself (Part 10) and of local variants | Platform lead, domain leads | |
| Quarterly | Model-release re-test of the failed-uses register | Platform team | Run the re-tests |
| Yearly | Plan and budget; platform unit prices; the deal review a year after each purchase | HoldCo board | |

**Escalation.** An exception goes to the workflow owner. Anything touching a client goes to the signatory the same day. An error that reached a client goes to the firm head and the risk lead within a day, and to the insurer where the policy requires. Any signatory can stop an agent on their work at once; the stop is reviewed at the next release gate, not before.

**Decision.** Eight measures, one calendar, the same everywhere.
**Reason.** A centre cannot compare firms, or reuse a fix, without common measures.
**Evidence.** Danaher's own account; one measured case on attention. Nothing from inside an AI roll-up (ledger Part 9, unknown). Constellation and Berkshire lasted without any of this, which is evidence against.
**At one, five, thirty.** One: the chief executive attends everything. Five: the monthly review is the chief executive's main job; comparison between firms starts to teach. Thirty: divisional heads run the monthly reviews.

## Part 10. The improvement system (Q4)

Ben's point is that maps and one-off projects are static. [source says] Fair for what is evidenced; the vendors' "living model" claims show no owner, no test and no correction changing a playbook (ledger section 3). His phrase "via AI" overstates what happens today: in every production account a machine may propose and a named person approves (R-WEB-SELFIMPROVE-01/02/03/06/07/09/12).

| | 1. Models get better | 2. The firm's playbooks, checks and tools get better | 3. The process gets better |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Who else gets it | Every rival | Nobody, unless they copy it | Nobody |
| What it needs | A register of failed and gated uses; a re-test at each release; the ability to switch model | Classed corrections; a regression set from real cases; a named approver; staged release; rehearsed rollback | Measures of the loop itself; an incident review |
| Who proposes | The platform team | Workflow owners, reviewers, and an agent reading traces | The domain leads and the risk lead |
| What tests it | The same regression sets | The regression set, then a shadow run, then one firm | A quarterly review |
| Who approves | Platform lead | The owner for a client-specific rule; the release gate for anything shared | PlatformCo operating committee |
| How it is undone | Pin the previous model | Roll back the playbook version | Reverse the rule change |

**What the centre adds.** A correction at one firm improves the playbook at every firm. The regression set grows with each firm's real traffic. Model re-testing is done once. [inferred] This is the strongest argument for the stance: the scarce inputs to loop 2 (test sets, rubrics, reviewers) pool, and a single firm of 30 cannot staff loop 1 or loop 3 at all.

**How you would know it works, other than by counting changes.** The same correction stops recurring (R-WEB-SELFIMPROVE-17). Review time per unit falls while errors reaching clients do not rise. Handling minutes and escalations per case fall against the day-30 baseline (R-LIB-IMPROVE-TECH-12). Changes proposed by firm staff, not only by engineers, keep coming. The latest firm reaches three live cases faster than the first.

**What to expect.** Modest compounding. [source says] About one point in a month at Intercom, with about 60% of machine proposals approved (R-WEB-SELFIMPROVE-03); it could not cleanly test its own loop (R-WEB-SELFIMPROVE-04). The strongest advocate says "I'm not sure anyone has a truly self improving company in every function" (R-LIB-IMPROVE-TECH-03).

**How often such systems last.** [source says] Danaher's has run since 1986 (R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-01). No sound failure rate exists for lean programmes; the "70%" and "90%" figures have no source; one pooled estimate for quality programmes is 37.7% (R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-34; read through a practitioner's review). Where they failed, the named causes are momentum lost after the analysis (R-WEB-MAPPING-23) and no standing team (R-WEB-MAPPING-22). Danaher's is carried by line executives who teach it and are rated on it (R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-03). All AI-era loops are under three years old.

**"Full context."** It would need every input, decision and correction in a readable form. Law and practice do not allow it: no blanket monitoring, no client material in shared learning, and much of the knowledge is in calls (101 calendar entries against 11 recorded notes in one build, R-LIB-IMPROVE-TECH-25). Where the firm cannot record, it writes the decision down afterwards, with its reason, and the owner adds the rule to the playbook by hand.

**Decision.** A gated loop per case, with the gate, the regression sets and the model re-test at the centre.
**Reason.** It is what exists in production, and its scarce parts pool.
**Evidence.** Fair, almost all from software firms. No measured loop at a firm of either reference type; reviewer hours per approved change unknown.
**At one, five, thirty.** One: the loop is five people in a room each week. Five: cross-firm corrections arrive; the first disagreement about a shared default. Thirty: the release gate is the group's main control and its main bottleneck.

## Part 12. Back office and shared services (Q7)

- **Central from month one:** group finance and reporting, payroll, IT and security, data protection, insurance buying, deal integration, training. **Stays in the firm:** client-facing administration, complaints, the reporting officer, the firm's own regulatory returns.
- **Rented, not built.** [source says] A finance close with audit log is rented by "more than 1,000 businesses" (R-WEB-STACK-23); "every buyer can automate invoicing" (R-LIB-ECONOMICS-11). Chroma's objection is right: this is no advantage.

Both inventories put back-office lines first (firm A: L2, B5, E4, M5; firm B: J18, J2). [source says] Small firms on one vendor's AI bookkeeping closed the month 7.5 days faster (R-WEB-STACK-27; pre-agent tools).
- **Charged at cost by headcount,** so its size is disciplined (R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-10).

**Decision.** Central, rented, charged at cost; the proving ground and not the prize.

**Evidence.** Fair for finance. Nothing with a method for HR, compliance or IT (ledger Part 12, unknown). The lasting decentralised groups did not consolidate the back office at all (R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-07/15), which is evidence against.
**At one, five, thirty.** One: the firm's practice manager plus rented tools and a group finance lead. Five: one finance team closes five firms on one chart. Thirty: a shared-service centre, billed and benchmarked.

**The risk this stance creates.** A shared playbook with a fault is wrong at every firm on the same day. A decentralised group cannot have that failure. **Evidence.** Strong on rules. Nothing on insurers' terms or price. Sampling against "all outputs" is unresolved (R-WEB-LAW-19 against R-WEB-LAW-25; S9).
**At one, five, thirty.** One: one policy, one conversation with one insurer. Five: a group insurance programme, and an insurer who has seen the release gate. Thirty: correlated error is the board's first risk; regulators treat the group as a group.

| Stage | The organisation |

| Thirty firms | A divisional layer. A second vertical, perhaps. The platform licensed to firms the group does not own, perhaps, as Dwelly plans (R-WEB-ROLLUPS-12) |

**What is carried from firm to firm:** the catalogue, the test, the checks, the permission model, the management system, the launch playbook, the engagement clauses. [source says] One founder estimates 80% of infrastructure carries (R-LIB-STRUCTURE-15; his estimate). **What is rebuilt each time:** client-specific rules, data clean-up, adoption (R-LIB-STRUCTURE-03).

**As models improve.** Expect to rebuild scaffolding each generation. [source says] "Every model that comes out makes the things we added for the last one pointless" (R-LIB-PEOPLE-23). Capture of a single task is now a built-in feature of the vendors' tools (R-WEB-MAPPING-25). What survives a model change is the test sets, the exception classes, the limits and the habit of release. Those are what the centre owns.

**Q5: does the work become a commodity, and what is left?** [source says] Where the client can see the cost fall and do the work, price or volume fell (R-WEB-COMMERCIAL-05/07/12). Regulated advice has not moved yet (R-WEB-COMMERCIAL-13/14). Both reference firms are thinly protected: firm A's reserved signature is audit only, and firm B has no statutory licence (inventory A section 3; inventory B 3.4). So I take the hard answer seriously: for these two firms, little durable is left beyond a few years' head start.

The strategy if that is true:

1. Buy at prices that work with no gain.
2. Take the window as capacity and volume, not price, while fixed fees hold it.
3. Give part of it back at renewal before clients ask.
4. Use the window to become the lowest-cost holder of client relationships and accountable signatures in the vertical. Those are what a group can own (R-LIB-ECONOMICS-33; an analyst's inference).
5. Treat the platform as a wasting asset that must be rebuilt, and say so to investors.

[inferred] A platform does not make a moat. It makes the group quicker to pass each model generation through to every firm. Whether that speed is worth a centre is the bet.

**Decision.** A window, not a moat; a platform designed to be rebuilt; a layer change near thirty firms.
**Reason.** Rentability, and both firms' thin protection.
**Evidence.** Filed price falls in comparable work; forecasts for the rest. Nothing on how long a loop lasts.
**At one, five, thirty.** Covered in the table.

## 16. The design applied to the two reference firms: what changes in year one

### Firm A (30 people, accountancy)

- **Structure.** Three partners sell. They take cash, group shares and a deferred payment on retention and milestones. They become firm head and signatories on salary. The audit book (lines J1 to J5, about 2,200 model hours) leaves the group with the responsible individual, or is sold.
- **Scored.** 8% of hours pass the test today, mostly software already in place. 52% sit behind a check that must be designed. The binding item on the big lines is item 4 (inventory A section 3). The platform's first build for this vertical is therefore checks: tie-outs, recomputation, blind-first sampled review.
- **First: back office.** L2, B5, E4, M5 (about 850 model hours). Proves permissions, logging and the sponsor role.
- **Three cases.** (1) The records chase across D1, F1, A5 (about 2,000 hours): written contact limits, opt-outs, a person takes any reply that is not a document. It attacks the client's delay, which sets the calendar. (2) Bookkeeping to VAT: A2, A1, B1 (about 5,500 hours). Per-client rules written by the bookkeepers. VAT filed only on client approval, through the software interface. (3) The year-end case in shadow for one cycle: D2 with D6 and E1, with automated tie-outs and the manager's review timed.
- **Left alone.** Anything inside HMRC's web pages or by phone (B2, C7, F6; about 1,300 hours; S10). Sign-off (D7, E3). Advice (I3, I5). Meetings (H3, N8).
- **People.** Four managers become workflow owners. Bookkeepers become exception handlers for the bookkeeping case and authors of its client rules, with reuse payments when that playbook goes to the next firm. Trainees keep doing D2 and F2 by hand on closed files and start measured checking. Nobody is made redundant.
- **Review.** Every tax deliverable is still reviewed by a person (S9). Review time is measured from day 30. [inferred] If preparation speeds up and review does not, the queue moves to the managers (inventory A, order 5 to 9). That will be visible in measure 3 within a quarter.
- **Clients.** Told of the sale on day one. AI clause at each renewal. Fees held. Freed capacity is offered first as the new quarterly-reporting service (F4).
- **What will not have happened by month 12.** A margin gain. A full statutory cycle on the new cases. Any evidence about reuse.

### Firm B (50 people, block management and lettings)

- **Structure.** One firm entity. No licence to protect. Client money stays under a principal with dual authorisation (lines A17, A18 never touched).
- **Diligence turns on one question.** Do the block and lettings systems accept writes from outside? (inventory B section 1.) If the vendor refuses, most item-3 scores fall to N. Under this stance that would mean migrating before building, or not buying.
- **Scored.** 6% of hours pass today; 49% sit behind a designed check. Binding items: readable state, the verifier, authority (inventory B 3.1). "It needs about four things built once": a completion and invoice check, written limits per client, a write path, a way to pull state from inboxes.
- **First: back office.** J18 post and email routing; J2 office accounts, rented.
- **Three cases.** (1) The repair, from report to paid invoice: B1, B2, B4, I13, I14, A6 (about 11,000 model hours). One verifier (photo or resident confirmation before close; no invoice paid on an unclosed job), one exception taxonomy, spend limits per client taken from the agreements. Payment release stays with people. (2) The money month: A4, A5 prepared (about 2,200 hours); the principal reviews, as the rule requires (S-B-05). (3) One fixed-procedure product: sales packs, G5 (700 hours), priced per pack, checked against the ledger and works register.
- **Left alone.** Site visits, viewings, emergencies (8,300 hours). Boards and landlords (5,600 hours). Section 20 notices stay under step approval because a defective consultation cannot be undone.
- **Rules.** If the firm is RICS-regulated: a register of AI systems, a quarterly risk register, dip samples, written notice to clients in advance, and a stated opt-out (S-B-04). The group register and release gate supply the first three. A board may opt its block out, so the old way must still run.
- **People.** Property managers and assistants write the repair playbook and the per-client limits. Managers are the scarce resource, so the gain is blocks per manager, measured, with no target set until the baseline exists. The inventory's 640 units per manager is a guess and no benchmark was found.
- **2027.** Eight lines change on 1 April and 1 November 2027 (S-B-08). The catalogue is amended once at the centre. [inferred] This is a small, real example of what a centre is for.
- **Where the platform fits worse than at A.** Less of the work is reachable, state lives in calls and site knowledge, and lettings may be demand-limited. The centre's reusable part is the repair case and the money month; the rest is local.

## 17. Fit: do the parts belong together?

| Test | How this design meets it | Where it strains |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Do incentives (Part 7) reward what the improvement system (Part 10) needs? | The loop needs written know-how, honest corrections and people who disagree with the agent. Authors are named, credited and paid on reuse. Reviewers are rewarded for disagreements that prove right. Nobody is paid on usage or on counts. The no-cuts rule removes the reason to withhold. Group equity makes a fix at another firm worth something to its author | The reuse payment has no precedent and no size. If small, it is a gesture. If large, it invites dispute about authorship |
| Do seller terms fit the redesign? | Deferred pay is on retention and milestones, not on profit, so sellers are not paid to protect hours | Sellers may prefer a profit earn-out they understand. Price may have to rise to compensate |
| Do decision rights (Part 3) match liability (Part 13)? | The signatory signs, accepts clients and can stop any agent on their work. The centre cannot overrule that except by replacing the firm head. The centre controls what it is liable for: the platform, the register, shared releases | A signatory is liable for work made by a playbook they did not write and cannot fully inspect. The staged release and the stop right are the answer. An insurer has not been asked whether it is enough |
| Does the management system (Part 9) measure what the incentives pay on? | The eight measures are the same measures that pay owners, firm heads and the centre | Measure 6 (capacity to P&L) depends on honest time data, which fixed-fee firms do not keep |
| Do the economics (Part 14) pay for the centre drawn in Part 7? | Not at one firm, and I say so. The centre is paid by unit charges that should fall per unit as firms are added. The design carries a stop rule at firm three | The whole case rests on the conversion cost falling. If it does not, the centre in Part 7 is too big at every scale |
| Does the commercial line (Part 5) fit the people promise (Part 7)? | No cuts is only affordable if capacity is sold. The growth lead and measure 6 exist for that | In a demand-limited firm (B's lettings side) the two promises conflict |
| Does disclosure (Part 5) fit the loop (Part 10)? | Clients are told; client material stays out of shared learning; opt-out is possible | Opt-outs and separation reduce what the loop can learn. The pooled test set is method and structure, not client data, and that may make it less valuable than I claim |

## 18. Where this stance is weakest

**The weakest premise.** That converting the fifth firm costs much less than the first. [source says] No services group has published that number; the only figure is a semiconductor customer's second team (R-WEB-STACK-05). Against it: each firm's process is "bespoke" even where the noun is shared (R-LIB-IMPROVE-TECH-36); mapping, data clean-up and adoption are rebuilt each time (R-LIB-STRUCTURE-03); adoption took months in person whatever the tool (R-LIB-PEOPLE-15). [inferred] If the firm-specific parts are most of the cost, a platform saves the cheap part and the centre is overhead. The "80% carries" figure is one founder's estimate about infrastructure (R-LIB-STRUCTURE-15).

**Other weak points, in order.**

1. **The precedents that lasted longest were decentralised.** Constellation, Berkshire and Halma ran lean centres that "can't order" (R-WEB-GOVERNANCE-01/10/11). My answer, that those firms did not need to change, is an argument and not evidence. The one mandated precedent, Danaher, is manufacturing-rooted and self-described.
2. **Correlated failure.** One bad release is wrong everywhere (Part 13).
3. **Migration.** Moving a firm's system of record in a deadline trade is how clients and staff are lost. The evidence is split (Dwelly against Shield) and thin.
4. **The centre's cost in the transition years,** with no known break-even number of firms.
5. **A strong centre can crowd out local judgement.** [source says] Central targets reached clinicians in vet groups (R-WEB-OWNERSHIP-28); Knights' staff churn rose to 15% (R-WEB-OWNERSHIP-30).
6. **Everything good said about the Long Lake position is said by Long Lake and its investors** (ledger section 1, shared origins).

**What would make me abandon it.**

- The third firm costs as much in engineer-weeks and practitioner-weeks as the first.
- Fewer than half the first firm's playbooks and checks pass the release gate at the second without being rewritten. (The threshold is my choice; set it before the second deal.)
- Client fee retention or regretted leavers at a migrated firm are clearly worse than at an overlaid one.
- An insurer or professional body requires firm-by-firm certification of every playbook, which removes the reuse.
- Model vendors ship the checks and permission model as standard features, so that a single firm can rent what the centre builds. [source says] Much of the machinery is already rentable (R-WEB-SELFIMPROVE-13/35; R-WEB-MAPPING-25).
- The system-of-record vendors refuse the write path at group level.

**Which type of business it suits worst.** High-touch, bespoke advice sold on a person's judgement and billed by the hour to clients who can compare and insource: little repeats, the saving passes to the client, and partners can leave with clients. Next worst: work that is mostly physical presence and local relationship, where a platform reaches a minority of hours. Firm B's lettings side leans that way. And any vertical where the group can only buy a handful of firms, because a platform with five users is a cost.

**It suits best:** statutory, recurring, fixed-fee work that is alike from firm to firm, with open filing interfaces, a capacity shortage and many small sellers. That is firm A's compliance core, which is also the most crowded market on file (R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-26). I have not resolved that tension.
