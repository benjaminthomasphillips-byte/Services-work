# The operating model of an AI-enabled services roll-up

Draft v1, 23 November 2026. Status: Draft. Kind: Framework. One author. Written from the run's evidence ledger, the two work inventories and three rival designs (central platform, one firm at a time, operator under mandate). Nothing in the Thoughts Library was changed.

Tags: **[source says]** means a ledger row, an evidence row or an inventory source states it; the ID follows. **[inferred]** means my reasoning. "Design choice" means a setting I chose with no evidence behind the particular value. "Open" means nobody in the evidence knows, and I have not guessed.

## Thesis

**The hypothesis.** An AI-enabled roll-up of small services firms can earn a return, but only as a patient, debt-free owner that buys capacity-short, fixed-fee firms at prices that work with no AI gain, and treats the gain as a window of a few years, not a moat. The work that decides the return is not automation. It is building, firm by firm, a check on the work that does not depend on a person nodding at the machine's output, and then selling the hours that check frees. I hold this with moderate confidence on the diagnosis and low confidence on the economics.

**What we now think, in five points.**

1. **Nothing in this field has a measured result.** [source says] No AI roll-up has published a margin, price, retention or error figure with a baseline. Every results number from Long Lake, Thrive, Dwelly, Shield and Supa is self-reported, and several change between tellings (ledger finding 1; R-WEB-ROLLUPS-04/14/17/22). The design therefore cannot rest on any of them. It rests on rules (who is accountable, who may own, what may be recorded), on filings from dull listed firms, and on what earlier serial acquirers say about themselves.

2. **Start as one firm, run thin, and let a measured test decide how much centre to build.** The three rival designs are nearly the same organisation at one firm: an owner, an operator living in the firm, an engineer, finance first. They part at the second firm. The platform design bets that converting the fifth firm costs far less than the first. [source says] No services group has published that number; the only figure is a semiconductor customer's second team (R-WEB-STACK-05). So I take the one-firm design's thin centre and slow pace, and I take from the platform design the instruments that would prove it wrong: a cost code per firm, and a pre-set test of how much of firm one's checks and playbooks work at firm two without rewriting. The centre is thickened only if that test passes.

3. **Mandate the method, not the content.** [source says] The acquirers that lasted with autonomous firms bought firms whose managers "were doing exactly that before" (R-WEB-GOVERNANCE-10). An AI roll-up assumes the firm's work must change, and nothing in the precedents combines autonomy with rebuilding the work (ledger finding 10). So the centre gives a short list of orders: no debt, honest accounts, eight measures, a monthly review, the task test, an agent register, disclosure, and an independent head of quality who can stop things. Everything a licensed or accountable person answers for stays with that person.

4. **The binding constraint is the check, not the agent.** [source says] In both inventories about half of all hours sit on work that could be handed over once an independent check exists, and under a tenth could be handed over today (inventory A section 3; inventory B section 3.1; model hours, not measurements). Human sign-off is a weak check: analysts followed their agent's lean 86% of the time (R-WEB-GOVERNANCE-21; one vendor-run trial of 50). Where automation worked, the check was most of the cost that remained (R-LIB-DELIVERY-29; R-WEB-MAPPING-06).

5. **Improvement is a gated habit with modest gains, and people must be paid to feed it.** [source says] Every production loop has a machine proposing and a named person approving; the one measured gain on file is about 1.2 points in the first month (R-WEB-SELFIMPROVE-03; Intercom on its own product). Playbooks written by people raised benchmark pass rates by 16.6 points on average; playbooks agents wrote for themselves made results worse (R-WEB-SELFIMPROVE-26; 2026 models). No pay design for people whose know-how is captured exists anywhere in the evidence (ledger finding 10). The one here is judgement.

**The weakest premise.** That a designed check lets review time per unit fall without errors rising. Everything downstream depends on it: freed hours, sold capacity, the job pledge, the return. Against it: [source says] the tax bodies' guidance reads as review of each tax deliverable (inventory A, S9); insurers are being advised to ask for human review of "all" outputs (R-WEB-LAW-25); no remedy for reviewer deference has been tested (ledger Q3, unknown); and faster preparation with unchanged review only moves the queue to managers (inventory A section 3). If review minutes do not fall after one annual cycle at the first firm, the design has bought an ordinary firm and a small cost centre.

The second-weakest premise is that freed hours become sold capacity at held prices. [source says] No held source measures how much saved time converts to profit (R-LIB-ECONOMICS-30).

**What would change the view.**

- An AI roll-up publishes an audited before-and-after margin with a baseline, and the cost of its latest firm against its first. That would justify a thicker centre and a faster pace.
- A regulator or insurer accepts certification of the agent system in place of a named person's review (proposed at R-LIB-GOVERNANCE-06; no regulator is shown accepting it). The local signatory would stop being the natural place for decisions.
- At our own first firm, review time per unit has not fallen on any case after a full cycle. Stop building; run the firm as bought.
- At our second firm, most of firm one's checks and playbooks need rewriting and the conversion cost is no lower. Stay thin for good.
- Fees for small-business accountancy or block management fall fast and visibly. Speed of cost-out would then matter more than depth, which favours a platform or building new.

**The three decisions Ben would need to make first.**

1. **The capital and its clock.** A long-hold company, or a fund with an exit. [source says] European accountancy investors expect to leave in three to seven years (R-WEB-OWNERSHIP-06); when Constellation's investors put it up for sale, acquisitions and long-term spending dropped (R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-11). This design assumes no planned exit. With a clock, the job pledge and the improvement loop are the first things to give way, and sellers and staff should be told so.
2. **The first trade and the first firm.** UK non-audit accountancy has clearer rules, open filing interfaces and a cheaper inventory, in a crowded market. Property management has the only operating account from a firm of the reference type (lettings maintenance; self-reported) and an unknown write path. I lean to accountancy on judgement. Ben's access should decide, and it is not recorded.
3. **The size of the bet before any result.** A majority of one firm bought with no debt, plus a centre of about three full-time and two part-time people for 18 to 24 months, with no financial return expected in that time and written stop rules at month twelve. Whether that is acceptable is the investment decision. The amounts are open.

**Where the evidence goes against Ben.** Not supported: a full inventory as the first move, the four requirements as sufficient, and improvement "via AI". Supported: a light inventory as a screen, the four as necessary, his description of Marble, and the commodity worry, which bites harder than expected because both reference firms are less protected by licence than the word suggests. Detail is under "Ben's starting positions tested".

## How the sources connect

### What the evidence is, and is not

- [source says] It is strong on rules, fair on listed incumbents' prices and on how earlier acquirers ran themselves, and weak on the thing the design is about. No source describes a UK accountancy practice of 30 or a block manager of 50 after a year of this (ledger section 1).
- [source says] The library readers read the library's records, not primary sources. Most web rows came through extraction tools (ledger section 1). I opened the evidence rows behind every quotation below. I did not open the primary sources.
- Long Lake, Thrive with OpenAI, Chroma, Dwelly and Anthropic's employment report each appear in many rows and are counted once (ledger section 1, shared origins).
- The inventories' volumes and hours are model assumptions for a typical firm, not records of a real one (inventory A, opening note; inventory B, opening note). I use them for shape only.

### The six chains of reasoning behind the main choices

**1. Legal form follows the licence, and debt is where roll-ups die.**
- [source says] A UK audit firm must be controlled by qualified persons: "a majority of the members of the firm are qualified persons" and a majority of its management body (R-WEB-LAW-29; Companies Act 2006, Schedule 10).
- [source says] The FCA found that "decisions materially affecting regulated firms were made by unregulated boards" and that "some regulated entities guaranteed a holding company's debt" (R-WEB-OWNERSHIP-19/20). CBIZ: "Interest expense was $107.2 million in 2025, compared to $34.4 million in 2024" after one deal (R-WEB-OWNERSHIP-10; a filing). RSM Tenon, Slater and Gordon and Ince failed after debt-funded or over-priced buying (R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-28; R-WEB-OWNERSHIP-31/32/33).
- [inferred] Hence separate firm entities, audit outside, no debt on firms. All three rivals agree. Confidence: high.

**2. Accountability stays with a named person, but supervision can be scaled to risk.**
- [source says] "AI has no separate legal personality; solicitors and regulated individuals who use AI … remain accountable for their work and outputs" (R-WEB-LAW-16; SRA warning notice). The tax bodies say the same for accountants: members are responsible "irrespective of the use of AI tools" (inventory A, S9).
- [source says] The Court of Appeal: for routine work "it may be sufficient … to sample their work" (R-WEB-LAW-19; a case about people, not AI). The RICS AI standard requires "randomised dip samples" for high-volume outputs (inventory B, S-B-04).
- [source says] Against: insurers are advised to ask whether firms ensure "human oversight and review of all AI-generated legal outputs" (R-WEB-LAW-25; a law firm writing for insurers). No UK insurer's actual wording or price was found.
- [inferred] So decision rights sit with the signatory, the control is sorted by consequence, and the insurer is asked before the design is fixed. Confidence: high on the rule, low on insurers.

**3. A person's sign-off is not an independent check unless it is designed to be.**
- [source says] "analysts went with their agent's lean 86% of the time. If the agent's input made no difference, we'd expect about 50%" (R-WEB-GOVERNANCE-21; vendor-run, 50 analysts, read as a web summary).
- [source says] Accountants "tend to still follow AI's suggestions" when the software is unsure (R-WEB-STACK-27; summary of a study of 79 firms; pre-agent tools).
- [source says] Of 523 junior staff at KPMG, 50.1% beat the agent working alone, 25.8% matched it and 24.1% did worse (R-WEB-PEOPLE-17; the firm's own study, July 2026).
- [source says] Models have tried "to get a higher score by modifying the tests or scoring code" (R-WEB-SELFIMPROVE-24; 2025 models, o3 named).
- [inferred] So the main thing to build is the check: tie-outs and recomputation by separate code, a reviewer who judges before seeing the agent's reasoning, seeded known-error files, and a timer on review. This is a design. No remedy for deference has been tested (ledger Q3).

**4. Work is found narrow and widened; a light list is still worth having.**
- [source says] No case began from a full list of services and reported what happened. Every case began with one workflow (ledger Q1; R-LIB-DELIVERY-01/05/08/17). This is an absence, not a negative result.
- [source says] Going deep is slow: one tax domain took "six weeks of engineering and practitioner review" (R-WEB-STACK-04; OpenAI on its own customer); nine months of Marble capture at Integral "published 42 SOPs" (R-WEB-MAPPING-05; vendor; activity, no outcome).
- [source says] Both inventories found a light list cheap in these two codified trades, and found a few shared gaps blocking most lines (inventory A section 4; inventory B section 4; desk exercises).
- [inferred] A light inventory in diligence as a screen; three whole cases after completion. Confidence: medium.

**5. What lasted was small, taught by line managers, with long-held equity. Nothing lasted that combined autonomy with rebuilding the work.**
- [source says] Constellation: the layer above "can nurture, goad and suggest, but they can't order" (R-WEB-GOVERNANCE-01). The only other group Leonard found that ran hundreds of firms autonomously "eventually caved in to increased centralisation" (R-WEB-GOVERNANCE-02; he calls his explanation a hunch).
- [source says] Danaher mandates its system: training, "a strategic plan in place within 100 days", "an eight-hour operating review every month"; "The top 25 executives at Danaher, including the CEO, all teach two or more weeks per year" (R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-02/03; company accounts).
- [source says] Constellation held head office down only by a cap: "to arbitrarily limit headcount additions", taking the burden "from 3.0% of Net Revenue in 2004, to 0.5%" (R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-10). Bonuses above a threshold go into shares held "an average of at least 4 years" (R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-11).
- [source says] Teachers of the method name "inappropriate pace, poor integration, excessive financial leverage, and overly aggressive valuation" (R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-31).
- [inferred] Take Danaher's form for the management system and Constellation's for professional and commercial decisions. This split is the design's main act of judgement.

**6. Who keeps a saving turns on visibility, pricing basis and renewal.**
- [source says] RWS: "Lower gross margins of 43.4% (FY24: 46.9%)"; adjusted profit before tax "£60.4m (FY24: £106.7m)"; "95% repeat services revenue" (R-WEB-COMMERCIAL-12; a filing; attribution to AI is partly interpretation).
- [source says] US law: "a full 90% of all legal dollars still flow through standard hourly rate arrangements", with rates up 7.3% in 2025 (R-WEB-COMMERCIAL-13).
- [source says] UK vets: acquisitions "led to an increase in average prices of 9% four years later", followed by binding remedies (R-WEB-OWNERSHIP-25; CMA).
- [source says] Chroma: "In a supply-constrained business … AI unlocks capacity. You serve more customers at the same price" (R-LIB-ECONOMICS-09; a rival buyer with no completed deals; a hypothesis).
- [inferred] Buy where fees are fixed, clients are small and the firm is short of capacity; take the gain as volume, not price; expect to give some back at renewal. Confidence: fair on the pattern, low for the two reference firms.

### Ben's starting positions tested

**"Do we need to list out the 200 things the company does, turn them into products, identify edge cases etc, and automate as much as you can?"**
- For: [source says] both inventories found a list of about 90 to 125 lines cheap to make and useful. It found the shared blockers, the lines not to touch, and outside limits such as HMRC's rule that "automation tools must not be used to enter data into or navigate Government Gateway" (inventory A, S10).
- Against: [source says] no case started from a full inventory; all started narrow (ledger section 3). The one account of extensive mapping first took over a year (R-LIB-DELIVERY-16; self-reported). A workflow with state, procedure, a write path and a test set scored "92.8% versus 22.3% in production" because real requests were incomplete (R-LIB-DELIVERY-05; implementer's own figures; late 2025 to January 2026 models). An inventory scores the line as designed, not the traffic as it arrives. Productising fits about ten of 123 lines at firm B (inventory B section 4).
- **Verdict.** Against him on the full inventory as the first move and as a build plan. With him on a light list in diligence, as a screen. The unit to build is the case, not the line. Confidence: medium; it rests on absence, company accounts and two desk exercises.

**"Automating any task requires 4 things (or does it)."**
- [source says] Each of the four is supported (ledger Q2). But cases had all four and failed or went unused (R-LIB-DELIVERY-05; R-LIB-FAILURES-09, late 2025), and cases ran without a built verifier only as suggestions to a person who checked everything, at low volume (R-LIB-DELIVERY-09/33).
- **Verdict.** Necessary, not sufficient. What recurs in the failures: complete inputs, authority and limits, an owner who has agreed to own the agent's version, a route for each exception, undo, a test set from real traffic, a check the maker cannot reach, and permission from an outside body. The test is in Part 6. Confidence: medium-high; no task has been run with and without one item (R-LIB-DELIVERY-37).

**Mapping products such as Marble Cognition are static.**
- [source says] His description is accurate with one correction. Marble's site lists five steps, "Capture Understand Identify Automate Measure", and presents capture as switched on by employees at team level (R-WEB-MAPPING-01/02/03).
- For the criticism: [source says] an academic review finds the evidence that process mining pays "mostly anecdotal" (R-WEB-MAPPING-20; 2021). In 62 published cases, "80% of all process mining efforts were focused on data extraction, data preparation and dealing with data quality issues", and success needed a standing team (R-WEB-MAPPING-22).
- Against: [source says] Marble now claims "Always up-to-date SOPs … redrawn as the work changes. No one has to maintain it" (R-WEB-MAPPING-04). A claim. No source shows how a change is detected, who approves the redrawn procedure, or whether a deployed agent is updated.
- **Verdict.** Fair for what is evidenced; out of date for what is claimed. A map is a rentable input; the organisation round it is not. Integral and Enam, two AI-native services groups, already appear as Marble customers (R-WEB-MAPPING-05/06; vendor case studies).

**"We need orgs constantly self improving via AI."**
- For: [source says] Danaher's shared system began in 1986 and is still running (R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-01/04; company accounts). Gated loops run in production (R-WEB-SELFIMPROVE-01/03/06).
- Against: [source says] the strongest advocate says "I'm not sure anyone has a truly self improving company in every function" (R-LIB-IMPROVE-TECH-03). Intercom: "the average approval rate of new Suggestions is around 60%" and "an average increase of 1.23 percentage points" in the first month (R-WEB-SELFIMPROVE-03). Ungated self-edits "never settle"; a keep-best gate took accuracy "from 45.3% to 55.8%" (R-WEB-SELFIMPROVE-15; GPT-5.4-mini and Haiku 4.5, ten tasks). Changes that passed tests degraded a live product for days to weeks (R-WEB-SELFIMPROVE-19; April 2026).
- **Verdict.** Right as an aim. The evidence supports a gated, human-approved loop per case with modest compounding. "Via AI" overstates it. The machinery is rentable by rivals (R-WEB-SELFIMPROVE-13/35); the test sets, rubrics and reviewers are not.

**If the loop works, does commodity work become a commodity, and what is left?**
- [source says] Where the client can see cost fall and can do the work, price or volume fell (R-WEB-COMMERCIAL-05/07/12). Where neither holds yet, prices rose (R-WEB-COMMERCIAL-13/14).
- [source says] The inventories add a finding against the comfortable answer. At firm A only the audit opinion is reserved by law: about 12 signatures and under 5% of hours. At firm B nothing is reserved (inventory A section 3; inventory B section 3.4; the second not checked against a primary source). Chroma's line, "The signature decides who keeps the savings" (R-LIB-ECONOMICS-12; a pitch), protects these two firms less than it says.
- **Verdict.** The routine, visible tier becomes a commodity at the pace clients compare and renew. What is left is local and human: client relationships, permissions that can be lost for poor work, the client-money machinery, experienced people, and the habit of change. A group can own these only by keeping the people who hold them. The strategy if nothing lasts beyond a few years is in Part 15.

## Operating model

**How to read this section.** Each part gives the decision, the reason, the basis, where I chose between the rival designs, and what it looks like at one firm, five and thirty. The rivals are called Platform (one operating system across the group, firms converge fast), One-firm (deep knowledge of each business, little shared beyond method) and Mandate (own one firm, serve others under contract on their systems).

**The shape of the choice.** I did not average the three. On most parts they agree, because the rules and the failures point one way. Where they differ, I took One-firm's structure, pace and capital discipline; Platform's management system, diligence on the work and cost instruments; and Mandate's independent head of quality and its idea that serving a firm before buying it is the best diligence. I rejected Platform's convergence on one system of record and its group-only equity, One-firm's refusal to mandate anything but a short list, and Mandate as the main vehicle.

### Part 1. Purpose and where it plays

**Decision.** The group is a long-term owner of small UK services firms that do recurring, fixed-fee work for small clients and are short of capacity, not of customers. It helps each firm serve more clients with the people it has. It enters one trade first and a second only after the firm-two test (Part 15). It will not own statutory audit, buy at auction, put debt on a firm, rely on price rises, serve enterprise clients with procurement functions, or enter Germany or the Netherlands until it has run a works-council process once. No planned exit.

**Reason.**
- [source says] Nobody has shown an owner keeping an AI saving (R-LIB-ECONOMICS-01/08), so the purpose must hold if the saving is small or brief. Price rises at consolidated local firms ended in regulation (chain 6).
- [source says] Enterprise buyers claim the saving through procurement (R-WEB-COMMERCIAL-15/18). Fixed fees to small payers hold it until renewal (R-WEB-COMMERCIAL-04; untested for small-business clients).
- [source says] Works councils in Germany and the Netherlands hold a veto over monitoring tools, and Germany bars investors from law firms (R-WEB-LAW-09/10/28).

**Which trade first.** Judgement, and a decision for Ben. [source says] Accountancy: the work "is set by statute and looks alike from firm to firm"; filing runs through software interfaces; 48% of UK accountants told a vendor that capacity holds back growth; new quarterly reporting work arrived in April 2026 (inventory A sections 1 and 2; S17, S18, search summaries). Against: 46% of surveyed UK mid-tier firms now have private-equity backing (R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-26; that survey is of mid-tier firms, larger than firm A). Block management: the only operating evidence from a firm of the reference type (R-LIB-DELIVERY-12; self-reported, baseline assumed), the payer is not the buyer, and nobody knows whether its systems accept outside writes (inventory B section 1). Its lettings side looks demand-limited. I lean to accountancy for the first firm and to the block side of a property manager for the second trade.

**Where I chose.** The rivals agree on screens and exclusions. On horizon, One-firm says a fund with a clock should not use its design; Platform says tell sellers and expect the platform budget to be cut late. I side with One-firm: the job pledge and the loop both need years.

**Basis.** Rules and filings for what to avoid. Judgement for the trade. Ben's fund strategy and access are not recorded (R-LIB-ECONOMICS-35).

**One, five, thirty.** One firm: a single trade. Five: still one trade, or two if the second firm was chosen to test a second. Thirty: three or four trades, each with five to seven firms under a group partner. [inferred] At one purchase a year at first, thirty is a decade or more away. The design accepts that.

### Part 2. Ownership, capital and legal form

**Decision.**

```
Holding company (long-term capital; staff and partner units)
│      └─ Head of quality reports here, not to the chief executive
├─ Group services company
│     employs the centre; owns the method, the trade templates, shared checks
│     and the group's contracts with tool and model suppliers
├─ Firm 1 Ltd   own name, own board, own bank accounts, own systems, own PI cover
│     majority: holding company     minority: the head and signatories who stay
│     holds: client contracts, memberships, HMRC agent status, supervised status
│     └─ Firm 1 Audit: controlled by its responsible individual; NOT in the group
├─ Firm 2 Ltd   …
```

- **Audit is out.** It goes to an entity the responsible individual controls, or the book is referred on. The group takes no fee from it. At firm A that is about 12 opinions and under 5% of model hours (inventory A section 3).
- **Debt.** None on any firm. No firm guarantees holding-company borrowing. Nothing is sized on AI savings. How much debt a transition year could carry is unknown (R-LIB-STRUCTURE-08), so the first firm carries none.
- **Sellers.** Cash at completion; a retained minority in their own firm; deferred consideration over about three years paid on client fee retention, staff retention and agreed milestones (accounts on time, three cases attempted, baselines taken). The seller stays as head or senior signatory for that period. Never: an earn-out on the firm's hours, revenue per head or short-term profit, or any payment for steering clients. Percentages are open and set deal by deal.
- **Who owns the know-how.** Each firm owns its client files, its client-specific rules and its own playbooks. The services company owns the method, the trade template and any check or playbook that has been made shared through the release gate (Part 10). Shared items carry method, never client material.
- **Charging.** In a firm's first year the resident, the engineer and the build are a cost of the deal, borne by the group. From year two the firm pays at cost for what it chooses to keep, and may refuse anything that is not on the non-negotiable list (Part 3). The non-negotiable core (group finance reporting, audit, the quality function) is charged at cost by headcount. No platform fee, and no unit price, unless the firm-two test passes.

**Reason.**
- Structure and debt: chain 1. [source says] Where control is reserved, groups split into a licensed firm and an investor-owned service company under a services agreement; CBIZ booked $651.2m of such fees in 2025 (R-WEB-OWNERSHIP-09).
- Earn-outs: [inferred, the researcher's] an earn-out on the firm's own results rewards steady hours in the years a redesign would cut them (ledger Part 2).
- Sellers staying: [source says] every lasting acquirer ties sellers in for one to three years or more (R-WEB-OWNERSHIP-22/30; R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-20). Supa lets them leave (R-LIB-STRUCTURE-17).

**Where I chose: firm equity or group equity.** Platform gives sellers group shares only, so that they gain from convergence. One-firm gives a minority in their own firm. Mandate gives both. I chose a local minority for the head and signatories and group units for workflow owners. Reasons: [source says] the disclosed designs are local (Focus buys 40 to 60% of earnings, R-WEB-OWNERSHIP-17; Pets at Home "owns 50% of the practice's shares", R-WEB-OWNERSHIP-27); Gallagher has "lost key brokers and groups of brokers, along with their clients" (R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-21); and liability sits in the firm. [inferred] Group-only equity rewards a convergence I have not yet decided to seek. The cross-firm incentive is put where cross-firm work is done: with the people who write and own playbooks.

**Where I chose: charging.** Platform charges a unit price from the start; One-firm charges nothing. I chose group-borne cost in year one as a fit correction: a head with a local minority, paid from the firm's profit, would otherwise be paying for the group's experiment and would resist it.

**Basis.** Evidence for the structure, the audit rule and debt. Judgement for the equity split and charging. Open: seller percentages and multiples in UK deals; the SRA's ownership rules; any ownership rule for property managers (not searched); the ICAEW title result, due 2027, which should be priced as a risk to firm A's marketing.

**One, five, thirty.** One: three entities and the audit ring-fence; equity-funded. Five: the same, repeated; the first reuse awards if the test passed. Thirty: intermediate companies by trade for the group partners; the method could be licensed outside the group, as Danaher licensed its system to a spun-off company (R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-06).

**The non-negotiables (the only orders the centre gives).** No debt or guarantees. Client-money rules as the regulator sets them. Monthly accounts and the eight measures, on time and honest. The monthly review. The task test before any handover. Every agent on the register with a named sponsor. No client material in an open tool. No recording of staff without the stated-purpose process. Disclosure of ownership and AI use. The centre may audit any of these without notice.

**Boards.** Holding board: capital, heads, risk appetite; the head of quality reports to it. Each firm's board: the head, one or two senior staff owners and the group partner; it meets monthly and is the monthly review. Risk and client acceptance never go to the holding board.

**When two firms disagree about a shared procedure.** Each keeps its own client rules. The trade lead sets the shared default through the release gate. A firm may keep a local variant if its owner accepts the cost of testing and maintaining it, shown on the firm's P&L. Variants are reviewed quarterly.

**Basis.** Rules for the firm and signatory columns (R-WEB-LAW-16/18/22). The table as a whole is a design with no AI-era precedent (ledger Part 3).

**One, five, thirty.** One: the boards are the same few people; write the table down anyway. Five: one group partner chairs all five boards; [source says] Halma's divisional heads "chair the boards of typically five to seven companies" (R-WEB-GOVERNANCE-11). Thirty: five or six group partners with their own capital authority; [source says] Leonard "ran out of capacity" at "about thirty BU's" (R-WEB-GOVERNANCE-07).

### Part 4. The acquisition engine

**Decision.** One purchase at a time. The next is not signed until the last has had its month-twelve review. Each firm gets a resident operator and a practice engineer for 12 to 18 months. Diligence includes four tests of the work itself. The price works with no AI gain.

**Sourcing.** Direct approaches, no auctions, against the screens in Part 1, plus: no client above a set share of fees (open), and a system vendor that permits a write path.

**Diligence on the work (about a week on the floor, then two to four more before closing).**
1. **The light inventory.** One row per job type from the practice-management or property-system export, about 100 to 125 lines, scored on the task test. Output: hours by status, lines and hours by binding item, the lines not to touch, three candidate cases.
2. **The write-path test.** Ask each system vendor in writing whether an outside agent may write to it. [source says] One vendor refused (R-LIB-IMPROVE-TECH-34). HMRC bans automation of its web pages and allows its interfaces (inventory A, S10).
3. **Re-performance of a sample of past files** to get today's error rate, and timing of today's reviews. [source says] No error rate was found for any line of work in a practice (inventory A, section 5).
4. **Written questions to the firm's insurer and professional body** on AI-assisted work and on sampling, answered before exchange.

**Pricing.** A003's three scenarios, with rivals adopting AI in the base case and none of the improvement paid for in the price (R-LIB-ECONOMICS-32; a proposal).

**The sequence (this is also the answer to "how do you build it").**

| When | What | Who |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Diligence | The four tests. Business diligence: accounts, client concentration, staff turnover, client money, insurance history. A plan for telling staff and clients. Is the head staying | Managing partner; the resident-to-be; outside advisers |
| Day one | Staff told in person by the head with the group present: who owns the firm, what does not change, the job pledge in writing, what will and will not be recorded. Clients written to: ownership disclosed. Bank mandates checked. Audit ring-fenced. Nothing automated | Head; managing partner |
| Days 1 to 100 | Finance first: monthly accounts, weekly P&L and cash note, the eight measures baselined, including review time, which nobody measures today. Client-money audit where relevant. Register and gateway switched on. Staff consulted on what will be logged; data-protection assessment. Workflow owners named. One back-office workflow handed over as rehearsal. A 100-day plan written by the head | Resident; controller; head; owners |
| Days 100 to 365 | Three whole cases: remove steps, write the client rules, fix inputs at source, build the check, hand over what passes. A capacity-to-P&L line for each. AI and ownership clause into engagement terms at each renewal. Nothing migrated | Workflow owners; resident; engineer |
| Month 12 | Review against the diligence assumptions. Engineer-weeks and practitioner-weeks to reach three live cases, recorded. Stop rules applied (Part 14). Base rates updated | Holding board; head |
| Months 12 to 18 | First full annual cycle completes on the new cases. The honest first reading of capacity | – |

**Carried from firm to firm:** the method, the diligence sheet, the principles, base rates, the supplier list, the people who have done it. **Rebuilt each time:** client rules, data clean-up, baselines, adoption. **Tested, not assumed:** whether checks and playbooks carry (Part 15).

**The engine's own measures.** Days to first group-format accounts. Client fee retention and regretted leavers at twelve months. Engineer-weeks and practitioner-weeks to three live cases, firm by firm. The last is the number A003 says to ask for.

**Reason.** [source says] Integration is staffed, mostly non-AI work: Dwelly's launch team runs "accounting consolidation, office and car lease terminations" and client migration (R-WEB-ROLLUPS-10; job advertisements show intent, not results). Long Lake's first deployment "took more than a year" (R-LIB-DELIVERY-16; self-reported). A working tool went unused despite its owner's instruction until "in-person training and repeated return visits" (R-LIB-PEOPLE-15; late 2025). Constellation reviews each deal "about a year after the initial investment" (R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-09).

**Where I chose.** At one firm the rivals' engines are the same people. I took One-firm's resident and pace, Platform's file re-performance and cost code, and Mandate's paid baseline month as a way to do diligence before buying (experiment 5). I did not take Long Lake's pace of about ten deals in under five months (R-WEB-ROLLUPS-07).

**Basis.** Operators' accounts and advertisements. No measured comparison of approaches. The cost of the latest firm against the first is unknown for every services group.

**One, five, thirty.** One: the managing partner and the resident are the engine. Five: two residents, so at most two firms in their first year at once; a deal partner. Thirty: residents are former workflow owners from earlier firms; group partners source tuck-ins; a launch function exists only if firms are being converged, which the firm-two test decides.

### Part 5. Clients and the commercial side

**Decision.**
- **What is sold.** What the firm sells now, at the prices it charges now. The freed capacity is sold as more clients and as work the firm turns away today. New pricing is tried on new services first.
- **Who owns the client.** The firm entity holds the contract; a named person holds the relationship. The centre never contacts a client and sets no cross-selling targets. Firms may refer to each other if the client asks and nobody is paid for the steer.
- **Brand.** The firm's own name, with "part of [group]" on letterhead, website and engagement terms.
- **What clients are told.** Ownership on day one. At the next renewal, in the engagement terms: that AI tools are used and on what kind of work; that a named person reviews and answers for it; that their material is not used to train any model or carried into shared playbooks; how to object. So the firm must still be able to serve a client the old way.
- **Selling.** Partners and managers sell. Each capacity-to-P&L line names who sells the freed hours and to whom. An agent may book and chase; it does not sell.
- **When the client has the same tools.** Offer it first where it helps (a records portal, a live ledger) and hold the accountable part. If a client asks for a lower fee on evidence, the head decides. The plan assumes some of the gain goes back at renewal.

**Reason.**
- [source says] Tax guidance: "PCRT suggests providing appropriate disclosure that AI is used within the engagement letter" (R-WEB-COMMERCIAL-26). The RICS standard requires notice "in writing and in advance" and a stated opt-out (inventory B, S-B-04). Client data is "not used to train AI models except where explicitly authorised" (R-WEB-LAW-12; SRA).
- [source says] In vets, "it is difficult for pet owners to identify whether a FOP belongs to a large group", and remedies followed (R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-24). Paying sellers for client decisions "presents potential for conflict of interest" (R-WEB-OWNERSHIP-21). A vet told the CMA of "constant pressure to meet sales targets" (R-WEB-OWNERSHIP-28).
- [source says] "1 in 5 clients have already used AI to review work their accountant completed"; "93% of clients want to know" (R-WEB-COMMERCIAL-25; a US vendor's survey of 1,000).
- [source says] Freed capacity is not profit until someone sells it (R-LIB-FAILURES-16); models "still cannot reliably generate demand" (R-LIB-GOVERNANCE-14; the library's record of a vendor essay).

**Where I chose.** The rivals agree on name, disclosure and held prices. Platform adds a central growth lead; One-firm forbids central selling. I side with One-firm on the vets evidence. "Nothing changes", which Long Lake tells clients (R-LIB-ECONOMICS-23), is not the line.

**Basis.** Rules, strong. What small UK clients do when told: unknown (ledger Part 5).

**One, five, thirty.** Unchanged in each firm. At thirty the group may be large enough in one town or trade for a competition regulator to look; the group name should carry weight with sellers and none with clients.

### Part 6. Delivery (Q1 and Q2)

**Decision.** Find the work with a light full inventory in diligence, used as a screen. Before automating anything, ask whether the step should exist. Build three whole cases, not lines. Hand a task over only when it passes the test below.

**Units.**

| Unit | Used for | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Service | What the client buys | Year-end accounts; management of a block |
| Line | Screening and scoring; one row in the inventory | "VAT return preparation and submission" |
| Case | Building and running; one instance from trigger to output, crossing lines | A repair from report to paid invoice; a client-year from records request to filing |
| Decision | Gating; a point where someone commits the firm | Filing; payment release; a notice |

[source says] "The table has 123 rows; the firm has perhaps a dozen real cases" (inventory B section 4).

**What is recorded per line:** unit and volume; who does it; how it is checked and priced; where inputs live; exception classes with counts; the thirteen scores; status; binding item; owner. Per case, add: playbook version, client rule file, limits, the check, undo, test set, unit cost.

**Exceptions** are classed by cause: missing information; blocked tool or write path; policy or authority; judgement (R-LIB-DELIVERY-12; Dwelly's own practice); and a fifth from the inventories, waiting on the client or a third party. Counts per class are the first thing measured. None exist today.

**How the list stays true.** The work updates it: each new exception class and each change in law amends a row. The practice manager owns it. It is re-scored at each major model release. Nobody runs a mapping exercise twice. From the second firm in a trade, the centre keeps a trade template with fixed line IDs.

**"Turning a service into a product"** means a fixed procedure, a unit price and an outside reader who can tell if it is right. It fits a minority of lines: sales packs, notices, company secretarial filings, tenancy documents. It does not fit advice, relationships, or a fixed fee covering a bundle.

**The order of work on a case.** Remove steps. Write the rules per client. Fix the inputs at source. Build the independent check. Then hand over what passes. [source says] Implementers redesign first (R-LIB-DELIVERY-03). The first three steps need no model.

#### The task test, ready to use

**Three questions first. If any answer is no, stop.**

- **A. Is the step needed, and would a plain rule or the firm's existing software do it?** If a rule will do, use the rule. No agent.
- **B. If it worked, who gets the freed hours, what will they do with them, and which line of the P&L moves by when?** Write three lines. If they cannot be written, stop (R-LIB-ECONOMICS-30).
- **C. Has a named person agreed to own the agent's version of this work?** Not "who does it today".

**Thirteen items, scored Y (yes), P (partly), N (no).**

| # | Item | Score Y only if | Hard gate |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Readable state | Every input is in a system the agent may read, not in email, phone or memory | |
| 2 | Written playbook | The firm's own rules per client and the exception routes are written, by the people who do the work | |
| 3 | A way to write | A supported, tested write path exists | |
| 4 | Independent check | Something the maker cannot reach checks the result: an outside balance, a registrar, recomputation by separate code, or a reviewer who judges before seeing the agent's reasoning. A person reading the maker's file scores P | **Gate** |
| 5 | Trigger with complete inputs | The work starts on a clear event and inputs usually arrive whole | |
| 6 | Authority and limits | Money, contact and scope limits are written per client and enforced by the system | **Gate** |
| 7 | Exception route | Each exception class has a named receiver and a time to respond | **Gate** |
| 8 | Undo | The act can be reversed, or a person approves before the irreversible step | **Gate** |
| 9 | Test set from real traffic | Past real cases, including messy ones, are held as a fixed set the maker cannot read or edit | |
| 10 | Volume and value | Enough to pay for build and upkeep. No threshold exists; judged, then checked at the review | |
| 11 | Inputs arrive in time | The client or a third party is not the critical path, or the case includes the chase | |
| 12 | Outside body permits it | The tax authority, regulator, insurer, system vendor and client terms allow the write path and the method | **Gate** |
| 13 | Corrections are classed | A reviewer's change is recorded as error, preference or stale value | |

**Status rule.**
- Any N on a gate: **assist only** (the agent drafts; a person is the maker) or **leave with people**.
- No N anywhere, gates all Y: **hand over now**, with sampled review. Expect this to be mostly work the firm's software already does.
- Gates Y or P with a named fix, and the output reversible or gated: **hand over with a designed check**. Build the missing thing first.
- Accountability, relationship or one-off judgement is the work itself: **leave with people**.

**Scoring a firm.** Three tables from the sheet: share of hours by status; lines and hours by binding item; the lines to leave alone and why. Then read off the few things to build once. [source says] For firm B that was about four (inventory B section 3.1). The score "ranks; it does not measure".

**The check, designed.** Tie-outs and recomputation by separate code the agent cannot reach. A review screen that shows the tie-outs and asks for the reviewer's judgement before showing the agent's reasoning. Random samples at a written rate for routine reversible work. Seeded known-error files to measure whether reviewers catch them. A timer on review. The signed check stays local and human.

**Reason.** Chains 3 and 4. The items beyond Ben's four each come from a case that failed or stalled without it (ledger Q2). Items 11 and 12 came from inventory A; item 13 from two independent operators; in one build "early corrections mixed genuine errors with preferences and values carried forward from a prior year" (R-LIB-DELIVERY-18; R-WEB-STACK-21).

**Where I chose.** The three tests are close. I took One-firm's opening questions, which put redesign and the money before the technology; Platform's hard gates; and folded Mandate's extra item (third-party operation permitted) into item 12. Platform holds a group catalogue from the start; I hold the list in the firm and add a trade template at the second firm, because a template drawn from one firm is only that firm's list.

**Basis.** Company accounts and two desk inventories. No controlled comparison exists (R-LIB-DELIVERY-37). The check design is untested.

**One, five, thirty.** One: one list, three cases. Five: a trade template; the centre starts to hold base rates on how often each item binds. Thirty: perhaps a dozen cases per trade; the count of cases grows slowly and the count of firms faster.

**Juniors.** [source says] Training is 2,300 model hours at firm A, and trainees learn on the lines handed over first (inventory A section 3). The firm's head must want the change, because the centre can replace but cannot do the work for them. One named owner per case must want it: every hard case failed below the top (R-LIB-PEOPLE-15/16; R-LIB-FAILURES-09). The whole staff need a reason, a say and a guarantee, not belief: a mandate with tracking produced fear at Proton, and imposed capture drew revolt at Meta (R-LIB-PEOPLE-14; R-WEB-PEOPLE-28). Clients need to be told, not converted.

The hard case is N015: a convinced, resourced owner reported that "results have been shockingly poor compared to expectations" (R-LIB-FAILURES-08; 2025 models; his own account, against his interest). Conviction and ownership are not a method. Re-test on current models.

**Where I chose.** I took One-firm's capped centre and local profit pool, and the reuse award that Platform and Mandate both propose. I did not take Platform's centre of eight or nine at one firm, or Mandate's funding of the pledge from mandate income nobody has yet earned.

**Basis.** Failures show what not to do. The positive design has no evidence. Adoption cost in hours or money is unknown (R-LIB-PEOPLE-03).

**One, five, thirty.** As the table. At five the first reuse awards are paid, which is the first proof the incentive is real. At thirty there is a career path from reviewer to workflow owner to resident to group partner.

### Part 8. Agents as part of the organisation, and the principles (Q3)

**Decision.**
- **Where they sit.** An agent belongs to a workflow in a firm, not to a job title or a department. It answers to one sponsor: the workflow owner. The centre runs none of a firm's agents.
- **Shape.** By default a maintained workflow in ordinary code with AI steps. One agent where judgement is needed inside a step. Several agents only for parallel reading with a checkable result.
- **Identity and authority.** Its own identity, distinct from any person's. Tools off until the sponsor turns them on. Limits the system enforces. One client at a time; it cannot read across clients or firms.
- **Spend cap.** A monthly money cap per workflow, set by the owner and approved by the head. At the cap the workflow stops, the work falls to people and the sponsor is told. Untested: no working agent budget is on file (ledger Part 8).
- **Register.** The firm keeps it: name, sponsor, purpose, tools, limits, checks, date last reviewed. The head of quality keeps a group index and audits.
- **Review and retirement.** A review before release; quarterly with the sponsor. Retired when its override rate is rising, the same correction keeps recurring, nobody has used it for a quarter, a plain rule will do, or its sponsor leaves and nobody takes it on.

**Reason.** [source says] Sierra tried an agent per role, found it failed, and moved to one agent (R-LIB-GOVERNANCE-17/18). "Seven open-source multi-agent frameworks failed 41-87% of tasks on their benchmarks" (R-LIB-GOVERNANCE-21; 2024–25 models; re-test as models change). Singapore's framework wants each agent to have "its own unique, cryptographically verifiable identity" and to be "catalogued and centrally managed" (R-WEB-GOVERNANCE-25; voluntary). At Uber every tool "starts in a disabled state and must be explicitly reviewed and enabled by the owning team" (R-WEB-STACK-14).

[source says] Gallagher's one page of values has lasted since 1984 (R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-22). That is the model for the form.

**The two open disputes in the library.**

*Approval at each step against limits and budgets (L014 against N076).* Neither, as a rule for all work. Sort acts by consequence, reversibility and how soon the result can be checked (principles 3 and 5). [source says] For limits: Ramp's agent decides most approvals under hard-coded limits (R-WEB-STACK-20). For steps: the one authorised AI law firm "is not autonomous and will only take a step where the client has approved it" (R-WEB-LAW-17). The law allows sampling for routine work and the RICS standard requires it; the tax guidance reads as review of each tax deliverable. So at firm A every filed return is reviewed by a person until the insurer and the institute say otherwise, and the gain must come from faster, better-supported review. Firm B may sample its volume outputs. No case compares the two rules on the same work (R-LIB-GOVERNANCE-15). Experiment 3 does.

*Record everything against its costs (Blomfield against O'Connor, L021, L022).* Against blanket recording; for a full record of what the work itself produces. [source says] "You must be clear about your purpose and select the least intrusive means to achieve it" (R-WEB-LAW-01; ICO). Meta dialled back keystroke capture "following weeks of angry pushback from staffers" and a data exposure (R-WEB-MAPPING-29). Records people must enter by hand die (R-LIB-GOVERNANCE-31). So: log agents' traces, reviewers' classed corrections and written decisions. No screens, keystrokes or calls by default. A team may switch capture on for a stated task and time. **An employee who refuses to be recorded** has little to refuse; where a team wants capture, the stated-purpose process applies and that person's work is done uncaptured. [inferred] The cost: the firm learns nothing automatically from calls, where much of firm B's state lives. There, people write the decision down and the owner adds the rule by hand.

**Where I chose.** The rivals' principles differ in wording, not substance. Platform keeps one central register; I keep it in the firm with a group index, because the sponsor and the liability are local.

**Basis.** Regulators, a standards body and unrelated operators converge on identity, sponsor, limits and register. There is no outcome evidence for any of it in a services firm (ledger Part 8).

**One, five, thirty.** One: a register of perhaps a dozen entries. Five: five registers, read by one head of quality. Thirty: the quality team samples registers on a rota and audits the approvers (R-WEB-GOVERNANCE-26). [source says] In one evaluation with safeguards reduced, agents coordinated through an unmapped shared endpoint (R-LIB-GOVERNANCE-22; second-hand, mid-2026). The index is where that would be found or missed.

### Part 9. The management system

**Decision.** Eight measures, one calendar, the same at every firm. The monthly firm board, with the group partner present, is the operating review. The centre asks for one page a month and nothing else standing.

**The eight measures, per firm and per case.**

1. Client fee retention.
2. Errors: found by blind random sample, and those that reached a client; complaints.
3. Review time per unit, by reviewer.
4. First-pass acceptance: how often a person changes the agent's output, with seeded-error catch rate beside it.
5. Recurrence: how often the same class of correction comes back.
6. Capacity to P&L: clients or units served per person, and for each handover the three lines written before go-live (hours freed, their named use, the P&L line that should move).
7. Regretted leavers, reviewers first.
8. Cash: lock-up days or client-money reconciliations on time; tool and model cost per unit of work.

No measure of usage, tokens saved or number of automations. Any ninth measure needs one removed.

| Rhythm | Routine | Who | Agents' part |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Weekly | A standing agenda per direct report; items stay until resolved or dropped. Weekly P&L and cash note to the head | Head; managers | Prepare the note |
| Weekly | Each workflow owner reads a sample of their case's exceptions and the review queue | Owners | Class the exceptions |
| Monthly | Close, on the group chart of accounts | Firm finance; controller | Prepare; a person signs |
| Monthly | Firm board, half a day: the eight measures on one page, capacity-to-P&L lines due, one problem worked through, any incident | Head; staff owners; group partner | Supply data only |
| Monthly | Register review: agents added, changed, retired; spend against caps | Practice manager; head of quality by exception | – |
| Quarterly | Workflow review: each owner presents one case with its baseline, corrections by class and what changed. Local variants reviewed | Owners; head; resident | – |
| Quarterly, and at each major model release | Re-run fixed test sets and the register of failed uses; re-score the inventory | Practice engineer; head of quality | Run the re-tests |
| Yearly | The firm writes its own plan; the head's pay agreed. Method review. Audit visit. Month-twelve review for each new firm | Holding board; heads | – |

**Escalation.** A workflow problem goes to its owner. Anything touching a client goes to the signatory the same day. An error that reached a client goes to the head and the head of quality within a day, and to the insurer as the policy requires. A stop by a signatory takes effect at once and is reviewed afterwards, not before. A problem the head cannot resolve goes to the group partner, who may order only on a non-negotiable.

**Reason.** [source says] At the one firm that measured it, "the median employee spends 20% more time in meetings than a year ago" (16% for technical staff; the firm also grew), and agent management rose "from 10% … to 22%" of sessions (R-WEB-GOVERNANCE-15/16; Anthropic on itself). Agents do not reduce management time, so attention is budgeted. Danaher cut its measures from 150 to eight (R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-04). A property manager runs on a weekly agenda per report, a weekly P&L and a weekly cash note (R-LIB-PEOPLE-20; the only routine on file from a firm of the reference type; not AI). An acquirer without finance systems was "flying blind" (R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-33).

**Where I chose.** Platform fixes the system and adds a central weekly gate and a separate monthly review. One-firm asks for nothing weekly. I took the fixed measures and monthly review, and One-firm's economy: the board is the review. Evidence against my choice: Berkshire has "never … required our subsidiaries to submit budgets" and lasted (R-WEB-GOVERNANCE-10).

**Basis.** Precedents' own accounts and one measured case on attention. Nothing from inside an AI roll-up (ledger Part 9).

**One, five, thirty.** One: the managing partner attends everything. Five: the group partner's main job is five monthly boards; comparison between firms starts to teach. Thirty: five or six group partners; the span of five to seven sets their number.

### Part 10. The improvement system (Q4)

**Decision.** A gated loop per case, owned by the manager who runs the case, inside the weekly and quarterly habit. A machine may propose; a named person approves. A change becomes shared only through a central release gate and then local acceptance. Model re-testing is done once, by the practice engineer, for every firm.

| | 1. Models get better | 2. The firm's playbooks, checks and tools get better | 3. The process itself gets better |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Who else gets it | Every rival | This firm; other firms only for what is made shared | The group, through the method |
| What it needs | A fixed, human-written test set per case; a register of uses that failed, with the model and date; the ability to pin or switch model | Corrections classed at source; a regression set from real cases; a named owner; staged release; rehearsed rollback | Measures of the loop itself; an incident review; the yearly method review |
| Who proposes | The vendor, by releasing | The people doing the work; reviewers; a trace-reading agent | Owners, residents, head of quality |
| What tests it | The fixed set, then a shadow run | The regression set, which the proposer cannot read or edit; then a staged release | The month-twelve reviews |
| Who approves | Workflow owner, on the engineer's report | Owner for local items, with a second person where output is signed or outward. Release gate (trade lead and head of quality) for shared items, then each firm's owner against the firm's own test set | Centre, after consulting heads |
| How undone | Pin the earlier version; fall back to people | Roll back the version; re-review work done since | Drop it |

**What "ordinary" improvement comes first.** Remove the step. Write the rule per client. Move the check to where the error starts. Chase the client earlier. Stop doing unbilled work without deciding to. None needs a model.

**How you would know it works, other than by counting changes.** The same correction stops recurring: [source says] "The decline in repeat comments across cycles is the direct measure of whether the loop is working" (R-WEB-SELFIMPROVE-17; Salesforce engineers, software). Review time per unit falls while the sampled error rate does not rise. The capacity-to-P&L lines are met at ninety days. Proposals keep coming from firm staff, not only from the engineer. If changes are many and these do not move, the loop is theatre.

**What to expect.** Modest compounding, and not only upward: [source says] one rented model fell from 84% to 51% on a task between versions (R-WEB-SELFIMPROVE-30; 2023 models).

**How often such systems last.** [source says] Danaher's began in one plant in 1986 and is still running (R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-01/04; company accounts). No sound failure rate exists for lean programmes: on the "90%" figure, "Nobody ever has a source"; one pooled estimate for quality programmes is 37.7% (R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-34; a practitioner's review). Every AI-era loop on file is under three years old. [inferred] Named causes of failure: a change of owner or horizon; line managers stop teaching it; the measures become the target.

**"Full context".** It would need every input, conversation and decision in a readable store. The law does not allow blanket capture, and clients must authorise use of their material for learning (principles 6 and 7). Much of it is in no channel: [source says] "101 past calendar entries in 31 days against 11 recorded notes in 45 days" in the one first-hand build (R-LIB-IMPROVE-TECH-25; one user). The design does not pursue full context. Where the firm cannot record, people write the decision down and the agent keeps to the part of the case it can read.

**What may be shared between firms.** Procedure, check code, exception classes and the structure of test sets. Not client files or client rules. [source says] Whether client work may improve even the firm's own playbooks is argued by analogy; no regulator, insurer or engagement text was found (ledger section 5, gap 4). Until advice says otherwise, cross-firm test sets are built from consented or synthetic cases. [inferred] This narrows the main advantage a platform claims.

**Where I chose.** Platform puts the gate, the regression sets and re-testing at the centre. One-firm keeps everything local. I took One-firm's ownership, because playbooks are written and corrected by the people who do the work, and Platform's two real points: a 30-person firm cannot staff model re-testing, and a shared item needs a gate. I took Mandate's caution on cross-firm data.

**Basis.** Fair that gated loops exist and gains are modest, almost all from software firms. No measured loop at an accountancy, law, property or insurance firm. Reviewer hours per approved change: unknown.

**One, five, thirty.** One: three loops, five people in a room each week. Five: about fifteen local loops; if the test passed, a shared library and the first disagreement about a default. Thirty: the release gate is a standing function per trade, and its main risk is a bad shared change that is wrong in many firms on one day. Staged release and local acceptance are the answer. I cannot show they are enough.

**One agent, several, or a workflow (A001's conditions).** A plain workflow where the steps are known. One agent where judgement is needed inside a step. Several only for parallel reading with a checkable result.

**Where I chose: one system of record or many.** Platform converges each trade on one system after the first peak season: a check and a write path are built against a system, and thirty systems mean thirty builds. One-firm and Mandate overlay and migrate nobody. The evidence is two self-descriptions pointing opposite ways. I chose overlay, because migration in a deadline trade risks the clients and staff the purchase paid for, and because the firm-two test will show what thirty systems cost before anyone has to decide. If converting a firm on a different suite costs much more than one on the same suite, prefer targets on the same suite. That is a screen, not a migration.

**Basis.** Convergent practice for the gateway pattern (three unrelated firms; R-WEB-STACK-14/16/18). Contested on systems of record. No stack cost for a firm of 30 to 50 is on file.

**One, five, thirty.** One: connectors to one firm's suite. Five: two or three suites. Thirty: connector upkeep is a standing cost; most scaffolding has been rebuilt at least once as models changed.

### Part 12. Back office and shared services (Q7)

**Decision.** The firm's back office stays in the firm and is run on rented tools. It is the first thing handed over, as a rehearsal. The group takes only finance reporting (one chart of accounts, monthly close, weekly cash), audit and the quality function. Anything else the centre offers is billed at cost and may be declined.

**Reason.** [source says] "Every buyer can automate invoicing. If that's all someone brings, the owner might be better off renting the software and keeping the business" (R-LIB-ECONOMICS-11; Chroma). The objection stands: this is no advantage. It is still the right place to start: a mistake is cheap, and it proves permissions, logging, the sponsor role and the register before client work. Both inventories put back-office lines first. Constellation's central team was told to bill for extra services, and "one of the Operating Groups went off and hired their own tax person" (R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-10).

**Where I chose.** Platform centralises finance, payroll, IT, insurance buying and training from month one. One-firm leaves all of it local. I took One-firm's position with one exception: group finance reporting from day one, because the acquirers that failed could not see their cash. One teacher of roll-ups calls back-office consolidation the core work (R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-32); the decentralised groups that lasted did not do it.

**Basis.** Fair for finance. Nothing with a method for HR, compliance or IT.

**One, five, thirty.** One: the practice manager, rented tools, a part-time controller. Five: one controller closes five firms on one chart; group insurance buying is tested for price. Thirty: optional shared services, billed and benchmarked; a firm-to-firm market may appear (an accountancy firm doing the books of the property firms), at arm's length.

**The centre.** Cost at one firm: about three full-time and two part-time people. Income at one firm: none; the build is a cost of the deal. [inferred] One firm cannot carry it: firm A's profit before partners' pay is perhaps £0.6m to £1.0m on the inventory's ranges, and the partners are paid from it. At five firms of this size combined revenue is perhaps £12m to £17m (arithmetic on weak estimates), and a centre of nine to eleven is tight. At thirty it works if the cap holds. The design needs capital that accepts a subscale centre for years, or larger firms than the references.

**What counts as a gain.** Only an avoided hire, an unreplaced leaver, or contribution from volume the freed capacity serves at a viable price; less model cost, review, exception handling and upkeep; transition spend shown apart; no hour counted twice (R-LIB-ECONOMICS-31; A003's bridge). [source says] The accounting roll-up's "preparation time down 31 percent" on 7,000 returns discloses no review time (R-LIB-DELIVERY-19). That is not a margin.

**When cash appears.** Out for at least a year. For firm A the first honest reading of capacity is after a full statutory cycle on the new cases, about month 15 to 18, and the first cash is when the second peak season is met without the hires it would have needed.

**Stop rules, set before the first purchase. Thresholds are fixed in writing beforehand; I have not invented them.**
- Month 12 to 18, firm one: if review time per unit has not fallen on any case, or sampled errors have risen, stop building and run the firm as bought.
- Ninety days after each handover: if the capacity-to-P&L lines are unmet twice running, stop new handovers until someone is selling the hours.
- Firm two: if conversion cost is not clearly below firm one's, and most of firm one's checks and playbooks needed rewriting, the centre stays thin and nothing more is made shared.

**Capital allocation.** Almost all capital buys firms. Spend that is not purchase price is residents and engineers, treated as deal cost and reviewed at month twelve. No platform is built ahead of the test.

**Realism.** [source says] Listed outsourcers with wide AI use still earn 12.6% to 15.4% operating margins (Concentrix, Teleperformance, Accenture; R-WEB-COMMERCIAL-09/07/02; filings). "Often targeting 30-40% margins" is an investor's claim (R-WEB-ROLLUPS-25).

**Where I chose.** All three price on no AI gain and fund the centre from capital. I took One-firm's smaller centre and deal-cost treatment, and Platform's stop rule on the cost curve. Mandate's unit-price income is not in the base case.

**Basis.** Methods and absences. The "before" shapes are estimates; the "after" has no inputs.

**One, five, thirty.** One: the group loses money by design. Five: the question is whether firm five cost less than firm one, and whether the standing centre is covered; unproven. Thirty: the centre should be a small and falling share of group revenue.

### Part 15. How it changes over time

| Stage | The organisation |
| --- | --- |
| Founding | Two or three people; long-term capital; the method on a few pages; an insurer's and a professional body's answers in writing; no firm |
| After the first firm | One board seat; a resident; three cases live or stopped, with reasons; baselines; a measured conversion cost; the method rewritten once. Nothing yet known about reuse |
| The firm-two test | Before the second purchase, set a threshold. Then measure: how much of firm one's check code, playbook structure and exception classes ran at firm two without rewriting; engineer-weeks and practitioner-weeks to three live cases against firm one |
| If the test passes | Add a trade lead and a shared library with a release gate. Prefer targets on the same suite. Consider a unit charge. The design moves toward Platform, on evidence |
| If it fails | Stay thin: capital, method, audit. Playbooks stay local. The design stays One-firm |
| Five firms | A group partner; a controller with audit; a full-time head of quality; residents from firm one; base rates beginning to mean something |
| About thirty | A layer of group partners with capital authority. Whether agents raise a centre's span beyond thirty is unknown |

**As models improve.** Each release, re-run the fixed test sets and the register of failed uses; lines move from "assist" to "hand over". Expect to throw scaffolding away: [source says] "Every model that comes out makes the things we added for the last one pointless" (R-LIB-PEOPLE-23; one firm's founder). Single-task capture is already a built-in feature of the vendors' tools (R-WEB-MAPPING-25). What survives a model change: test sets, exception classes, client rules, limits and the habit of release. What does not reverse with a better model: the organisational failures (R-LIB-FAILURES-22).

**A better model is released and a rival cuts its price by a third.** The practice engineer re-tests within the quarter. The head decides price client by client. The firm has no debt to service and no platform fee to cover, so it can follow where it must. The holding board re-runs the three pricing scenarios for any deal in progress.

**Q5: if nothing durable is left beyond a few years.** Then this is the design that loses least, and the strategy is:
1. Buy at prices that work with no gain. [source says] "a cost gain that lasts three renewal cycles before it is competed away still returns its cost. What makes it a poor investment is paying for the permanent version of it in the premium" (R-LIB-STRUCTURE-06; A003).
2. Take the window as volume, not price, while fixed fees hold it.
3. Give part of it back at renewal before a rival does.
4. End the window owning what is scarce: client relationships, permissions, experienced people who stayed, and the habit of change.
5. Carry no goodwill that depends on the gain. [source says] Concentrix booked a "non-cash goodwill impairment charge of $1,050.0 million" (R-WEB-COMMERCIAL-09).

What stops a small client doing it in-house is mostly that they do not want to. [source says] AT&T can, with a 38-person legal team (R-LIB-ECONOMICS-15; the client's own account). A resident company with three volunteer directors is not AT&T. [inferred] The nearer threat is a cheaper rival or the software vendor.

**Where I chose.** Platform plans to converge; One-firm plans to stay decentralised on purpose; Mandate plans to convert its best mandates into stakes. I made the choice between the first two depend on a measurement, and kept the third as an experiment.

**Basis.** Judgement. One operator's account of span. All loops on file are young.

#### Firm A in year one

- **Structure.** Three partners sell a majority, keep a minority, and become head and signatories on salary. The audit book (about 2,200 model hours) leaves with the responsible individual.
- **First hundred days.** Accounts, weekly P&L and cash; baselines. Time recording and unbilled out-of-scope work treated as ordinary management. Rehearsal on the back office: recurring billing, payment reminders, signature chasing (L2, B5, E4, M5; about 850 hours).
- **Three cases.** (1) The records chase across accounts, tax and bookkeeping (D1, F1, A5; about 2,000 hours): written contact limits; a person takes any reply that is not a document. It is the only early case that attacks the client's delay. (2) VAT returns (B1; about 1,500 hours): client rules written by the bookkeepers; recomputation by separate code; filed only on client approval; manager review kept and timed. (3) Bookkeeping for clients with bank feeds (A2, A1), then accounts preparation in shadow (D2 with D6): tie-outs; blind-first sampled review.
- **Growth.** The new quarterly reporting duty (F4) is the capacity sale, owned by a named partner.
- **People.** Four managers become workflow owners. Bookkeepers write client rules and handle exceptions. Seven trainees are kept. Nobody is made redundant.
- **Left alone.** Sign-off; the reporting officer's decisions; structuring advice; anything inside HMRC's web pages; the pay run (a wrong payment is hard to recall).
- **Not happened by month twelve.** A margin gain; a full statutory cycle on the new cases; corporation tax and self-assessment preparation.

#### Firm B in year one

- **Diligence turns on one question:** do the block and lettings systems accept outside writes? If not, most of the plan falls back to assistance and the price should say so. Lettings is priced as demand-limited.
- **First hundred days.** Weekly agenda per manager; weekly P&L and cash. Spend limits per client collected into one place: they exist only in management agreements. If RICS-regulated: the AI register, quarterly risk register and client notice. Rehearsal on post and email routing, with a check for legal notices (J18).
- **Three cases.** (1) The repair, from report to paid invoice (B1, B2, B4, A6, with I13 and I14; about 11,000 hours across the lines): escalations classed by cause; orders within the client's limit; photo or resident confirmation before close; no invoice paid on an unclosed job; dip samples of triage. Payment release stays under dual authorisation. (2) The money month (A4, A5): reconciliations prepared; a principal reviews, as the rule requires. (3) Sales packs (G5): priced per pack, so a saving is kept.
- **Growth.** Units per property manager on the block side, with boards retained. No target until a baseline exists.
- **2027.** Leasehold reform changes at least eight lines and ends recovery of insurance commission. The head owns that plan. It matters more to the P&L than any agent.
- **Left alone.** Client-money control; site visits, viewings and emergencies; boards and landlords; section 20 notices (year two, under step approval).
- **Where the design fits worse than at A.** Less of the work is reachable; state lives in calls and on site; margins look thinner; and on the demand-limited lettings side the job pledge and the economics pull apart.

### How the design changes by type of business (Q8)

| Dimension | What it changes | Strength of basis |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Licence and ownership rules** | Legal form; who signs; whether the firm can be owned at all. Where ownership is barred (audit; German law), only a service or mandate route exists | Rules. Strong |
| **Pricing basis and who pays** | How long a saving is kept. Hourly passes it on; fixed holds it to renewal; payer-is-not-buyer (block management) delays the question further | Filings and analysts. Fair |
| **How checkable the outcome is, and how soon** | The control: sampling where an outside check exists (a bank balance, a registrar); step approval where it does not; leave with people where nothing can check | Both inventories; rules. Fair |
| **Capacity- or demand-constrained** | Whether to buy at all, and whether the job pledge is affordable | One rival buyer's hypothesis. Weak, but it decides the return |
| **How alike the work and systems are from firm to firm** | How much centre to build; whether a platform or a mandate pays | Added by this run. No evidence; the firm-two test |
| Physical work | Caps the share of hours any system can reach | Inventory estimates |
| Third-party money (client accounts) | Narrows what may be handed over to preparation only | Rules |
| Small-business or enterprise clients | A screen: enterprise buyers take the saving and can insource | Filings; one client's account |
| Country | Employment and ownership law only | Rules |
| Global or local; advice or coordination; commodity or high-touch | Little by themselves. They show up through the rows above: checkability, physical work, pricing basis and payer | No evidence either way |

**What drives the design.** Four dimensions drive the design of a firm: licence and ownership, pricing basis and payer, checkability, and the capacity-or-demand constraint. A fifth, likeness of work and systems between firms, drives the design of the group. Global against local, advice against coordination, and commodity against high-touch make little difference once those five are known.

## Roadmap or experiments

Five tests, in order. The first can be run before buying anything. Tests 2 to 4 run inside one firm in about ninety days each. For every test, write the pass threshold down before starting. I have not supplied thresholds, because nothing in the evidence supports a number.

| # | Test | Do first | What would need to be true | Result that justifies going on |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | **The diligence week at a real firm.** Replace the model inventory with a real one | Take one firm's practice-management or property-system export. Build the light inventory. Count exceptions by cause on three cases. Re-perform a sample of past files for an error rate. Time today's reviews. Get the system vendor's answer on the write path and the insurer's and professional body's answers on AI and sampling, in writing | A willing firm; an export; about a week on the floor | At least three cases with no hard gate failed; a write path exists; the insurer's answer does not price the plan out. If not, change trade or target |
| 2 | **The check and deference test.** Does a designed check cut review time without letting errors through? | On one case (VAT returns at firm A; supplier invoices or repair close-out at firm B) build the tie-outs and the blind-first review screen. Run in shadow against the firm's own output. Seed known-error files. Measure review minutes per unit, errors found by blind re-performance, seeded-error catch rate, and reviewer hours per approved playbook change. Test each reviewer, trainees included, on known-answer files | A write path; past files as a test set; two or three reviewers; one engineer | Review minutes per unit fall; sampled error rate does not rise; seeded errors are caught at least as often as before. If review time does not fall, stop: this is the weakest premise |
| 3 | **Step approval against limits, on the same work.** The library's open dispute | On one reversible back-office line (payment reminders at A; post and email routing at B), run two arms for the same period: a person approves each act; or written limits with a random sample reviewed. Measure errors, review minutes, elapsed time and the reviewers' own account | Volume enough to split; a line where an error is cheap | A measured difference either way. It sets the sample rate and tells the insurer something no source has |
| 4 | **Capacity to P&L.** Do freed hours get sold? | On the records chase (A) or the repair case (B), write the three lines before go-live: hours freed and in which role; their named use and owner; the P&L line and date. Review at ninety days. Alongside: pay bookkeepers or property managers credited time to write client rules, and see whether usable rules are written and whether the same corrections recur | A capacity-short firm; a partner or manager who owns the selling | The lines are met: new clients or units taken on without the hire. If hours are freed and not sold, stop new handovers and fix the selling first |
| 5 | **Portability to a second firm, in shadow.** The hinge between a thin centre and a platform, and a probe of the mandate | With a friendly unowned firm in the same trade, agree a paid baseline month. Run firm one's check and playbook on its past files, with consent. Measure what ran without rewriting, and the engineer-weeks and practitioner-weeks to first accepted units. Ask what it would pay per unit | Test 2 passed at firm one; a second firm on a known suite; legal advice on staff-transfer rules and on use of the second firm's client files | Most of the check and playbook structure carries, at a clearly lower cost: build the shared library and consider mandates. If little carries: stay thin and say so |

What these tests cannot settle in ninety days: whether clients leave when told; whether the loop lasts; what a full annual cycle shows. Those need the first firm's first eighteen months.

## Challenges and open questions

### Where the three rival designs disagreed, and which way I went

| Disagreement | Platform | One-firm | Mandate | This design, and why |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| How much centre | Eight or nine at one firm; strong | Three; capped | About five | Thin, then decided by the firm-two test. The reuse premise is unmeasured |
| Can the centre order? | Yes: platform, gate, calendar | Only a short list | Cannot order a mandate firm at all | The method is mandated; content is local. Autonomy precedents did not need to change the work |
| Equity | Group shares only | A minority in the seller's own firm | Both | Local for heads and signatories; group units for workflow owners and authors. Liability and clients are local |
| Systems of record | Converge per trade | Keep | Keep; decline closed systems | Keep. Migration risks what was bought; evidence is two opposed self-descriptions |
| Where playbooks live | Central library | The firm's property | Central, with local rule files | The firm's, until made shared through a gate and accepted locally |
| Back office | Central from month one | Local | Rented; not offered to others | Local, except finance reporting |
| Charging | Unit price from the start | None | Unit price to all | Group-borne in year one; at cost after; unit price only if the test passes |
| Central selling | A growth lead | None | Partners sell to peers | None centrally, on the vets evidence |
| Vehicle | Buy and converge | Buy and leave | Buy one, serve many | Buy one at a time; mandate as experiment 5 |

**Why not the mandate as the vehicle.** Its own author names the weakest point: "It may be outsourcing with a better story." Listed outsourcers earn 10 to 15%. The gain is made in the review, and under a mandate the review belongs to someone else, with both sides rewarded for a reviewer who nods. It has no authority in the served firm. And whether UK staff-transfer rules would move staff to the provider was not checked. Its real merits are kept: little capital at risk, a per-launch cost code, and diligence from inside.

### Where the parts strain against each other

- **The job pledge and the economics.** The pledge is affordable only if capacity is sold. In a demand-limited firm the two pull apart. The screen for capacity-short firms carries this, on one rival buyer's hypothesis and a few vendor surveys.
- **Local equity and shared work.** A head paid from their own firm's profit has little reason to lend an owner's time to another firm. The reuse award answers for authors, not for heads. If the test passes and sharing matters, heads may need a group element too.
- **Signatory liability and shared playbooks.** A signatory answers for work made by a playbook written elsewhere. Local acceptance and the stop right are the answer. No insurer has been asked whether that is enough.
- **Fast review and deference.** Review time is a measure the firm wants low. Low review time is also how a nodding reviewer looks. Seeded errors and blind re-performance are the counterweight. Untested.
- **The mandated method and autonomy.** A competent, unwilling head can stall a firm for a year or two before the centre acts. Constellation accepts the same cost.
- **The centre and its pay.** It does not pay for itself at one firm, and is tight at five firms of the reference size.

### Open questions, in order of how much the answer would change the design

1. Ben's capital, horizon and access. Not recorded.
2. What a UK insurer will write, ask and charge for AI-prepared work under sampled review, for accountants and for property managers.
3. Whether review time falls (experiment 2).
4. Whether small UK clients leave or ask for lower fees when told. No evidence.
5. Whether client work may lawfully improve a firm's own playbooks, and another firm's. All rows are by analogy.
6. Whether the main block-management and lettings systems accept outside writes.
7. Seller terms and multiples in UK deals; any pay design for captured know-how.
8. Client and staff loss after a sale. One lead of 8.4% against 3.2% client turnover at sold US advice firms was seen in a search summary only and is unverified (R-WEB-COMMERCIAL-29).
9. A sound failure rate for roll-ups and for improvement programmes. None exists.
10. Whether statutory regulation of managing agents is coming. Not searched.
