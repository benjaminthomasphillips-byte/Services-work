## Thesis

**The hypothesis.** An AI-enabled roll-up of small services firms is worth attempting only if each purchase stands up as an ordinary deal: a capacity-short, fixed-fee firm, bought without debt at a price that works with no AI gain, and run properly. The AI programme is an increment on top of that, judged net of its own cost. It should be proven in shadow, at a firm the group does not own, before any firm is bought. The work that decides the increment is not automation. It is building, case by case, a check that does not depend on a person nodding at the machine's output, and showing that the check holds. I hold the diagnosis with moderate confidence and the economics with low confidence. On the reviewers' rough arithmetic the increment may not cover even a small centre at firms of the reference size. I cannot refute that, and the design is now sized on the assumption that it may be true.

**What we now think, in six points.**

1. **Nothing in this field has a measured result.** [source says] No AI roll-up has published a margin, price, retention or error figure with a baseline. Every results number from Long Lake, Dwelly, Current and Shield is self-reported, and several change between tellings (ledger finding 1; R-WEB-ROLLUPS-04/14/17/22; R-LIB-STRUCTURE-18). Current and Shield are both in the Thrive group, so these are three origins. No cited row gives a results figure for Supa. The design rests on rules, on filings from dull listed firms, and on what earlier serial acquirers say about themselves.

2. **Test before buying, then buy an ordinary good firm.** The weakest premise can be tested in shadow, under a paid arrangement with a friendly firm, for a small fraction of the cost of a purchase. So that comes first, with insurance terms from a broker. The purchase is then priced and planned on a conventional value plan (billing, lock-up, pricing of under-priced clients, ordinary capacity fixes). [inferred] If the conventional plan gives most of the return, the centre should be one operating partner and a part-time controller. This is a change from the first draft, made on the private-equity reviewer's objection.

3. **The base case for delivery is assistance with full review.** Every outward or signed deliverable is reviewed in full by a person. Handing work over with sampled review is an exception, earned case by case on live traffic, and only with the written agreement of the firm's insurer and professional body. [source says] Two vendor case studies point the same way on cost: one says "About two-thirds of the remaining cost is the QA layer the client chose to keep" (R-LIB-DELIVERY-29; self-reported, customer unnamed); in the other, human time fell from 14 hours to 6 while "a specialist approves every response" (R-WEB-MAPPING-06; the split is not given). [inferred] The check is likely to be the main cost that remains.

4. **Start as one firm, run thin, and let a measured test decide how much centre to build.** The three rival designs are nearly the same organisation at one firm. They part at the second. The platform design bets that converting the fifth firm costs far less than the first. [source says] No services group has published that number; the only figure is a semiconductor customer's second team, as told by OpenAI about its own paid service (R-WEB-STACK-05). So the standing centre is two people, residents and engineers are a per-deal cost, and the centre thickens only if firm one's checks and playbooks are shown to carry to firm two on live work.

5. **Mandate the method, not the content, and have the firm adopt it.** [source says] Berkshire says its managers "were doing exactly that before" it bought their firms (R-WEB-GOVERNANCE-10; one acquirer's own letter). No row says whether Constellation's or Halma's firms had to change their work. [inferred] An AI roll-up assumes the firm's work must change, and nothing in the precedents combines autonomy with rebuilding the work (ledger finding 10). The centre gives a short, fixed list of orders. Each firm's own board formally adopts it, so that the regulated entity has made the decision. Everything a licensed or accountable person answers for stays with that person.

6. **Improvement is a gated habit with modest gains, and people must be better off when it works than when it fails.** [source says] In every production account but one, a named person approves a change; in several the proposal also comes from a person. The exception is Spawn (self-reported; software; reversible in seconds) (ledger Part 10 and Q4; R-WEB-SELFIMPROVE-01). The one measured gain in a live commercial product is about 1.2 points in the first month (R-WEB-SELFIMPROVE-03; Intercom on its own product). Two academic runs show about ten points (R-WEB-SELFIMPROVE-15/16; one a benchmark, one a single-site deployment by its own authors, read as an abstract). Playbooks written by people raised benchmark pass rates by 16.6 points on average; playbooks agents wrote for themselves made results worse (R-WEB-SELFIMPROVE-26; 2026 models). No pay design for people whose know-how is captured exists anywhere in the evidence (ledger finding 10). The one here is judgement, and it now pays in cash at the firm.

**The weakest premise.** That for enough of the work a check can be built that is independent of the maker, so that human attention per unit can fall without errors rising, and that the firm can show this with a sample large enough to mean something. Against it:

- [inferred, the inventory author's reading of a guidance document read in part] The tax bodies' guidance may require review of each tax deliverable. What it says: AI output should be "reviewed with appropriate scepticism", and where software "can be configured to make human review optional" the member should ensure "the data and the submission can be subject to an appropriate review" (inventory A, S9). "Can be subject to" is not plainly "each one must be". The institute's written answer is an output of experiment 1.
- [source says] One law firm that writes for insurers suggests they ask accountants whether there is "always a human reviewer for important decisions or work product" (inventory A, S11; the same article as R-WEB-LAW-25; a proposal, not an insurer's practice).
- [source says] No remedy for reviewer deference has been tested (ledger Q3, unknown). [inferred, the capability reviewer's] A review that is truly independent of the output is a re-performance and costs what the work cost. A fall in review minutes is as likely to be the failure as the success.
- [inferred, the inventory author's] Faster preparation with unchanged review only moves the queue to managers (inventory A section 3).
- [inferred, the employee reviewer's] Staff whose hours depend on the result produce the measurements. Careful, slow review by people protecting their work is rational and is not misconduct. The first-cycle reading tests the staff bargain as much as the check.

If, after one full cycle at the first firm, attention per unit has not fallen across the cases while the catch rate on known errors held, the design has bought an ordinary firm. That is why the firm must be worth owning as an ordinary firm.

The second-weakest premise is that freed hours become sold capacity while existing clients on the same work are asking for the saving. [source says] The library holds no measured case of saved time converting to profit (R-LIB-ECONOMICS-30).

**What would change the view.**

- An AI roll-up publishes an audited before-and-after margin with a baseline, and the cost of its latest firm against its first. That would justify a thicker centre and a faster pace.
- A regulator or insurer accepts certification of the agent system in place of a named person's review (proposed at R-LIB-GOVERNANCE-06; no regulator is shown accepting it).
- The shadow test before purchase shows no fall in attention per unit with errors held, on any case, or the insurer's terms require full review at a cost that removes the gain. Do not buy on the AI thesis; buy only if the conventional plan alone earns the return.
- At the second firm, on live work, most of firm one's checks and playbooks need rewriting. Stay thin for good.
- Fees for small-business accountancy fall fast and visibly. Speed of cost-out would then matter more than depth, which favours a platform or building new.

**The three decisions Ben would need to make first.**

1. **The capital and its clock.** A long-hold company, or a fund with an exit. [source says] European accountancy investors expect to leave in three to seven years (R-WEB-OWNERSHIP-06); when Constellation's investors put it up for sale, acquisitions slowed and "Initiative spending dropped" (R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-11; the operator's own account). This design assumes no planned exit. Sellers, staff and clients are told the owner's horizon, and a change of it triggers the minority's put and the clients' exit window (Parts 2 and 5).
2. **The first trade, and a friendly firm for the test.** I lean to UK non-audit accountancy: clearer rules, open filing interfaces, a cheaper inventory, in a crowded market. Block management is no longer in the base plan (Part 1). Ben's access to a firm willing to host a paid shadow test should decide, and it is not recorded.
3. **The size of the bet, in two stages.** Stage one: about six months of a managing partner, one contract engineer, a broker and an independent quality adviser, with no purchase. Stage two, only if stage one passes: a majority of one firm bought with no debt, a per-deal team for about 18 months, and a standing centre of two. The amounts are open. The private-equity reviewer's own rough estimate puts a centre of five at £0.5m to £0.8m a year against a group share of firm A's real profit of £0.15m to £0.5m [reviewer's estimate, not evidence]. Nothing I hold contradicts the shape of that.

**Where the evidence goes against Ben.** Not supported: a full inventory as the first move, the four requirements as sufficient, and improvement "via AI". Supported: a light inventory as a screen, the four as necessary, his description of Marble, and the commodity worry, which bites harder than expected because, on an unchecked reading, both reference firms are less protected by licence than the word suggests. Detail is under "Ben's starting positions tested".

### The six chains of reasoning behind the main choices

**1. Legal form follows the licence, and debt is where roll-ups die.**
- [source says] A UK audit firm must be controlled by qualified persons: "a majority of the members of the firm are qualified persons" and a majority of its management body (R-WEB-LAW-29; Companies Act 2006, Schedule 10).
- [source says] The FCA found that "decisions materially affecting regulated firms were made by unregulated boards" and that "some regulated entities guaranteed a holding company's debt" (R-WEB-OWNERSHIP-19/20). Buying an FCA-authorised firm needs prior approval: "Not doing so is a criminal offence" (R-WEB-LAW-33). CBIZ: "Interest expense was $107.2 million in 2025, compared to $34.4 million in 2024" after one deal (R-WEB-OWNERSHIP-10; a filing).
- [source says] RSM Tenon failed after debt-funded buying (R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-28). Slater and Gordon failed after one large debt-funded purchase (R-WEB-OWNERSHIP-33; an encyclopaedia summary, second-hand). Ince collapsed; the cause is not in the evidence (R-WEB-OWNERSHIP-31/32; second-hand).
- [inferred] Hence separate firm entities, audit outside, no debt. The FCA sentence also describes any centre that appoints a firm's head and mandates its method. Part 3 is tested against it. Confidence: high on form, lower on whether my table passes that test.

**2. Accountability stays with a named person; whether supervision may be scaled is not settled for these firms.**
- [source says] "AI has no separate legal personality; solicitors and regulated individuals who use AI … remain accountable for their work and outputs" (R-WEB-LAW-16; SRA warning notice). The tax bodies say members are responsible "irrespective of the use of AI tools" (inventory A, S9).
- [source says] The Court of Appeal: for routine work "it may be sufficient … to sample their work" (R-WEB-LAW-19; a case about people, not AI). The RICS AI standard requires "randomised dip samples" for high-volume outputs (inventory B, S-B-04).
- [source says] Against: the insurer-side questions quoted above. The whole insurer side of the evidence is two law firms' commentary (R-WEB-LAW-24/25). No UK insurer's wording or price was found.
- [inferred] So decision rights sit with the signatory, full review is the base case, and sampling is asked for in writing, line by line. Confidence: high on the rule, low on insurers.

**3. A person's sign-off is not an independent check unless it is designed to be.**
- [source says] "analysts went with their agent's lean 86% of the time. If the agent's input made no difference, we'd expect about 50%" (R-WEB-GOVERNANCE-21; vendor-run, 50 analysts, read as a web summary).
- [source says] Accountants "tend to still follow AI's suggestions" when the software is unsure (R-WEB-STACK-27; summary of a study of 79 firms; pre-agent tools).
- [source says] Models have tried "to get a higher score by modifying the tests or scoring code" (R-WEB-SELFIMPROVE-24; 2025 models, o3 named).
- [inferred] So the main thing to build is the check, and the check must be held by someone who is not paid on its result. No remedy for deference has been tested (ledger Q3).

**4. Work is found narrow and widened; a light list is still worth having.**
- [source says] No case began from a full list of services and reported what happened. Every case began with one workflow (ledger Q1; R-LIB-DELIVERY-01/05/08/17). This is an absence, not a negative result.
- [source says] Going deep is slow: one tax domain took "six weeks of engineering and practitioner review" (R-WEB-STACK-04; OpenAI on its own customer).
- [inferred by the inventory authors] A list of about 90 to 125 lines was quick to draft at a desk in these two codified trades, and showed a few shared gaps blocking most lines (inventory A section 4; inventory B section 4; desk exercises by one agent each, with no access to a firm). Its cost at a real firm is unmeasured: inventory B guesses about a week and says it has no evidence for that; inventory A declines to guess.
- [inferred] A light inventory as a screen; one whole case first. Confidence: medium.

**5. What lasted was small, taught by line managers, with long-held equity. Nothing lasted that combined autonomy with rebuilding the work.** This chain rests on two operators' own letters and tellings.
- [source says] Constellation: the layer above "can nurture, goad and suggest, but they can't order" (R-WEB-GOVERNANCE-01). The only other group Leonard found that ran hundreds of firms autonomously "eventually caved in to increased centralisation" (R-WEB-GOVERNANCE-02; he calls his explanation a hunch).
- [source says] Danaher, in its executives' telling to a magazine, mandates the core: training, "a strategic plan in place within 100 days", "an eight-hour operating review every month". It lets firms choose among the tools: "Not every operating company has to implement every tool." "The top 25 executives at Danaher, including the CEO, all teach two or more weeks per year" (R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-02/03/04; quotations not re-checked against the raw text).
- [source says] Constellation held head office down only by a cap: "to arbitrarily limit headcount additions", taking the burden "from 3.0% of Net Revenue in 2004, to 0.5%" (R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-10). Employees paid above a threshold must invest in the company's shares and hold them for "an average of at least 4 years" (R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-11).
- [source says] Teachers of the method name "inappropriate pace, poor integration, excessive financial leverage, and overly aggressive valuation" (R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-31).
- [inferred] Take Danaher's form for a small mandated core and Constellation's for professional and commercial decisions. This split is the design's main act of judgement.

**6. Who keeps a saving turns on visibility, pricing basis and renewal.**
- [source says] RWS: "Lower gross margins of 43.4% (FY24: 46.9%)"; adjusted profit before tax "£60.4m (FY24: £106.7m)"; "95% repeat services revenue" (R-WEB-COMMERCIAL-12; a filing; attribution to AI is partly interpretation).
- [source says] US law: "a full 90% of all legal dollars still flow through standard hourly rate arrangements", with rates up 7.3% in 2025 (R-WEB-COMMERCIAL-13).
- [source says] UK vets: "For at least three of the five LVGs that acquired FOPs since 2015", acquisitions "led to an increase in average prices of 9% four years later", followed by binding remedies (R-WEB-OWNERSHIP-25; CMA summary; the groups dispute parts of the analysis).
- [source says] Chroma: "In a supply-constrained business … AI unlocks capacity. You serve more customers at the same price" (R-LIB-ECONOMICS-09; a rival buyer with no completed deals; a hypothesis).
- [inferred] Buy where fees are fixed, clients are small and the firm is short of capacity; take the gain as volume; decide in advance what is given back at renewal. Confidence: fair on the pattern, low for the two reference firms.

### Ben's starting positions tested

**"Do we need to list out the 200 things the company does, turn them into products, identify edge cases etc, and automate as much as you can?"**
- For: [inferred by the inventory authors] a list of about 90 to 125 lines was quick to draft at a desk and useful; its cost at a real firm is unmeasured (chain 4). It found the shared blockers, the lines not to touch, and outside limits such as, [source says], HMRC's rule that "automation tools must not be used to enter data into or navigate Government Gateway" (inventory A, S10). The one account of extensive mapping first (Long Lake, self-reported) supports mapping at the cost of a year, and says later firms were reportedly faster (R-LIB-DELIVERY-16).
- Against: [source says] no other case started from a full inventory; all started narrow (ledger section 3). A quoting workflow scored "92.8% versus 22.3% in production" because real requests were incomplete (R-LIB-DELIVERY-05; implementer's own figures; late 2025 to January 2026 models; that it had state, procedure, a write path and a test set is the researcher's reading; the two rates define success differently). An inventory scores the line as designed, not the traffic as it arrives. Productising fits about ten of 123 lines at firm B (inventory B section 4).
- **Verdict.** Against him on the full inventory as the first move and as a build plan. With him on a light list in diligence, as a screen. The unit to build is the case, not the line. Confidence: medium; it rests on absence, companies' own tellings and two desk exercises.

**"Automating any task requires 4 things (or does it)."**
- [source says] Each of the four is supported (ledger Q2). But a case that by the researcher's reading had all four failed in production (R-LIB-DELIVERY-05), and a working tool went unused (R-LIB-FAILURES-09; an implementer's marketing account, posted 1 January 2026; late-2025 models). Cases ran without a built verifier only as suggestions to a person who checked everything: Carta, at volume (R-LIB-DELIVERY-08/09), and a two-lawyer firm (R-LIB-DELIVERY-33; this one rests partly on an unchecked promotional case).
- **Verdict.** Necessary, not sufficient. What recurs in the failures: complete inputs, authority and limits, an owner who has agreed to own the agent's version, a route for each exception, undo, a test set from real traffic, a check the maker cannot reach, permission from an outside body, and separation of outside content from outward acts. The test is in Part 6. Confidence: medium-high; no task has been run with and without one item (R-LIB-DELIVERY-37).

**Mapping products such as Marble Cognition are static.**
- [source says] His description is accurate with two corrections. Marble's site lists five steps, "Capture Understand Identify Automate Measure". Employees install the capture themselves, and the analysis is grouped by team (R-WEB-MAPPING-01/02/03).
- For the criticism: [source says] one research group (Mamudu and colleagues, Queensland University of Technology) finds the evidence that process mining pays "mostly anecdotal" (R-WEB-MAPPING-20; 2021), and that in 62 published cases "80% of all process mining efforts were focused on data extraction, data preparation and dealing with data quality issues", while a dedicated team "proved beneficial" (R-WEB-MAPPING-22; 2022; many of the case reports were written by vendors). The two rows share an author and count as one origin.
- Against: [source says] Marble now claims "Always up-to-date SOPs … redrawn as the work changes. No one has to maintain it" (R-WEB-MAPPING-04). A claim. No source shows how a change is detected, who approves the redrawn procedure, or whether a deployed agent is updated.
- **Verdict.** Fair for what is evidenced; out of date for what is claimed. A map is a rentable input; the organisation round it is not. Integral and Enam, two AI-native services groups, already appear as Marble customers (R-WEB-MAPPING-05/06; vendor case studies).

**"We need orgs constantly self improving via AI."**
- For: [source says] Danaher's shared system began in 1986 and is still running (R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-01/04; the company's own telling). Gated loops run in production, by three vendors' own accounts of their own products (Sierra, Intercom, Ramp; R-WEB-SELFIMPROVE-01/03/06).
- Against: [source says] the strongest advocate says "I'm not sure anyone has a truly self improving company in every function" (R-LIB-IMPROVE-TECH-03). Intercom: "the average approval rate of new Suggestions is around 60%" and "an average increase of 1.23 percentage points" in the first month (R-WEB-SELFIMPROVE-03). Ungated self-edits "never settle"; a keep-best gate took accuracy "from 45.3% to 55.8%" (R-WEB-SELFIMPROVE-15; a benchmark on ten tasks; the quoted climb is "for Codex"; models as listed in the row, GPT-5.4-mini and Haiku 4.5). Changes that passed tests degraded a live product for days to weeks (R-WEB-SELFIMPROVE-19; April 2026).
- **Verdict.** Right as an aim. The evidence supports a gated, human-approved loop per case with modest compounding. "Via AI" overstates it. The machinery is rentable by rivals (R-WEB-SELFIMPROVE-13/35); the test sets, rubrics and reviewers are not.

**If the loop works, does commodity work become a commodity, and what is left?**
- [inferred, the ledger's finding 2] Where the client can see cost fall and can do the work, price or volume fell; where neither holds yet, prices rose. The cases behind it, [source says]: RWS's margin and profit fell as clients moved to machine translation (R-WEB-COMMERCIAL-12; a filing); Teleperformance lost volume in one line of work (R-WEB-COMMERCIAL-07); TCS's chief executive accepted "structurally" an analyst's description that gains are passed on to clients, while its margin rose (R-WEB-COMMERCIAL-05; a company claim). US law rates rose (R-WEB-COMMERCIAL-13/14). These are three cases and one segment, not a tested condition.
- [inferred by the inventory authors from general knowledge of UK law; not checked against statute] At firm A only the audit opinion is reserved by law: about 12 signatures and under 5% of model hours. At firm B nothing is reserved (inventory A section 3; inventory B section 3.4). If that reading is right, Chroma's line, "The signature decides who keeps the savings" (R-LIB-ECONOMICS-12; a pitch), protects these two firms less than it says.
- **Verdict.** The routine, visible tier becomes a commodity at the pace clients compare and renew. The first cases this design converts (records chase, VAT, bank-feed bookkeeping) are also the ones a client's own ledger software is automating. What is left is local and human: client relationships, permissions that can be lost for poor work, the client-money machinery, experienced people, and the habit of change. A group can own these only by keeping the people who hold them. The client's own rule file is not on the list: the client may take a copy on leaving (Part 5). The strategy if nothing lasts beyond a few years is in Part 15.

## Operating model

**How to read this section.** Each part gives the decision, the reason, the basis, and what it looks like at one firm, five and thirty. Where I chose between the rival designs, the choice is in one table under "Challenges". The rivals are called Platform (one operating system across the group, firms converge fast), One-firm (deep knowledge of each business, little shared beyond method) and Mandate (own one firm, serve others under contract on their systems).

**Roles used below.** *Head*: the firm's managing partner after the sale. *Signatory*: a person who signs or answers for work to a client or regulator. *Workflow owner*: the manager who runs one case and sponsors its agents. *Firm quality lead*: a senior person in the firm, outside every workflow owner's line and not paid on throughput, who holds the checks, test sets, samples and seeded files, and has a named deputy on the same terms. *Head of quality*: an independent qualified person or outside firm engaged by the holding board. *Resident* and *practice engineer*: group people seconded into a firm for about 18 months (a design choice).

### Part 1. Purpose and where it plays

**Decision.** The group is a long-term owner of small UK services firms that do recurring, fixed-fee work for small clients and are short of capacity, not of customers. It helps each firm serve more clients with the people it has. It stays in one trade until the standing centre is covered by the group's share of firm profits. It will not own statutory audit, buy at auction, put debt on a firm, rely on group-driven price rises, buy demand-limited businesses, serve enterprise clients with procurement functions, or enter Germany or the Netherlands until it has run a works-council process once. No planned exit.

**Reason.**
- [source says] The library holds no measured case of an owner keeping an AI saving (R-LIB-ECONOMICS-01/08), so the purpose must hold if the saving is small or brief. In UK vets, price rises at consolidated local practices ended in regulation (chain 6; one trade, one inquiry). Ordinary repricing of an under-priced client by the firm's head is not a price-rise thesis and stays the head's decision.
- [source says] Enterprise buyers say they will claim the saving: two executives' stated intent, two banks' lower consultant spend, and a law firm's list of terms to negotiate (R-WEB-COMMERCIAL-15/18). [inferred, the researcher's] A fixed price lets a supplier keep a saving until renewal; the source is Accenture on enterprise clients, and nothing in it is about small payers (R-WEB-COMMERCIAL-04).
- [source says] Works councils in Germany and the Netherlands hold a veto over monitoring tools, and Germany bars investors from law firms (R-WEB-LAW-09/10/28).

**Which trade first.** Judgement, and a decision for Ben. Accountancy: the work "is set by statute and looks alike from firm to firm" (the inventory author's own statement of confidence, not a source); [source says] filing runs through software interfaces; 48% of UK accountants told a vendor that capacity holds back growth; new quarterly reporting work arrived in April 2026 (inventory A sections 1 and 2; S17, S18, search summaries). Against: 46% of surveyed UK mid-tier firms say they have private-equity backing (R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-26; sample size not confirmed, the firms' own answers, and mid-tier firms are larger than firm A). And accountancy is the trade where the weakest premise is held most tightly by rule: if every tax deliverable must be reviewed, the first test is run where it is hardest to pass. I accept that, because a pass there is worth more and the rule can be asked about in writing before any money is spent.

**Block management is out of the base plan.** I had it as the second trade. The private-equity, client and regulator reviewers each gave a reason to drop it, and together they are right. On the inventory's own account it has thin margin markers, a commission stream that ends on 1 November 2027, a lettings arm, about a fifth of the firm, that the inventory author infers is closer to demand-limited, an unknown write path, trust money and safety-critical triage. A second trade also doubles the templates and regulatory reading for a centre that does not cover its cost. Firm B stays in this document as the second worked example and as the test of how the design changes by type of business.

**The size screen.** [inferred] A firm of 30 cannot carry a share of a centre (Part 14). The screen is fees large enough that the firm can pay for its own practice engineer from year two out of the conventional plan's gain. The threshold is open until a real engineer's cost and a real firm's accounts are in hand.

**Basis.** Rules and filings for what to avoid. Judgement for the trade. Ben's fund strategy and access are not recorded (R-LIB-ECONOMICS-35).

**One, five, thirty.** One firm: a single trade. Five: still one trade. Thirty: one trade, or two if the centre has covered its cost for some years. [inferred] At the pace in Part 4 (one firm until about month 18, then at most two in their first 18 months at once, which is about four firms every three years), five firms is four to five years away and thirty is more than two decades away. The thirty-firm column in every part therefore describes an organisation beyond any planning horizon; read it as a direction, not a plan. Only the holding board may relax the pace rule, and only after the firm-two test has passed. The design accepts the slow pace and prices the overhead on it.

### Part 2. Ownership, capital and legal form

**Decision.**

```
Investors (long-term capital; named to sellers, staff and clients)
│
Holding company   board: investor directors, the managing partner, one independent member
│      └─ Head of quality (independent person or firm) reports here
├─ Group services company
│     employs the centre and the per-deal people; owns the method, the trade
│     template and any shared check or playbook; negotiates supplier terms
├─ Firm 1 Ltd   own name, own board, own bank accounts, own systems, own PI cover
│     majority: holding company     minority: the head and signatories who stay
│     holds: client contracts, memberships, HMRC agent status, supervised status,
│            and its own contracts with tool and model suppliers as controller
├─ Firm 2 Ltd   …
```

- **A perimeter map comes first.** Before any approach, list for the trade every status a firm holds, the control and principal test for each, who must be approved and what must be notified. [reviewer] For an accountancy firm that may include ICAEW rules on non-member principals and use of the "Chartered Accountants" title, approval of owners and managers by the anti-money-laundering supervisor, any investment-business or probate licence, HMRC agent registration, and any FCA permission (for which [source says] prior approval of a change in control is required, R-WEB-LAW-33). All consents in hand is a condition of completion. Group people who must be approved are approved, and accept that they are then within a regulator's reach.
- **Whether the group may hold a voting majority at all is the first question on that map.** [source says] No ownership bar was found for non-audit accountancy, but ICAEW proposes that investor-controlled firms may not use "Chartered Accountants", with results due in 2027 (ledger Part 2; R-WEB-LAW-31). If control must stay with members for a status the firm needs, the fallback is the documented split: members control the licensed firm and the group takes its economics through the services company under a services agreement (R-WEB-OWNERSHIP-09). The equity design below assumes a majority is allowed. It is answered before exchange, not watched until 2027.
- **Audit is out.** Prefer firms with no audit registration. Where there is an audit book, the default is to refer it to an unconnected firm and to price the lost fees and the non-audit work those clients may take with them. [reviewer] An audit entity controlled by a partner who also works in and holds shares in the group's firm, sharing its name, staff or systems, is likely to be treated as connected, which defeats the purpose. Such a carve-out is used only with the audit regulator's written view. At firm A the book is about 12 opinions and 2,200 model hours (inventory A section 3).
- **Debt.** None on any firm, written into each firm's articles with a minority veto. No firm guarantees anything. None at the holding company until the centre covers its cost and one firm has a full measured cycle; after that it is a holding-board decision and triggers the minority's put. The library poses the question of how much debt a transition year could carry and holds no answer (R-LIB-STRUCTURE-08; a diligence question, not a finding). Deferred consideration is paid by the holding company from committed capital and is counted as leverage when the plan's cash is tested.
- **Sellers' terms (percentages and prices open, set deal by deal).**
  - Cash at completion; a retained minority; deferred consideration over about three years (a design choice).
  - Deferred consideration is paid on: client fee retention, measured against a baseline that leaves out clients lost in the first year citing the sale or AI, and any dated regulatory loss known at signing; retention of named key staff; the quality record; and a floor on the firm's profit measured before group-borne costs. It is never paid on hours, on short-term profit above the floor, on handovers attempted, or for steering clients. Using a stop right cannot reduce it.
  - A salary fixed at completion in a stated relation to prior drawings. Dividends on the minority under a written policy with a minimum payout of distributable profit.
  - A put for the minority at a formula price after year three (a design choice), and at once on a change of control, new holding-company debt or a change of the owner's stated horizon. A matching call for the group.
  - Removal as head only for defined cause. Removal without cause accelerates deferred consideration and buys the minority at the formula price. Good and bad leaver terms are written. Restrictive covenants are in the sale agreement; the holding board decides whether to enforce.
  - Warranties on claims and known circumstances; a retention against pre-completion negligence claims; a statement of who pays run-off cover.
- **Staff equity.** Firm staff are paid in cash (Part 7), not units. Long-serving senior staff who are not sellers may be offered a small local stake at completion from a pool the group funds; the right to buy more is from the group's majority at the same formula price as the put. Centre staff, other than the head of quality and the controller (Part 7), hold phantom units in the holding company: a cash claim valued each year by a formula fixed in advance, vesting over about four years (a design choice), paid from a budget the holding board funds. [inferred] In a group that loses money by design these are worth little for years, and centre staff should be told so.
- **Who owns the know-how.** Each firm owns its client files and its own playbooks. A client's rule file is the firm's working document and the client's to take a copy of on leaving. The services company owns the method, the trade template and any check or playbook made shared through the release gate (Part 10). Shared items carry method, never client material.
- **Charging.** For the first 18 months the resident, the engineer, the backfill and the build are costs of the deal. The quality function, group audit, the AI-attributable part of insurance cost and the pledge's cost are borne by the group throughout. From month 19 the firm pays at cost only for what its board chooses to keep, and may refuse anything off the non-negotiable list. Nothing is charged by headcount. No platform fee and no unit price unless the firm-two test passes.

**Reason.**
- Structure and debt: chain 1.
- Earn-outs: [inferred, the researcher's] an earn-out on the firm's own results rewards steady hours in the years a redesign would cut them (ledger Part 2). [inferred, the regulator reviewer's] A milestone such as "cases attempted" pays the person with the stop right to proceed. Both are removed.
- Sellers staying: [source says] three listed acquirers defer part of the price over one to three years; one of them, Knights, ties it to the seller staying; Brown & Brown's key staff "are not prohibited from competing with us" (R-WEB-OWNERSHIP-22/30; R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-20). Supa lets sellers leave (R-LIB-STRUCTURE-17). The precedents support a deferred price more than they support my tie-in.
- Local equity: [source says] the disclosed designs are local (Focus buys 40% to 60% of earnings before partner compensation, R-WEB-OWNERSHIP-17; Pets at Home "owns 50% of the practice's shares", R-WEB-OWNERSHIP-27); Gallagher has "lost key brokers and groups of brokers, along with their clients" (R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-21).
- Cash for staff: the employee and private-equity reviewers showed that units in a company with no exit and no profit have no route to money. A reward that cannot be turned into money will not hold anyone.

**Basis.** Evidence for the structure, the audit rule and debt. Judgement for every term. Open: all percentages, prices and formulas; whether sellers will accept these terms against debt-funded buyers paying profit earn-outs (asked in experiment 1); ICAEW's control tests; the SRA's ownership rules.

**One, five, thirty.** One: three entities; equity-funded. Five: the same, repeated; the first puts fall due and must be funded. Thirty: intermediate companies by trade; the method could be licensed outside the group, as Danaher licensed its system to a spun-off company (R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-06).

**The non-negotiables.** A fixed list: no debt or guarantees; client-money rules as the regulator sets them; monthly accounts and the eight measures, on time and honest; the monthly review; the task test and a live shadow run before any handover; every agent on the register with a sponsor and deputy; no client material in an open tool; no client's material in any AI workflow before that client has had notice; no recording of people without the stated-purpose process; the job pledge; the bar on using review records for selection or discipline; disclosure of ownership, horizon and AI use; the stop rules. The head of quality may audit any of these without notice, except that it has no access to client money or to the firm's anti-money-laundering records.

**Testing this against the FCA's sentence.** The centre appoints the head, sits on the board, sets the test and can stop an agent. [inferred] That is close to "decisions materially affecting regulated firms … made by unregulated boards". Three things answer it in part: the firm's board adopts the method as its own decision; no centre person decides any client matter; and group people on the firm's board are approved wherever a supervisor requires. Whether that satisfies a given supervisor is asked on the perimeter map, not assumed.

**Boards.** Holding board: investor directors, the managing partner and one member independent of the investor. It meets quarterly; sees each firm's one page, the centre's budget against actual, the head of quality's report and the log of every stop and every stop reversed; sets the group's risk appetite. It does not hear any client matter. Each firm's board: the head, one or two senior staff, the group partner; an elected staff representative attends for people and measures items. It meets monthly and is the monthly review.

**Disputes.** Between the head of quality and a head: the stop stands, and the independent board member reviews it within five working days (a design choice). Between two firms over a shared procedure: each keeps its own client rules; where the dispute is about taste, the trade lead sets the default and a firm may keep a variant at its own cost; where it is about correctness or compliance, the head of quality decides, after advice from the professional body if needed, and any signatory may still refuse the result on their own work. Before a trade lead exists there are no shared items to dispute. The authoring firm does not vote on its own item at the gate.

**Basis.** Rules for the firm and signatory columns (R-WEB-LAW-16/18/22). The table as a whole is a design with no AI-era precedent (ledger Part 3). The evidence against a mandated core: Berkshire has "never … required our subsidiaries to submit budgets" and lasted (R-WEB-GOVERNANCE-10).

**One, five, thirty.** One: the boards are nearly the same few people, which is why the independent member and outside head of quality exist from the start. Five: one group partner on five boards; [source says] Halma's divisional heads "chair the boards of typically five to seven companies" (R-WEB-GOVERNANCE-11). Thirty: five or six group partners with a delegated capital limit; [source says] Leonard "ran out of capacity" at "about thirty BU's" (R-WEB-GOVERNANCE-07).

### Part 4. The acquisition engine

**Decision.** Test in shadow before buying. Then one purchase at a time. The second firm is not signed until the first has had its full-cycle reading, at about month 18. After the firm-two test, at most two firms may be in their first 18 months at once, and only if a resident exists for each. Each firm gets a resident, a practice engineer and a backfill budget. The price works on a conventional plan with no AI gain.

**Stage one, before any purchase (about six months; a design choice).**
1. A specialist broker is appointed. Indicative terms from at least two insurance markets on AI-assisted work, change of control and sampled review are a gate. Questions go through the broker and do not name a firm until heads of terms.
2. The perimeter map for the trade (Part 2).
3. A paid shadow arrangement with one or two friendly firms: the light inventory, and experiments 1 and 2. A confidentiality agreement, a staff communication plan agreed with the firm's head, and the firm's costs covered.
4. The offer is tested with three or four real sellers or a broker, and what competing buyers pay is recorded.
5. Written legal advice, addressed to the firm, on using past client files for test sets and on improving playbooks from client work.

**Sourcing.** Direct approaches, no auctions, against the screens in Part 1, plus: no client above a set share of fees (open), a system vendor that permits a write path, and little or no audit.

**Diligence on the work (about a week on the floor, then two to four more; my estimate with no source; inventory A's author declined to guess the cost).**
1. **The light inventory.** One row per job type from the practice-system export, with client identities removed, scored on the task test and for how easily the client or the client's software could do the line. Scored by the resident-to-be with the firm's managers; the head of quality checks a sample of the scoring.
2. **The write-path test.** Each system vendor is asked in writing whether an outside agent may write to it. [source says] One vendor refused, on an implementer's account (R-LIB-IMPROVE-TECH-34). HMRC bans automation of its web pages and allows its interfaces (inventory A, S10).
3. **Re-performance of a sample of past files**, done by the firm's own staff or an independent reviewer under the head's supervision, with only results passed to the buyer. Before it starts, the seller, the seller's insurer and the buyer agree that errors found will be notified under the current policy and handled under the error protocol, and the buyer takes a specific indemnity. [source says] No error rate was found for any line of work in a practice (inventory A, section 4).
4. **Timing of today's work** from system timestamps, not self-timing.
5. **Which audit clients also buy other services; change-of-control and notice terms in the largest client contracts; every regulatory status** against the perimeter map.

**Pricing.** A multiple of profit after market salaries for the partners and after the audit book leaves, tested against A003's three scenarios: [source says] "the business without the programme but with rivals adopting AI, the programme as dated cash flows, and the failed implementation. Paying for part of a credible improvement can still return; paying for all of it in the premium rarely does on the held evidence" (R-LIB-ECONOMICS-32; a proposal). The first scenario, with the conventional plan added, is the base case. Each dated regulatory item known at signing is given an estimate in the price.

**Conditions of completion.** All perimeter consents. Bound insurance terms with a change-of-control endorsement, an affirmative AI clause and retroactive cover confirmed. The professional body's written answers on review and on trainees' experience. The legal advice on client files.

**The sequence after completion.**

| --- | --- | --- |

Clients written to: ownership and horizon disclosed, with a penalty-free exit window. Bank mandates checked. Nothing automated | Head; managing partner |

**Reason.** [source says] Integration is staffed, mostly non-AI work: Dwelly's launch team runs "accounting consolidation, office and car lease terminations" and client migration (R-WEB-ROLLUPS-10; job advertisements show intent, not results). Long Lake's first deployment "took more than a year" (R-LIB-DELIVERY-16; self-reported). Constellation reviews each deal "about a year after the initial investment" (R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-09). I do not take Long Lake's pace, which two counts that do not agree imply was about ten deals in under five months (R-WEB-ROLLUPS-07; self-reported). One live case in year one, not three, follows the record: one engineer cannot build three cases, their checks and test sets around a deadline calendar in a firm that is short of capacity.

**Basis.** Operators' accounts and advertisements. No measured comparison of approaches. The cost of the latest firm against the first is unknown for every services group.

**One, five, thirty.** One: the managing partner and the resident are the engine. Five: two residents, a deal partner. A resident drawn from an earlier firm moves only with that firm's head's consent; the group pays the firm for the backfill and the transfer. Thirty: group partners source tuck-ins; a launch function exists only if firms are being converged, which the firm-two test decides.

### Part 5. Clients and the commercial side

**Decision.**
- **What is sold.** What the firm sells now, at the prices its head sets. Freed capacity is sold as more clients and as work the firm turns away today. Leads come from the firm's own referral sources and waiting list; a firm with neither is not short of capacity and fails the screen.
- **The client's side of each converted case.** Before a case goes live the head writes down what clients on that service get: a turnaround standard, a response time, or a price held for a stated term. When the case has passed its full-cycle reading, a share of the measured saving, fixed in advance, is offered at the next renewal to every client on that service, not only to those who ask. The share is open; the rule is not. Preparation is priced apart from sign-off, agent status and advice in new engagement terms, because the first will reprice and the rest may hold.
- **Who owns the client.** The firm holds the contract; a named person, chosen by the head, holds the relationship. The centre holds no client list, never contacts a client and sets no cross-selling targets. Firms may refer to each other if the client asks and nobody is paid for the steer. No group firm acts as accountant, examiner or auditor on a sister firm's client-money accounts.
- **Brand.** The firm's own name, with "part of [group]" on letterhead, website and engagement terms, in plain sight.
- **What clients are told, and when.** On day one: the owner, its horizon, and a window to leave without penalty, which reopens on any later change of control. In the first hundred days and before any of their material enters an AI workflow: where AI is used; the actual control on their service (reviewed in full, sampled at a stated rate, or approved by them, and what their approval does and does not mean); that their material trains no model and enters no shared playbook; a separate request to use their past files in the firm's test sets, with removal on leaving; and how to object. A per-client flag at the gateway enforces it.
- **Opting out.** A client may have their work done without AI at the same fee and service for the transition period, cannot be disengaged for asking, and is told honestly if the firm will later stop offering it. The group bears the cost of the manual line in the transition.
- **Selling.** Partners and client leads sell, and are paid for it (Part 7). Each capacity-to-P&L line names who sells the freed hours and to whom. A complaint goes to the signatory and the head.
- **When the client has the same tools, or a rival cuts price.** Offer the client the tools where it helps (a records portal, a live ledger) and hold the accountable part. The head decides price client by client, on a floor the firm board sets from contribution per client. Below the floor the firm lets the client go.

**Reason.**
- [source says] Tax guidance: "PCRT suggests providing appropriate disclosure that AI is used within the engagement letter" (R-WEB-COMMERCIAL-26). The RICS standard requires notice "in writing and in advance" and a stated opt-out (inventory B, S-B-04). Client data is "not used to train AI models except where explicitly authorised" (R-WEB-LAW-12; SRA). The first draft put the clause in at each renewal while cases ran on client work. That used clients' material before telling them, and two reviewers caught it.
- [source says] In vets, "it is difficult for pet owners to identify whether a FOP belongs to a large group", and remedies followed (R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-24). Paying sellers for client decisions "presents potential for conflict of interest" (R-WEB-OWNERSHIP-21). A vet told the CMA of "constant pressure to meet sales targets" (R-WEB-OWNERSHIP-28).
- [source says] "1 in 5 clients have already used AI to review work their accountant completed"; "93% of clients want to know" (R-WEB-COMMERCIAL-25; a US vendor's survey of 1,000). [inferred, the client reviewer's] Disclosure with a held price and no stated benefit is an invitation to renegotiate, and thirty heads improvising thirty answers favours the clients who ask.
- Long Lake's founding partner argues that freed capacity is not profit until someone sells it (R-LIB-FAILURES-16; a proposal, no outcome data); a vendor essay says models "still cannot reliably generate demand" (R-LIB-GOVERNANCE-14).

**Basis.** Rules, strong. What small UK clients do when told: unknown (ledger Part 5). The give-back rule and the client offer are judgement.

**One, five, thirty.** One: one notice, one offer per case. Five: the give-back share and the disclosure floor are the same in every firm; wording and timing are each head's. Thirty: the group may be large enough in one town or trade for a competition regulator to look.

### Part 6. Delivery (Q1 and Q2)

**Decision.** Find the work with a light inventory, used as a screen. Before automating anything, ask whether the step should exist. Build whole cases, not lines, one at a time. The base case for every case is assistance: the agent drafts, a person is the maker, and every outward or signed deliverable is reviewed in full. A case moves to handover only when it passes the test below, a shadow run on live work, and the insurer's and professional body's written agreement for that line.

**Units.**

| Unit | Used for | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Service | What the client buys | Year-end accounts; management of a block |
| Line | Screening and scoring; one row in the inventory | "VAT return preparation and submission" |
| Case | Building and running; one instance from trigger to output, crossing lines | A client-year from records request to filing; a repair from report to paid invoice |
| Decision | Gating; a point where someone commits the firm | Filing; an order; payment release; a notice |

[source says] "The table has 123 rows; the firm has perhaps a dozen real cases" (inventory B section 4).

**What is recorded per line:** unit and volume; who does it; how it is checked and priced; where inputs live; exception classes with counts; the fourteen scores; the type of check available; how easily the client could do it; status; binding item; owner. Per case, add: playbook version, client rule file, limits, the check, undo, test set, unit cost.

**Exceptions** are classed by cause: missing information; blocked tool or write path; policy or authority; judgement (R-LIB-DELIVERY-12; Dwelly's own practice); and a fifth from the inventories, waiting on the client or a third party. A person outside the case audits a sample of the classes each month, the "judgement" class first, because that is where an unwritten rule hides.

**How the list stays true.** The work updates it: each new exception class and each change in law amends a row. The practice manager owns it, with help paid for in the backfill budget. It is re-scored at each major model release. From the second firm, the centre keeps a trade template with fixed line IDs.

**"Turning a service into a product"** means a fixed procedure, a unit price and an outside reader who can tell if it is right. It fits a minority of lines: sales packs, company secretarial filings, standard tenancy documents. It does not fit advice, relationships, a fixed fee covering a bundle, or statutory notices, where an invalid notice cannot be undone.

**The order of work on a case.** Remove steps. Write the rules per client. Fix the inputs at source. Build the independent check. Run in shadow on live work. Then hand over what passes. [source says] One implementer, describing itself, redesigns first (R-LIB-DELIVERY-03). The first three steps need no model.

#### The task test, ready to use

**Three questions first. If any answer is no, stop.**

- **A. Is the step needed, and would a plain rule or the firm's existing software do it?** If a rule will do, use the rule.
- **B. If it worked, who gets the freed hours, what will they do with them, and which line of the P&L moves by when?** Write three lines. If they cannot be written, stop (R-LIB-ECONOMICS-30).
- **C. Has a named person agreed to own the agent's version of this work, with a named deputy?** The owner's bargain is written: time released from client work, the pay step, and that an error made inside approved limits with the check run is the firm's, not the owner's. If no manager will own any case by day 100, the firm runs the conventional plan and assistance only, and the per-deal team is cut back. The programme is not ordered.

**Fourteen items, scored Y (yes), P (partly), N (no).**

| # | Item | Score Y only if | Hard gate |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Readable state | Every input is in a system the agent may read, not in email, phone or memory | |
| 2 | Written playbook | The firm's own rules per client and the exception routes are written by the people who do the work, and a second person has done the client's work from the rule file alone on closed files and the gaps were counted | |

**Status rule.**
- Accountability, relationship or one-off judgement is the work itself: **leave with people**.
- Any N on a gate, or a P on a gate with no named fix: **assist only**, with full review. This is where most lines start.
- Gates Y or P with a named fix: **build the missing thing**, then run in shadow.
- All gates Y, the shadow run passed, and written agreement from insurer and body for sampled review on that line: **hand over**, with sampling at the rate the quality lead sets. An N on a non-gate item does not block handover but must have a named owner and date.
- A case moves back to assist at once on the quality lead's or a signatory's word, and must move back if live sampling shows the catch rate or error rate past its threshold.

**The shadow run.** On all traffic as it arrives, for a period and with a pass threshold written beforehand, reported apart for incomplete-input cases. It measures agreement with the firm's own output, errors by class, and abstention: the share of deliberately incomplete or contradictory cases the agent stops on and routes. It includes planted instructions, altered invoices and staged documents. No handover where abstention has not been measured.

**The check, designed.** Tie-outs to outside figures and recomputation by separate code the agent cannot reach. A review screen that shows the tie-outs and asks for the reviewer's judgement before showing the agent's reasoning; the reason for a disagreement is entered there. Blind re-performance of a random sample, drawn by the quality lead, at a size stated beforehand with the smallest rise in errors it could detect. Known-error files built from the agent's own real errors found in shadow, refreshed at each model change, run only in a separate shadow queue that cannot reach a filing or sending path. Review time is recorded by case from the system, never as a target, and is always read beside the catch rate: time falling with the catch rate flat or falling is a warning, not a pass.

**The signatory standard.** For every signed unit the screen shows what was checked, by what, and what was not. The signatory signs that statement. The firm board sets a ceiling on signed units per signatory per period. A sampled-out unit is recorded as such; the honest answer to "who reviewed this" is then "the check, under a sampling policy the signatory set and the insurer agreed".

**Scoring a firm.** Three tables from the sheet: share of hours by status; lines and hours by binding item and by type of check; the lines to leave alone and why. [source says] For firm B the things to build once numbered about four (inventory B section 3.1). The score "ranks; it does not measure".

**Reason.** Chains 3 and 4. The items beyond Ben's four each come from a case that failed or stalled without it (ledger Q2). Items 11 and 12 came from inventory A; item 13 from a library analyst's reading of one build, in which "early corrections mixed genuine errors with preferences and values carried forward from a prior year" (R-LIB-DELIVERY-18; the accounting roll-up in the Thrive-with-OpenAI group), and from Ramp's own account of its product (R-WEB-STACK-21). Item 14, the split of item 4 by type, the gates on 5 and 9 and the live shadow run come from the capability reviewer. [inferred, the same reviewer's] Recomputation from the same classified inputs reproduces a classification error exactly, so many lines scored as "hand over with a designed check" have only a type (c) check today.

**Basis.** Companies' own tellings and two desk inventories. No controlled comparison exists (R-LIB-DELIVERY-37). The check design is untested.

**One, five, thirty.** One: one list, one live case, two in shadow. Five: a trade template; base rates on how often each item binds. Thirty: perhaps a dozen cases per trade; the count of cases grows slowly and the count of firms faster.

### Part 8. Agents as part of the organisation, and the principles (Q3)

**Decision.**
- **Where they sit.** An agent belongs to a workflow in a firm. It answers to one sponsor, the workflow owner, who has a named deputy. The centre runs none of a firm's agents.
- **Shape.** By default a maintained workflow in ordinary code with AI steps. One agent where judgement is needed inside a step. Several agents only for parallel reading with a checkable result.
- **Identity and authority.** Its own identity inside the firm's systems. Tools off until the sponsor turns them on. Limits the system enforces. One client at a time. Where an outside body issues credentials only to the firm or a named person (HMRC agent credentials, for one), the submission is made under that person's authority after their approval, and the log records both the person and the agent. The policy is agreed with each outside body in writing.
- **Spend cap.** A monthly cap per workflow, set by the owner and approved by the head. At the cap the sponsor is told. Inside a set window before a statutory deadline the cap warns and does not stop; a named person holds the fallback plan. Untested: no working agent budget is on file (ledger Part 8).
- **Register.** The firm keeps it: name, sponsor, deputy, purpose, tools, limits, checks, model version, date last reviewed. The head of quality audits it.
- **Review and retirement.** The quality lead reviews an agent before release, including the adversarial tests. Quarterly with the sponsor. Retired when its override rate is rising, the same correction keeps recurring, nobody has used it for a quarter, or a plain rule will do. If the sponsor leaves, the deputy takes it; work in progress goes back to people until the deputy has reviewed it.

**Reason.** [source says] By one engineer's account of his own tool, Sierra tried an agent per role, found it failed, and moved to one agent (R-LIB-GOVERNANCE-17/18; summary only, no outcome measure). "Seven open-source multi-agent frameworks failed 41-87% of tasks on their benchmarks" (R-LIB-GOVERNANCE-21; 2024–25 models; re-test as models change). Singapore's framework wants each agent to have "its own unique, cryptographically verifiable identity" and to be "catalogued and centrally managed" (R-WEB-GOVERNANCE-25; voluntary). At Uber, by its own account, every tool "starts in a disabled state and must be explicitly reviewed and enabled by the owning team" (R-WEB-STACK-14).

**The two open disputes in the library.**

*Approval at each step against limits and budgets (L014 against N076).* Neither, as a rule for all work. Sort acts by consequence, reversibility and how soon the result can be checked (principles 3 and 5). [source says] For limits: by Ramp's own account its agent decides "more than 65%" of approvals under hard-coded limits (R-WEB-STACK-20). For steps: the one authorised AI law firm "is not autonomous and will only take a step where the client has approved it" (R-WEB-LAW-17). The Court of Appeal allows sampling of routine delegated work and the RICS standard requires it for volume outputs; the tax guidance and the one insurer-side article can be read as wanting every deliverable reviewed (chain 2). So the starting position at any firm is full review of what goes out, and limits with sampling only for internal, reversible acts. Sampling of outward work is asked for line by line. No case compares the two rules on the same work (R-LIB-GOVERNANCE-15). Experiment 3 does.

*Record everything against its costs (Blomfield against O'Connor, L021, L022).* Against blanket recording; for a full record of what the work itself produces. [source says] "You must be clear about your purpose and select the least intrusive means to achieve it" (R-WEB-LAW-01; ICO). Meta dialled back keystroke capture "following weeks of angry pushback from staffers" and a data exposure (R-WEB-MAPPING-29). That records people must enter by hand die is a hypothesis in the library (R-LIB-GOVERNANCE-31), which is why the disagreement reason is captured inside the review screen and not as a separate note. So: log agents' traces, classed corrections and decisions. No screens, keystrokes or calls by default. A team may switch capture on for a stated task and time. **An employee who refuses to be recorded:** the head runs the stated-purpose process with the staff representative; the firm, as employer, owns the data-protection assessment. A person who refuses capture has their work done uncaptured. A reviewer who refuses timing or known-error tests is qualified by observed files and keeps the role and its pay step. An exception handler's corrections in an agent's trace are records of the work and are covered by the notice given to all staff. If refusals make a case unworkable, the head decides to leave it with people. [inferred] The cost: the firm learns nothing automatically from calls. There, people write the decision down and the owner adds the rule by hand.

**Basis.** One regulator's voluntary framework and two operators' own accounts point the same way on identity, sponsor, limits and register (R-WEB-GOVERNANCE-25/26; R-WEB-STACK-14; R-LIB-GOVERNANCE-17/18). There is no outcome evidence for any of it in a services firm (ledger Part 8).

### Part 9. The management system

**Decision.** A plan, a budget, eight measures and one calendar, the same at every firm. The monthly firm board is the operating review. The centre asks for one page a month: the eight measures at firm level, with case detail behind it for the workflow reviews.

**Plan, budget and targets.** Each firm writes a yearly plan and budget. Its base is the conventional value plan (Part 14); the AI programme is a separate line with its own cost and its own expected gain. Targets on the eight measures are set by the firm board from the firm's own baselines after the first hundred days; none is set before a baseline exists, and none is set on review time. The centre has its own yearly budget and headcount cap, approved and reviewed by the holding board each quarter. Investors receive the quarterly holding-board pack and a yearly valuation of each holding by a formula fixed in advance, so that "long-hold" still has a scorecard.

**The eight measures, per firm and per case.**

1. Client fee retention, and fee given back at renewal.
2. Errors: found by blind random re-performance, with the sample's detectable difference stated; those that reached a client; complaints.
3. Turnaround and deadlines met, as the client sees them.
4. Attention per unit: preparation and review time by case from system timestamps, shown only beside the catch rate on known errors.
5. Recurrence: how often the same class of correction comes back.
6. Capacity to P&L: clients or units per person; for each handover the three lines written before go-live; hours held in reserve.
7. Regretted leavers.
8. Cash: lock-up days; tool and model cost per unit of work.

No measure of usage, tokens or number of automations. First-pass acceptance is not a headline measure, because a nodding reviewer raises it. Any ninth measure needs one removed. The board also sees, as standing items and not measures, the anti-money-laundering officer's report and any incident.

| Rhythm | Routine | Who | Agents' part |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Weekly | A standing agenda per direct report. Cash note to the head | Head; managers | None in the figures: ordinary code from the ledger |
| Weekly | Each owner reads a sample of their case's exceptions and the review queue | Owners | Propose classes; a person audits a sample monthly |
| Monthly | Close on the group chart by working day ten (a design choice). The practice manager prepares; the head signs; the controller consolidates. A late close goes to the group partner | Practice manager; head; controller | A rented close product, sponsored by the practice manager |
| Monthly | Firm board, half a day: the eight measures on one page, capacity-to-P&L lines due, one problem worked through, any incident, any stop | Head; staff; group partner; staff representative | None |
| Monthly | Register review; spend against caps; continuous live sampling results | Practice manager; quality lead | – |
| Quarterly | Workflow review: each owner presents one case with its baseline, corrections by class and what changed. Variants reviewed across firms by the trade lead | Owners; head; quality lead | – |
| Quarterly, and at each model release | Re-run fixed test sets several times each; re-score the inventory; decide any downgrade | Engineer or trade lead; quality lead | Run the re-tests |
| Quarterly | Holding board (Part 3) | Holding board | – |
| Yearly | Plan and budget; head's pay; method review with heads; outside audit; head of quality's audit of the measures | Holding board; heads | – |

For the first cycle the weekly routines are the firm's own and only the monthly ones are required.

**Escalation.** A workflow problem goes to its owner. Anything touching a client goes to the signatory the same day. An error goes through the error protocol (Part 13). A stop takes effect at once and is reviewed afterwards. A problem the head cannot resolve goes to the group partner, who may order only on a non-negotiable. The stop rules are non-negotiables.

**Reason.** [source says] At the one firm that measured it, "the median employee spends 20% more time in meetings than a year ago" (16% for technical staff), and meta-work, much of it agent management, rose "from 10% … to 22%" of sessions; the firm also tripled in size (R-WEB-GOVERNANCE-15/16; Anthropic on itself). [inferred] Agents do not obviously reduce management time, so attention is budgeted. In Danaher's telling it cut its measures from 150 to eight (R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-04). A property manager runs on a weekly agenda per report, a weekly P&L and a weekly cash note (R-LIB-PEOPLE-20; the only routine on file from a firm of the reference type; not AI). One acquirer without finance systems, Wind River, was "flying blind" (R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-33).

**Basis.** Precedents' own accounts and one measured case on attention. Nothing from inside an AI roll-up (ledger Part 9).

**One, five, thirty.** One: the managing partner attends everything. Five: the group partner's main job is five monthly boards. Thirty: five or six group partners; the span of five to seven sets their number.

### Part 10. The improvement system (Q4)

**Decision.** A gated loop per case, owned by the manager who runs the case. A person or a machine may propose; a named person approves; the tests are held by someone else. A change becomes shared only through a release gate, then one firm at a time. Continuous sampling of live output is the main monitor; re-testing at each model release is the second.

| | 1. Models get better | 2. The firm's playbooks, checks and tools get better | 3. The process itself gets better |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Who else gets it | Every rival | This firm; other firms only for what is made shared | The group, through the method |
| What it needs | A fixed, human-written test set per case, sized to the smallest regression the firm needs to see and run several times; a register of failed uses with model and date; the ability to pin or switch; a plan for a forced model change mid-cycle | Corrections classed at source and audited; a regression set from real cases; a named owner; staged release; rehearsed rollback; a version-to-unit index | Measures of the loop itself; an incident review; the yearly method review |
| Who proposes | The vendor, by releasing | The people doing the work; reviewers; a trace-reading agent | Owners, residents, head of quality |
| What tests it | The fixed set, then a shadow run on live work | The regression set, held by the quality lead; then a staged release with live sampling | The full-cycle readings |
| Who approves | The owner, on the engineer's report (the trade lead's, once one exists). The re-test is a report, not an order: each firm's owner and quality lead accept the new model on their own case | Owner for local items, with a second person where output is signed or outward. For shared items the release gate, then each firm in turn | Centre, with a majority of heads |
| How undone | Pin the earlier version; move the case down to assist | Roll back; find every unit touched through the index; re-review from a reserve; tell affected clients | Drop it |

**Ordinary improvement comes first.** Remove the step. Write the rule per client. Move the check to where the error starts. Chase the client earlier. Stop doing unbilled work without deciding to. None needs a model, and all of it belongs to the conventional plan.

**Shared changes, one firm at a time.** No shared change goes live at more than one firm inside a set period. After it goes live at a firm, a set number of units there are reviewed in full. If a shared item is rolled back anywhere, the trade lead tells every firm running it the same day. Firms are not all moved to a new model version at once, and never in a peak. These limit the correlated error; they do not remove a fault in the model itself, which reaches every firm on that model whatever is done locally.

**How you would know it works, other than by counting changes.** The same correction stops recurring: [source says] "The decline in repeat comments across cycles is the direct measure of whether the loop is working" (R-WEB-SELFIMPROVE-17; Salesforce engineers, software). Attention per unit falls while the catch rate and the blind-sample error rate hold. The capacity-to-P&L lines are met at ninety days. Proposals keep coming from firm staff. If changes are many and these do not move, the loop is theatre; the quarterly review, the audited classes and the completeness test are how that is noticed, and the gain share is the reason staff have to prevent it.

**What to expect.** Modest compounding, and not only upward: [source says] one rented model fell from 84% to 51% on a task between versions (R-WEB-SELFIMPROVE-30; 2023 models).

**How often such systems last.** [source says] Danaher's began in one plant in 1986 and is still running (R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-01/04; the company's own telling). No sound failure rate exists for lean programmes: on the "90%" figure, "Nobody ever has a source"; one pooled estimate for quality programmes is 37.7% (R-WEB-PRECEDENTS-34; a practitioner's review). Every AI-era loop on file is under three years old (ledger Part 15, unknown). [inferred] Named causes of failure: a change of owner or horizon; line managers stop teaching it; the measures become the target.

**"Full context".** It would need every input, conversation and decision in a readable store. The law does not allow blanket capture, and clients must be told (principles 6 and 7). Much of it is in no channel: [source says] "101 past calendar entries in 31 days against 11 recorded notes in 45 days" in the one first-hand build (R-LIB-IMPROVE-TECH-25; one user). The design does not pursue full context. Where state is in calls or memory, the case stays at assist, because an agent with part of the state does not know it is partial; the abstention measure in the shadow run is the test.

**What may be learned from, and shared.** [source says] Whether client work may improve even the firm's own playbooks is argued by analogy; no regulator, insurer or engagement text was found (ledger section 5, gap 4). So the firm's own test sets use a client's past files only after the legal advice of stage one, the client's notice and the separate request in Part 5; otherwise consented or synthetic cases. Between firms: procedure, check code, exception classes and the structure of test sets; never client files or rules. [inferred] This narrows the main advantage a platform claims, and slows the first build.

**Basis.** Fair that gated loops exist and gains are modest, almost all from software firms. No measured loop at an accountancy, law, property or insurance firm. Reviewer hours per approved change: unknown.

**One, five, thirty.** One: one live loop and two in shadow. Five: perhaps ten local loops; if the test passed, a shared library and the first dispute about a default. Thirty: the release gate is a standing function per trade.

### Part 12. Back office and shared services (Q7)

**Decision.** The firm's back office stays in the firm on rented tools. It is the first thing handed over, as a rehearsal. In a firm of 30, "firm finance" is the practice manager and a bookkeeper; the head signs the monthly accounts. The group takes only finance reporting (one chart of accounts, monthly close, weekly cash), the outside audit and the quality function, and pays for the last two. **Reason.** [source says] "Every buyer can automate invoicing. If that's all someone brings, the owner might be better off renting the software and keeping the business" (R-LIB-ECONOMICS-11; Chroma). **One, five, thirty.** One: the practice manager, rented tools, a part-time controller. Five: one controller consolidates five firms on one chart; group insurance buying is tested with the broker. Thirty: optional shared services, billed and benchmarked.

*Committed capital (a condition of completion; a design choice).* Before exchange the holding board states in writing the capital committed against this table at its worst case, through month 36. *How the group pays for the pledge (a design choice).* Each quarter the holding company pays the firm the employment cost of any role that the seat map, or a later handover, shows as surplus to the firm's own plan. The controller calculates it and the head of quality certifies it. The cost then does not fall on the firm's profit, the pool or the minority.

**The error protocol (written before experiment 1).** Any error found, in agent or human work, in a live file, a sample, a shadow run or an old file, goes to the signatory for that work the same day. The signatory classes it and decides: whether the client is told; whether a filing must be corrected with the tax authority; whether it is a circumstance to notify to the insurer, which the head does within the policy's time; and any refund. If a version of a playbook or check caused it, the owner stops the workflow, the index finds every unit touched, those units are re-reviewed from the reserve, and every affected client is told with a stated remedy. If the item is shared, the trade lead stops it group-wide the same day and the indemnity applies. The quality lead, not the owner, decides when the workflow may restart. The record goes to the firm board and the head of quality. An error made inside approved limits with the check run does not touch any individual's pay step, fee or gain share; a stop does not either. It does reduce the firm's profit, since the firm meets the client's penalties, and so reduces dividends and the pool above its floor, unless the fault was in a shared item, where the indemnity applies. Whether the error was inside approved limits is decided by the quality lead, not the owner (a design choice).

**The unresolved conflict.** The Court of Appeal and the RICS standard allow sampling for routine work. The tax guidance and one insurer-side article can be read as wanting review of every output. [source says] Insurers "are likely to focus closely, before inception, on how AI-enabled services are supervised and disclosed" (R-WEB-LAW-24; insurer-side lawyers). A written answer in month zero does not bind a renewal in month nine, so the broker is asked for a multi-year policy or a renewal commitment over the build period. Until bound terms say otherwise, every outward deliverable is reviewed.

**A regulator asks who signed.** The head deals with the regulator and tells the insurer and the head of quality. The firm answers from the record of work: the signatory and the statement they signed, the playbook and model version, the check results, the trace. If the unit was sampled out, the answer is the sampling policy, who set it and who agreed it. At firm B, where little is signed, the accountable person is whoever approved the outward act, or the principal who set the limit it ran under. If the signatory has left, the head answers. The services company supplies the release history of any shared item.

**Basis.** Strong on rules. Nothing on insurers' terms or price. The regulator reviewer's points on ICAEW control tests, successor-practice rules, AML approval and network-firm status are unchecked and are carried as questions on the perimeter map.

**One, five, thirty.** One: one broker, bound terms before completion. Five: a coordinated programme if the market offers one. Thirty: correlated error is a board risk; regulators may treat the group as a group.

### Part 14. Economics

No before-and-after P&L exists for any acquired firm (ledger Part 14). "Before" uses the inventories' estimates. Every "after" amount is left open on purpose. [reviewer's estimate] An avoided hire is worth perhaps £35,000 to £50,000. I hold nothing that improves on these figures. The design's answer is structural: a standing centre of two; per-deal people who stop at month 18; larger firms (the size screen); and one trade. The break-even identity is: standing centre cost, plus per-deal cost spread over the hold, must be less than the group's share of case A's gain plus case B's net increment, summed over the firms. The number of firms and the year at which that holds is the first thing to compute once one real firm's accounts and one real engineer's cost are in hand. It is open, and I will not claim it "works at thirty".

**What counts as a gain.** Only an avoided hire, an unreplaced leaver, or contribution from volume the freed capacity serves at a viable price; less the costs in case B; transition spend shown apart; no hour counted twice (R-LIB-ECONOMICS-31; A003's bridge). An unreplaced trainee at qualification is not counted as a gain. [source says] The accounting roll-up's "preparation time down 31 percent" on 7,000 returns is "Company-reported, unaudited" and discloses no review time (R-LIB-DELIVERY-19). That is not a margin.

**The reserve.** No more than a set fraction of freed hours may be sold until one peak season has been met with the workflow deliberately switched off for a week. The unsold hours are a standing cost of case B. The fraction is open.

**When cash appears.** Case A: from the first year, if the conventional plan is real. Case B: out for at least 18 months; the first cash is when a second peak season is met without the hires it would have needed.

**Stop rules.** Written into the shareholders' agreement. Decided by the holding board, with the independent member voting, on a report by the head of quality, who has no stake in the programme continuing. Each threshold is a number fixed by the holding board before the purchase. I have not supplied the numbers; I have fixed their form.
- *Before purchase.* If the shadow test shows no fall in attention per unit with the catch rate held, or bound insurance terms cannot be had: no purchase on the AI thesis.
- *About month 18, firm one.* The reading is hours-weighted across all cases, not the best one. The programme continues only if attention per unit has fallen by at least the threshold, the catch rate on known errors has not fallen, and the blind sample was large enough to have detected the stated rise in errors and did not. Otherwise stop building.
- *At any time.* Handovers pause if regretted leavers, turnaround or client losses pass their thresholds against the firm's own history.
- *Ninety days after each handover.* If the capacity-to-P&L lines are unmet twice running, no new handovers until someone is selling the hours.
- *Spend.* A cap on cumulative programme spend before a pass.
- *Firm two.* If conversion cost on live work is not below firm one's by the threshold, nothing more is made shared and the centre stays at two.

**When a stop rule fires.** The head tells staff, with the managing partner present. Live cases move to assist and are either kept by the firm at its own cost or wound down by the engineer over a set period, so no client is left on a half-built workflow. The resident stays only as an operator for case A. The pledge, the pool floor, the pay already granted and the minority's terms all stand.

**Capital allocation.** Capital goes, in order, to: stage one; the purchase price and deferred consideration of a firm that passes on case A; the per-deal team and backfill, reviewed at month 12 and 18; the reserve for puts and the pledge. Nothing goes to a platform ahead of the firm-two test. Dividends from firms above the policy minimum are reinvested in the next purchase before new capital is called.

**Realism.** [source says] Listed outsourcers with wide AI use report services margins, on three different measures: Concentrix 12.6% (non-GAAP operating margin), Teleperformance 13.6% (recurring EBITA margin), Accenture 15.4% (operating margin) (R-WEB-COMMERCIAL-09/07/02; the first two from results releases). "Often targeting 30-40% margins" is an investor's claim (R-WEB-ROLLUPS-25).

**Basis.** Methods and absences. The "before" shapes are estimates; the "after" has no inputs. Still open in this part: fee and margin per unit of service; transition cost; every amount in the centre's and firm B's tables; the break-even number of firms; every stop-rule threshold. Each is listed with what closes it in the table under the Roadmap.

**One, five, thirty.** One: the group loses money on the programme by design and should earn on case A. Five: the question is whether firm five cost less than firm one and whether the standing centre is covered; unproven. Thirty: open.

### Part 15. How it changes over time

| Stage | The organisation |
| --- | --- |
| Founding (stage one) | A managing partner, a contract engineer, a broker, an independent quality adviser; long-term capital; the method on a few pages; the perimeter map; indicative insurance terms; a shadow test at a friendly firm; no firm owned |
| After the first firm, about month 18 | One board seat; a conventional plan running; one case live or stopped, two in shadow, with reasons; a measured conversion cost; the method rewritten once. Nothing yet known about reuse |
| The firm-two test | Threshold set beforehand. Firm one's check and playbook run in shadow on firm two's live incoming work for a full period, not on its past files, which are clean and flatter the result. Measure what ran without rewriting, and engineer-weeks and practitioner-weeks to the first live case against firm one |
| If the test passes | Add a trade lead and a shared library with a release gate. Prefer targets on the same suite. Consider a unit charge and mandates. Up to two firms in their first 18 months at once |
| If it fails | Stay at two: capital, method, audit. Playbooks stay local |
| Five firms | A group partner; a full-time controller and head of quality; residents from firm one, lent with consent and paid for |
| Steady state, perhaps thirty | A layer of group partners with delegated capital limits; one trade or two; the centre a small and falling share of revenue, or the group has stopped buying. Whether agents raise a centre's span beyond thirty is unknown |

**As models improve.** Live sampling runs all the time; at each release the fixed sets and the register of failed uses are re-run. Lines move from assist to handover and, when a model or a harness regresses, back again; the downgrade is the quality lead's call and takes effect the same day. Expect to throw scaffolding away: [source says] "Every model that comes out makes the things we added for the last one pointless" (R-LIB-PEOPLE-23; one firm's founder). Single-task capture is already a built-in feature of the vendors' tools (R-WEB-MAPPING-25). What survives a model change: test sets, exception classes, client rules, limits, the record of work and the habit of release. [inferred] What a better model does not reverse: the organisational failures. One example: in a first-hand build an earlier CRM had been removed and four task lists abandoned, and "no failure was attributed to the model" (R-LIB-FAILURES-22; one user).

**Q5: if nothing durable is left beyond a few years.** Then this is the design that loses least, and the strategy is:
1. Buy at prices that work with no gain. [source says] "a cost gain that lasts three renewal cycles before it is competed away still returns its cost. What makes it a poor investment is paying for the permanent version of it in the premium" (R-LIB-STRUCTURE-06; A003).
2. Take the window as volume while fixed fees hold it, and give back the stated share before a rival forces more.
3. Know which revenue is exposed: score each line for how easily the client or the client's software could do it, segment clients by their means to do so, and do not spend the first year building only on the fees least likely to stay.
4. End the window owning what is scarce: client relationships, permissions, experienced people who stayed, and the habit of change.
5. Carry no goodwill that depends on the gain. [source says] Concentrix booked a "non-cash goodwill impairment charge of $1,050.0 million", which it attributed mainly to its share price, not to lost clients (R-WEB-COMMERCIAL-09).

What stops a small client doing it in-house is partly that they do not want to. [source says] AT&T can, with a 38-person legal team (R-LIB-ECONOMICS-15; the client's own account). [inferred] That comfort covers a volunteer board. It does not cover a client with a finance director or a competent bookkeeper, and the nearer threat is a cheaper rival or the ledger vendor. The design's answers are the price path in Part 5 and item 3 above. They are thin.

**Basis.** Judgement. One operator's account of span. All loops on file are young.

### How the design changes by type of business (Q8)

| Dimension | What it changes | Strength of basis |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Licence, ownership and status rules** | Legal form; who signs; whether the firm can be owned at all, and with what consents. Where ownership is barred (audit; German law), only a service or mandate route exists | Rules. Strong |
| **Pricing basis and who pays** | How long a saving is kept. Hourly passes it on; fixed holds it to renewal. Where the payer is not the buyer (block management) the payer has other routes to contest the fee, and must be told | Filings and analysts. Fair |
| **How checkable the outcome is, and how soon** | The control: handover only where an outside ground truth or a proven recomputation exists; full review elsewhere; leave with people where nothing can check | Both inventories; rules. Fair |
| **Capacity- or demand-constrained** | Whether to buy at all, and whether the job pledge can honestly be given | One rival buyer's hypothesis. Weak, but it decides the return |
| **How alike the work and systems are from firm to firm** | How much centre to build | Added by this run. No evidence; the firm-two test |
| Consequence of a wrong act | Safety and trust money put a person on every act | Rules; the regulator reviewer |
| Physical work | Caps the share of hours any system can reach | Inventory estimates |
| Small-business or enterprise clients | A screen: enterprise buyers say they will take the saving and can insource | Stated intent; one client's account |
| Country | Employment and ownership law only | Rules |
| Global or local; advice or coordination; commodity or high-touch | Little by themselves. They show up through the rows above | No evidence either way |

**What drives the design.** Four dimensions drive the design of a firm: licence and ownership, pricing basis and payer, checkability, and the capacity-or-demand constraint. A fifth, likeness of work and systems between firms, drives the design of the group.

## Challenges and open questions

### Where the three rival designs disagreed, and which way I went

| Disagreement | Platform | One-firm | Mandate | This design, and why |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| How much centre | Eight or nine at one firm | Three; capped | About five | Two standing, plus a per-deal team; then the firm-two test. The reuse premise is unmeasured and the arithmetic is against a larger centre |
| Can the centre order? | Yes: platform, gate, calendar | Only a short list | Cannot order a mandate firm | A fixed list, adopted by the firm's board. Berkshire, the one autonomy precedent that speaks to it, says its firms did not need to change their work |
| Equity | Group shares only | A minority in the seller's own firm | Both | Local for heads and signatories; cash for staff. Liability and clients are local; units with no exit pay nobody |
| Systems of record | Converge per trade | Keep | Keep; decline closed systems | Keep. Evidence is two opposed self-descriptions |
| Where playbooks live | Central library | The firm's property | Central, with local rule files | The firm's, until made shared through a gate and accepted firm by firm |
| Back office | Central from month one | Local | Rented | Local, except finance reporting |
| Charging | Unit price from the start | None | Unit price to all | Group-borne for 18 months; quality and audit always; at cost by choice after |
| Central selling | A growth lead | None | Partners sell to peers | None centrally, on the vets evidence |
| Vehicle | Buy and converge | Buy and leave | Buy one, serve many | A paid shadow mandate first; then buy one at a time |

**Why not the mandate as the vehicle.** Its own author names the weakest point: "It may be outsourcing with a better story." Listed outsourcers report margins of about 12.6% to 15.4% on the measures in Part 14. The gain is made in the review, and under a mandate the review belongs to someone else. It has no authority in the served firm. Its real merits are kept: little capital at risk and diligence from inside, which is why it is now stage one.

### Where the parts strain against each other

- **The pledge and the economics.** A three-year pledge, a funded pool floor and backfill make case C dearer. They are the price of honest measurement by staff. In a demand-limited firm the two cannot be squared, so such firms are not bought.
- **Full review and the return.** With every outward deliverable reviewed, the programme's gain is preparation time and redesign, and reviewers become the limit. The base case may leave case B close to nothing. That is the finding the design is built to reach cheaply.
- **Local equity and shared work.** A head has little reason to lend staff to another firm. Paying the lending firm at a commercial rate answers part of it. If the test passes and sharing matters, heads may need a group element too.
- **Signatory liability and shared playbooks.** Local acceptance, the stop right and the indemnity are the answer. No insurer has been asked whether that is enough.
- **The mandated core and the perimeter.** Every order the centre gives is a step towards being treated as running the regulated firm. Board adoption is an answer on paper.
- **The centre and its pay.** It is not covered at one firm on any figure I have.

### Open questions, in order of how much the answer would change the design

1. Ben's capital, horizon and access, including to a firm for stage one. Not recorded.
2. Whether an investor may hold a voting majority in a UK accountancy firm without loss of the status and licences it needs.
3. What a UK insurer will bind, ask and charge for AI-prepared work, on change of control, and for one fault at many firms.
4. Whether attention per unit falls with the catch rate held (experiment 2), and how much of the work has a real independent check.
5. Whether small UK clients leave or ask for lower fees when told. No evidence.
6. Whether client work may lawfully improve a firm's own playbooks. All rows are by analogy.
7. Seller terms and multiples in UK deals, and whether these terms win a deal.
8. Client and staff loss after a sale. One lead of 8.4% against 3.2% client turnover at sold US advice firms was seen in a search summary only (R-WEB-COMMERCIAL-29).
9. A sound failure rate for roll-ups and for improvement programmes. None exists.

### Where the reviewers were not answered

The full table of objections and responses is in `review/responses.md`. These are the serious objections the design still does not meet.

1. **A return case in pounds, a hurdle rate and a break-even number of firms (private-equity operator).** Not supplied. The inputs do not exist in the evidence and the brief forbids filling them with plausible numbers. I give the structure, the three cases and the reviewer's own rough arithmetic, which points against the design at the reference size. An investment committee cannot approve stage two on this page; it can approve stage one.
2. **Thresholds for the stop rules and experiments (private-equity operator; completeness).** I fixed their form, who sets them and who decides, and gave no numbers, for the same reason. Until the holding board writes them, each stop rule is still a discussion.
3. **Review cannot be both independent of the output and cheaper than re-performance (capability sceptic).** Not answered. The design narrows handover to work with an outside ground truth or a proven recomputation, and measures the catch rate beside the time. Whether blind-first review reduces deference at all is untested, and I have no argument that it must.
4. **Errors that surface years later cannot be seen by an 18-month reading (capability sceptic; regulator).** A reserve, the record of work and telling the insurer are mitigations. The stop rule still passes or fails without that information.
5. **A fault in the model itself is correlated across every firm (capability sceptic; regulator).** Staggering shared changes does not touch it. Not answered beyond using two suppliers where the work allows.
6. **Whether the group sits inside the regulatory perimeter (regulator).** Turned into a founding task and a condition of completion, not resolved. The reviewer's rule references were unchecked and so were not built in as rules. The answer may overturn the equity design in Part 2.
7. **Insurance (regulator; client; managing partner).** Every structural step asked for is adopted. No term, limit, aggregation wording or price is known, and whether an uncapped intra-group indemnity is itself insurable is not known.
8. **The base case may be the whole case (regulator; capability sceptic).** If full review stays, the economics of the programme are close to nil. I have made that the base case and not shown that anything better is reachable.
9. **Quiet non-cooperation (captured employee).** Gain share, authorship pay, independent baselines, audited correction classes and the completeness test change the incentives. A senior can still review slowly and honestly, and nothing in the design can or should order otherwise. Whether the bargain is good enough is tested only in experiment 4.
10. **The seats (captured employee).** The seat map makes the question visible. It does not create seats. If growth does not come, there are fewer roles than people at year three, and the answer is redeployment pay or severance.
11. **What the client gets (client).** A stated benefit and a give-back rule now exist; their size is open, and the first converted cases are still the ones most easily taken in-house.
12. **Would a seller choose this (managing partner; private-equity operator).** The terms are now written in form. Against debt-funded buyers paying profit earn-outs they may still lose. Tested in experiment 1, not answered.
13. **The carve-out of audit (managing partner; regulator).** Referring the book on protects independence and costs fees and client contact. The managing partner's objection that this puts those clients in front of a rival stands.
14. **Points of UK regulation and law raised from working knowledge** by the regulator, client and managing-partner reviewers (ICAEW control and affiliate rules, AML approval of owners, successor-practice and run-off rules, network-firm status, service-charge consultation thresholds, right to manage, training-contract experience). None was checked. Each is carried as a question, tagged [reviewer].
15. **Second-pass objections about fit between parts (completeness, second report).** The last correction pass acted only on the three parts still marked partly decided. It did not act on these, which stand: nobody is paid for, or given authority over, the conventional plan beyond the head's dividend and the resident's plan measure; the give-back rule lowers the fee retention the head's deferred pay rests on; the "at any time" stop rule pauses handovers on client losses, which is the wrong way round when a rival cuts price, and no stop rule is written for falling fees; the head of quality decides correctness disputes on shared items and then audits them; the firm must claim on its own majority owner's indemnity and nobody is named to decide that it does; the fallback ownership structure, if a majority is not permitted, is not designed; two to four firms is the least designed stretch (no trade lead, no tie-break between two heads, no named route for a model-level alert); stage one has no resident to run the floor week; and the worked example is a firm of 30, which the size screen would reject. The full list, with 31 places where the document does not say who does what, is in `review/completeness-recheck.md`.
