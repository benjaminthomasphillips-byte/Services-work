# Work inventory: reference firm A (30-person UK accountancy practice)

Stage 4 of the run. Written 23 November 2026. One author, no sub-agents. Nothing was written to Notion.

**What this is.** A desk-built inventory of a model firm, not a record of a real one. No real firm's job list, time records or fee ledger was seen. Rules and regulator texts are cited as read. Every volume, hour and fee in the table is a model assumption, built so that the parts add up to published benchmarks. They are there so the test can be scored and the shape of the firm seen. They are not findings and must not be quoted as such. In a real diligence each would be replaced by an export from the target's practice-management system.

**Tags.** [source says] = a source states it; the source ID (S1 to S21, section 5) or ledger row ID follows. [inferred] = my reasoning or general knowledge of UK practice, not checked against a source in this run. "Model assumption" = a number I chose.

**How sure.** Sure of the rules in sections 1 and 3 that carry a source. Fairly sure of the list of lines: the work of a UK practice is set by statute and looks alike from firm to firm. Not sure of any hour figure: treat shares of hours as right to within about ten points either way, and single lines as worse than that.

### Revenue and margin (estimates)

- **Fee income: about £2.2m to £2.8m. Estimate.** Basis: [source says] median fees per fee earner about £95,000 for 2023 (S2; search summary only), times 26, gives about £2.5m. - **Profit before partners' pay: about 28% to 36% of fees. Estimate.** Basis: [source says] an "All UK average" net margin of 35.8%, with the top quarter of employing firms at "41% or above" (S4; a software vendor's survey of 520, self-reported, and the average includes sole practitioners, whose margins the report itself says are "structurally" inflated). **Insurance and AI.** This is ledger gap 2. What was found:

- [source says] Minimum cover is £2m "for any single claim and in the aggregate" where fee income is £800,000 or more. The policy must be "qualifying insurance" from a participating insurer on terms matching "ICAEW's approved minimum wording". The aggregate excess may not exceed "the higher of either £3,000, or 3% of a firm's gross fee income" (S8, regulations 3.2, 3.3, 3.7, effective 1 October 2026; read in part). For this firm that is £2m of cover and an excess of up to about £75,000.
- [source says] A keyword search of those regulations found no mention of artificial intelligence. The minimum wording itself was not read. So I cannot say AI-produced work is covered; I can say the regulations do not carve it out.
- [source says] The tax bodies' joint guidance of January 2026: "Members are ultimately responsible for any work they produce, and for regulated firms any work which the firm prepares, irrespective of the use of AI tools in its creation." AI output "should also be regarded as if it were prepared by a less experienced junior colleague and reviewed with appropriate scepticism". Where tax software "can be configured to make human review optional", the member should ensure "the data and the submission can be subject to an appropriate review". An engagement-letter statement on AI "can help support transparency". Putting client data into public AI tools "is likely to constitute a breach of client confidentiality, unless the client has consented" (S9; read in part).
- [source says] A law firm that writes for insurers suggests they ask accountants: "What processes verify the accuracy of AI-generated financial statements, tax returns, and audit reports?" and whether there is "always a human reviewer for important decisions or work product". It notes wordings "commonly have exclusions which exclude situations where recovery rights have been limited by contract", which bears on a firm's contract with its AI supplier (S11; proposals to insurers, not insurers' practice).
- [source says] A trade report says the notable addition to proposal forms is a question on AI use (S20; solicitors' market; search summary only).
- **Still open:** any UK insurer's actual question, wording or price for an accountancy firm; whether sampling, in place of review of every output, is acceptable to an insurer (the conflict noted at ledger Part 13 stands). The January 2026 guidance reads as review of each tax deliverable, which is stricter than R-WEB-LAW-19.

## 2. The inventory

**Unit.** A line is a repeatable piece of work with one trigger and one output, at the level a practice-management system would hold as a job type or a recurring task. 87 lines: 61 for clients, 26 for the firm itself.

**The task test, scored Y (yes), P (partly), N (no), in this order:** 1 readable state; 2 written playbook with exceptions; 3 a way to write; 4 a verifier independent of the maker; 5 a clear trigger; 6 authority and limits; 7 a named human owner and exception route; 8 a way to undo; 9 a test set from real past cases; 10 volume and value enough to pay for build and upkeep. So `PPYPYPYYYY` means items 1, 2, 4 and 6 are partly met and the rest are met.

**How I scored.** As the firm stands today, before any build. Item 1 is P wherever any input arrives by email, phone or paper. Item 2 is P where the institute's or software's checklist exists but the firm's own rules per client do not. Item 3 follows the HMRC rule above. Item 4 is Y only where something outside the maker checks the result (a bank balance, a registrar, a pension provider, a supplier statement); a manager reading the maker's file is P; nothing is N. Item 6 is scored on whether limits could be stated simply, since none are written today. Item 9 is Y where prior-year files exist in volume. Item 10 is my judgement; no threshold exists (ledger Q1, unknown).

**Status rule.** *Hand over now*: no N, and items 3 and 4 are met or the error is cheap and caught downstream. *Hand over with a designed check*: the work is specifiable and the output can be reversed or is gated, but today's check is a person nodding or an input is off-system; a check must be built first. *Assist only*: the agent drafts, a person remains the maker. *Leave with people*: accountability, relationship or judgement is the work.

**Roles:** P partner, M manager, S senior, T trainee or semi-senior, B bookkeeper, PY payroll, A administrator, PM practice manager, RI audit responsible individual, MLRO reporting officer.

## 4. Verdict on the inventory method

The question was: "do we need to list out the 200 things the company does?"

**What building the list was good for**

1. **It found the shared blockers.** Three things recur across dozens of lines: no independent check, client-specific rules that are unwritten, inputs outside any system. One workflow studied alone would have shown each as a local problem. The list shows they are the platform.
2. **It showed where hours are.** Ten lines hold about 40% of hours. The other 77 share the rest.
3. **It separated what software already does from what is new.** This stops a group paying to build what the firm's suite includes.
4. **It found hard outside limits early:** HMRC's ban on automating its web pages (S10) and the review duty in the tax bodies' guidance (S9).
5. **It showed the knock-on effects between lines:** preparation feeds review; the first lines to go are the training ground.
6. **It tested the test.** Item 4 did most of the sorting. Item 7 sorted nothing as scored. Item 10 could not be scored against a threshold because none exists. Two things the ten items miss came up repeatedly: whether the client supplies inputs in time, and whether an outside body permits the write path. I would add both.

**What it cost**

- This desk version: one agent session, about two dozen searches and fetches, no access to a firm. A real one needs the practice-management export (job types, counts, time by job code), which gives the first five columns quickly, and then interviews for the exception and checking columns. I have no measured cost for that and will not guess one. The ledger's nearest figures are for going deep, not for listing: six weeks per tax domain (R-WEB-STACK-04) and 42 procedures after nine months of capture (R-WEB-MAPPING-05; vendor).
- [inferred] For accountancy the list is cheap. The profession has already cut its work into job types with statutory triggers, and the software holds them. That is less true of advisory work and would be less true of reference firm B.

**What it cannot tell you**

- How often each exception happens. The table names exceptions; it has no counts, and the ledger says the exception, not the task, holds the cost (R-LIB-DELIVERY-02/12).
- The real state of inputs, client by client.
- Whether the firm's reviews catch errors now. No error rate was found for any line.
- Whether staff will use what is built, or will say what they know (R-LIB-FAILURES-09; R-WEB-PEOPLE-30).
- How this firm differs from the model. "Bespoke even where the noun is shared" (R-LIB-IMPROVE-TECH-36).
- Hours saved, or what a saved hour is worth. All hour figures here are assumptions.
- It also invites a false finish. 87 lines look like a build plan. They are a map at one date, which is the "static" fault under examination in Q4.

**Which first move is better for a firm like this**

Both, in a set order, and they are different jobs.

1. **In diligence: a light full inventory.** One to two pages, about 100 lines, from the practice-management export, scored coarsely on the test. Use it as a screen and to pick where to start. It is cheap here and it caught things a narrow start would miss.
2. **After completion: go deep on three workflows, not 87.** My candidates from the table: the back-office cluster (order 1), the records chase (order 3), and one preparation line with its check built (VAT, order 6). Each gets real exception counts, a test set from the firm's own past files, a named owner and a designed check.
3. **Do not turn the 87 lines into 87 builds or "products".** Let the list grow from the floor and be re-scored at each model release.

This agrees with the ledger's verdict on Q1 (start narrow; use the inventory as a screen) and adds one thing: for a UK accountancy practice the screen is cheap enough, and showed enough, that skipping it would be a mistake. Confidence: medium. It rests on a model firm and on the absence of any case that tried a full inventory first and reported the result.

**Where this disagrees with something upstream.** The ledger's Q5 assumption treats the "signed, accountable tier" as protected for now. For firm A that tier is small in law (audit only). I have shown the disagreement in section 3 and not resolved it.
