# Work inventory: reference firm B (block management and lettings, about 50 people)

Stage 4 of the run. Written 23 November 2026. One agent, no sub-agents. Nothing was written to Notion.

**What this is.** A desk-built inventory of a typical firm, not a record of a real one. I had no access to a firm's system, timesheets or staff. The list of work comes from the RICS Code's own list of an agent's services, trade-body notes, statute dates and my general knowledge of the sector. Every volume and every hour figure is an estimate. The scoring against the ten-item test is my judgement of a typical firm on a typical system. **How sure I am:** fairly sure the list of lines is close to complete; moderately sure of the pattern of scores; not sure of any single volume or hour figure. A count from one real firm's system would replace most of section 2's numbers in a week.

Tags: **[source says]** and **[inferred]**. Ledger rows are cited by ID (R-…). Sources found for this piece are cited as S-B-01 to S-B-17 and listed in section 5.

## 1. The firm in one page

**What it does.** Two businesses under one roof [inferred as the typical mixed shape; the brief allows either or both]:

- **Block management** (about four-fifths of the firm): managing agent for about 260 blocks holding about 7,000 leasehold flats. Clients are resident management companies, right-to-manage companies and some freeholders. The people who pay are the leaseholders, through the service charge.
- **Lettings and property management**: about 600 managed tenancies for about 450 private landlords, plus some let-only work.

The shape (260 blocks, 7,000 units, 600 tenancies) is my assumption. It is sized from the fee evidence below so that 50 people is plausible. The TPI sample averages 55 homes an estate (117,052 homes in 2,137 estates) [source says, S-B-01]; I have used 27 because a 50-person regional firm manages smaller blocks than the 13 firms in that sample. That is a guess.

**People by role (all estimates; no published staffing benchmark was found).**

| Team | Roles | People |
|---|---|---|
| Direction | Managing director | 1 |
| Block management | Head of block management 1; senior property managers 3; property managers 8; assistant property managers 5; customer service 3; compliance coordinator 1; building surveyor 1; insurance and company-secretarial administrator 1; handover and sales-pack administrator 1 | 24 |
| Client accounts (block) | Head 1; service-charge accountants 3; purchase ledger 2; credit control 2 | 8 |
| Lettings | Lettings director 1; negotiators 3; tenancy administrator 1; property managers 4; inspector and inventory clerk 1; lettings client accounts 1 | 11 |
| Central | Finance 1; HR and office manager 1; IT and systems 1; marketing and new business 1; compliance lead 1; reception and administrator 1 | 6 |
| **Total** | | **50** |

That gives about 640 units and 24 blocks per portfolio manager, with one assistant between two managers, and 150 tenancies per lettings manager. Checks on those ratios: Dwelly says about 100 units per lettings manager is the baseline it started from (R-WEB-ROLLUPS-14, self-reported); a consultant's blog says about 90 (S-B-17, search summary only). So my 150 may be high. For block management I found no ratio at all: the 2019 Fixflo survey says firms with repairs software have "the highest number of leasehold units managed per property manager" but the write-up gives no number (S-B-12). **Open.**

**Pay.** Advertised block property manager salaries in 2026 run about £35,000 to £45,000, senior roles to about £50,000 to £55,000 in London (S-B-15, search summary of job advertisements; not read individually).

**Revenue (estimate, with the arithmetic).**

- *Block core fee.* The TPI index gives total management fees of £42.99m across 117,052 homes in 2026 budgets, and says all categories include VAT [source says, S-B-01]. That is £367 a home with VAT, about £306 without [my arithmetic]. An undated leaseholder-association sheet gives £200 to £380 a flat; a London agent gives £250 to £500 plus VAT (S-B-10, search summaries, the second a commercial source). At £250 to £310 a unit, 7,000 units give **£1.75m to £2.2m**.
- *Block extra fees* (section 20 and major works, sales packs, company secretarial, licences, insurance income). TPI says extra fees for major works are "normal practice" (S-B-02). I found no figures for any of them. One cross-check: HML Holdings, a listed block manager until it was taken private, reported revenue of £28.11m in the year to March 2019 and 74,000 units a year earlier (S-B-13, search summary only, years do not match exactly). That is roughly £380 of total revenue a unit, against a core fee of about £300 today, which suggests extras of a fifth to a quarter on top. Weak. **Treat the extras as open.**
- *Lettings.* Published guides put full management at about 10% to 15% of rent plus VAT, tenant-find at up to one month's rent (S-B-11, search summaries of commercial guides; no authoritative survey found). I did not fetch an average rent, so I give no lettings revenue figure. **Open.**
- *Whole firm.* If extras add a fifth to block fees, block revenue is about £2.1m to £2.6m. Lettings on top. Total is probably £3m to £4m, or £60,000 to £80,000 a head. **This is an estimate resting on one sourced fee figure and two weak cross-checks.**

**Margin (estimate).** No benchmark for a firm of this size was found. Two weak markers: a third-party summary shows HML's EBITDA margin at 9.8% and 12.4% in two unlabelled years (S-B-13); a conference speaker put the average margin of the top 1,000 UK letting agents at 11.9% (S-B-14, unnamed speaker, basis unknown). A sponsored trade piece calls block-management margins "tight". The 2019 survey found firms growing units without growing profit (S-B-12). A range of roughly 8% to 15% before owner's pay adjustments is consistent with these and proves nothing. **Open; Companies House accounts of six to ten firms of this size would settle it.** Dwelly's and Supa's accounts were not retrieved.

| Service | Basis | Who pays | Evidence |

Two points matter for the design. First, almost all revenue is fixed fee or a share of rent, not hourly. A saving in hours does not shrink the bill by itself (compare R-LIB-ECONOMICS-02/03/04/06). Second, the payer is not the buyer in block management. Leaseholders pay; a volunteer board buys. Fee pressure comes at re-tender, and from the tribunal's test that charges be reasonable (S-B-01, appendix).

**Clients.** About 220 client entities for 260 blocks, mostly resident companies run by volunteer directors [inferred]. About 450 landlords; Dwelly's claim is that landlords "rarely switch" (R-LIB-STRUCTURE-26, self-reported).

**Systems (typical; market shares not found).**

- Block system of record: a block-management package holding leases, ledgers, client bank accounts, jobs and contractor panel. MRI says its Qube product covers "almost one-third of the ARMA membership base" (ARMA is now part of TPI) and over a million units (S-B-16, the vendor's own claim). Others exist (Dwellant, Blockman, Resident, Propman were named in my search and returned nothing usable).
- Lettings system of record: a lettings CRM and client-accounting package. A vendor's own ranking of add-ons lists a tenant-onboarding tool, a referencing provider and an identity-check tool as most used (S-B-16, vendor data).
- Repairs reporting tool; a document store or shared drives; shared inboxes; phones; an office accounting package; a banking platform with dual authorisation.
- **Whether any of these systems lets an outside agent write to it is not known.** One vendor in the ledger refused the write path (R-LIB-IMPROVE-TECH-34). This is the first thing to ask in diligence.

**Regulators and rules.**

- No statutory licence for managing agents or letting agents in England as at this date [inferred from the absence of any such rule in the sources read; not searched directly]. Regulation is by membership: RICS for regulated firms, The Property Institute (members "agree to abide by the RICS Service Charge Residential Management Code", S-B-02), Propertymark for lettings.
- The RICS Code, 4th edition, is effective from 7 April 2026 and lists the core services (S-B-03).
- Client money: service-charge money is held on statutory trust; reconcile monthly, within four weeks of month end (S-B-03); reconciliations "reviewed by a principal or senior staff member" and payments under dual authorisation (S-B-05). Propertymark requires yearly client-account documents, and an independent accountant's report above £1m of client funds (S-B-06).
- AI: the RICS standard "Responsible use of artificial intelligence in surveying practice" has bound members and regulated firms since 9 March 2026 (S-B-04). Detail in section 3.
- Leasehold reform dates now fixed: a right to information and new litigation-cost rules from 1 April 2027; prescribed demands, standard accounts with a qualified accountant's report, annual reports, an insurance-commission ban and a published schedule of administration charges from 1 November 2027 (S-B-08, a law firm's note dated 29 October 2026).
- Insurance work touches FCA rules (S-B-03 6.5g). Company secretarial work and higher-rent lettings may bring the firm under money-laundering supervision [inferred; not checked, see section 5].

**What limits growth.** Capacity, on the evidence available, and specifically experienced property managers.

- [source says] 95% of 170 surveyed block managers had grown their portfolios in five years, nearly half by more than 35%, yet struggled to turn growth into profit (S-B-12; 2019, a software vendor's survey).
- [inferred] Blocks are won at re-tender and by referral; work arrives faster than managers can be hired and trained. Each added block adds email, repairs and a board to attend. Margin is lost in the manager's inbox, which is where section 2 finds the hours.
- [inferred] Lettings is closer to demand-limited: landlords are selling up in some markets and each instruction is competed for. The library's own caution applies: a capacity-bound lettings firm "becomes demand-bound once cheap coordination lets it serve every landlord in its town" (LIB-ECONOMICS, context 1).
- So the block side fits Chroma's condition for an owner keeping a saving (short of capacity, R-LIB-ECONOMICS-09) better than the lettings side. Confidence: moderate, resting on one old survey and inference.

## 2. The inventory

**How to read the table.**

- 123 lines. Abbreviations: PM property manager; APM assistant property manager; CS customer service; SCA service-charge accountant; PL purchase ledger; CC credit control; PMS the property-management system of record; DMS document store.
- **Volume:** (d) derived by arithmetic from the firm's shape; (g) a guess that a count from the system would replace.
- **Hours:** my estimate of staff hours a year on the line. They total 84,740, against about 82,500 available from 50 people at 1,650 hours. The overrun is estimation error, left visible.
- **Test 1-10:** ten letters in the order of the task test. Y yes, P partly, N no. Order: 1 readable state; 2 written playbook with exceptions; 3 a way to write; 4 a verifier independent of the maker; 5 a clear trigger; 6 authority and limits; 7 a named owner and exception route; 8 a way to undo; 9 a test set from real past cases; 10 volume and value enough to pay for build and upkeep.
- **Status:** four values. **Binding item** is the missing item that most holds the line back.
- Scoring conventions I applied. Item 7 is Y everywhere because a named person owns every line today; the exception *route* is mostly unwritten, which I have scored under item 2. Item 9 is never Y: no firm of this kind holds a test set; P means past cases sit in the system and a set could be assembled. Item 3 assumes the system of record can be written to through its normal screens or an interface; if the vendor refuses, most Y and P scores on item 3 fall to N.
- "Hand over now" mostly means rule-driven software that many firms already run. It is not agent work (compare R-LIB-DELIVERY-34: some gains need no agent).

### 3.4 Where the licensed signature sits

**It is thin.** This is the main difference from reference firm A.

- [inferred] There is no reserved activity and no statutory licence to manage a block or let a home. Nothing in this firm's work must by law be signed by a licensed individual in the way an audit report must.
- What exists instead is a set of narrower named accountabilities:
  - **Client money.** A principal reviews reconciliations and authorises payments (S-B-05). An independent accountant reports yearly (S-B-06). This is the closest thing to a licensed signature and it covers lines A5, A17, A18, I24, J1.
  - **Service-charge accounts.** Examined by an independent qualified accountant, outside the firm (S-B-03; statutory from 1 November 2027, S-B-08). The signature belongs to someone else. It is a ready-made independent verifier for line A10.
  - **AI outputs with material impact** (regulated firms only). A written reliability decision "prepared by, or under the supervision of, an appropriately qualified and named surveyor who accepts responsibility for its use" (S-B-04). This is a new signature created by the AI rule itself.
  - **Insurance**, under FCA rules; **building safety**, where the client is the accountable person and the agent acts for it; **gas, electrical and fire assessments**, signed by outside competent persons the firm instructs.
  - **Statutory steps without a licence but with penalties**: section 20 notices, deposit protection, right-to-rent checks, prescribed demand forms.
- Consequence for the roll-up. Chroma's claim that "the licensed signature decides who keeps" the saving (R-LIB-STRUCTURE-30) gives firm B little protection. A client with the same tools is not stopped by a licence. What holds the client is the client-money machinery, the trust of a volunteer board, local contractors, and the nuisance of changing agent. Confidence: moderate; the absence of a statutory licence was not checked against a primary source, and the government has consulted on regulating agents (S-B-12 records 60% of firms expecting it in 2019).

**What the RICS AI standard changes** [source says, S-B-04, read in the original]:

- It applies only where AI output has "a material impact on the delivery of the surveying service". The firm must record that determination.
- A written register of AI systems; a risk register "reviewed and updated at least quarterly"; written due diligence on suppliers.
- A written reliability decision per output, by a named surveyor. But for automated or high-volume outputs "it is generally neither necessary nor proportionate to scrutinise each output". Firms "must therefore undertake randomised dip samples of the outputs at regular intervals", and "remain accountable for each output".
- Clients must be told "in writing and in advance". Terms of engagement must state when and where AI is used, "if available, the extent of professional indemnity cover for use of AI systems", how to contest and seek redress, and "how a client can opt out … if at all".

Three things follow. The standard backs sampling over step approval for volume work, which supports the ledger's reading of R-WEB-LAW-19 and sits against the insurers' proposed "all outputs" question (R-WEB-LAW-25). It forces the disclosure the ledger already assumed (Part 5). And the opt-out clause means a board could refuse AI on its block, so the firm must be able to run a block the old way. **Not found:** any insurer's wording or price on AI for property managers; any AI rule from TPI; any formal AI rule from Propertymark, which has only said that agents may use AI on complaints if "final decisions … always rest with a trained professional" (S-B-07). A law firm writing for insurers says they "will want to consider how the standard affects risk assessment" and that it "will inform the assessment of professional negligence claims" (S-B-09). That is a forecast, not a policy term. It is unclear how far the standard reaches a firm that is in TPI or Propertymark but not RICS-regulated.

## 4. Verdict on the method: "do we need to list out the 200 things the company does?"

**What building the list was good for.**

1. **It showed the firm needs a few shared things, not 123 builds.** The binding items cluster: verifier, authority, readable state, write path. That finding only appears when every line is scored on the same test. Starting from one workflow would not have shown it.
2. **It found the lines not to touch,** and why. Client money control, physical visits, the board relationship. A workflow-first start can blunder into these.
3. **It located the signature,** and found it thin. That changes the investment case for this type of firm more than any automation estimate.
4. **It separated agent work from plain software.** About a tenth of the lines are rules the system already runs.
5. **It gave a map of who waits for whom,** which says which measures will mislead.
6. **It is a usable diligence screen.** The same sheet, filled from a target's system, shows volumes, where checks are a person nodding, and whether the system can be written to.

**What it cost.** For me, a few hours at a desk, because the sector's work is unusually well codified: the RICS Code lists the core services from (a) to (p) and the extras (S-B-03 6.5 and 6.6). [inferred] At a real firm, a list at this grain is a matter of days: the management agreement, the system's task and job types, the ledger's transaction counts, and a half-day each with five team leads. I have no evidence for that estimate. The ledger's only timings are for a different thing: nine months of capture for 42 procedures (R-WEB-MAPPING-05) and six weeks to build one tax domain (R-WEB-STACK-04). Those are the cost of going deep, not of listing.

**What it cannot tell you.**

- **Whether any line will work.** A workflow with state, procedure, write path and test set scored 92.8% in tests and 22.3% in production because real requests were incomplete (R-LIB-DELIVERY-05). An inventory scores the line as designed, not the traffic as it arrives.
- **Real volumes, exception rates and hours.** Mine are estimates. Even at a real firm, hours per line are not recorded; nobody in this trade keeps timesheets on fixed-fee work [inferred].
- **The exceptions that matter.** I listed the commonest. The costly ones are known to the people doing the work and may not be writable (R-LIB-FAILURES-32) or offered (R-WEB-PEOPLE-30).
- **Whether staff will use what is built** (R-LIB-FAILURES-09).
- **How the lines join.** The table has 123 rows; the firm has perhaps a dozen real cases (a repair, a block-year of money, a tenancy, a major work, a sale, a take-on). The row is the wrong unit to build.
- **The score is opinion.** Two scorers would differ on "partly". It ranks; it does not measure.
- **It goes stale.** Three statutory dates in 2027 change at least eight lines (S-B-08). Nothing in the method keeps it true.

**Which first move for a firm like this.** Both, in a fixed order, and neither is "200 products".

1. **A light full inventory first, as a screen.** About 120 lines, one row each, scored on the ten items, filled from the system in about a week. Do it in diligence. Use it to find the shared missing items, the no-go lines, the write path and the three or four cases worth building. Do not write a playbook per line.
2. **Then build two or three whole cases, not lines.** For this firm: the repair case from report to paid invoice (ten-list items 1, 3, 4); the money month from receipt to reconciliation (item 2); and one fixed-procedure document product, sales packs or section 20 (items 6, 9). Write playbooks, exception classes and test sets only for those, with the people who do the work.
3. **Let the inventory be updated by the work,** not by a mapping exercise: each new exception class and each change in law amends a row.

This agrees with the ledger's assumption for Q1 (start from a few workflows; light inventory as screen) and adds one thing: **for this type of firm the light inventory is cheap enough that skipping it is a false economy**, because the profession's own code has already written most of the list. For a firm whose work is not codified, that may not hold. It disagrees with "turn them into products" as a general aim: productising fits perhaps ten of 123 lines here (fixed procedure, a unit price, an outside reader), namely sales packs, notices, section 20, company secretarial, deposit and tenancy documents. Most of the rest is one fixed fee covering a bundle, and the client does not buy the parts.

**How sure.** Fairly sure of the order (screen, then cases). Not sure the week-long estimate holds at a real firm. The verdict rests on one desk exercise and on the absence of any case that began with a full inventory and reported (ledger Q1).
