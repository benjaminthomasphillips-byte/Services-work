## What this adds up to

Inference, mine, not the sources'.

1. The library's answer to "who keeps the saving" is a framework, not a finding. Pricing basis, where the bottleneck sits and who else competes decide it. No case measures it. The design should treat retention as an assumption and build the test into the first firm.
2. The nearest things to observation point one way for comparable, hourly or unit-priced work: filed deflation at RWS and WPP, 10 to 15 per cent pass-through in IT services on the sellers' account, a quarter of law firms discounting, a named client (AT&T) taking the junior tier in-house. They point the other way where the buyer cannot compare or does not negotiate the fee: audit fees unmoved after ten years of tools, debtor-paid collection, Klarna pricing its own service.
3. So "commodity" is likely for the routine, specifiable tier and not shown for the signed, accountable tier. What is left, on the sources' own arguments, is the licence and signature, the client relationship and records, distribution, and the ability to make change stick. Playbooks and evaluation sets are said to be quick to copy.
4. For reference firm A (accountancy), the fee basis must move or hours saved shrink the bill, and the entry market is crowded. For reference firm B (lettings), the operating evidence is better, but the gain is a window before the firm is demand-bound against rivals on the same vendor software.
5. Entry price matters more than the AI case. A temporary gain pays at a small-firm multiple and does not at a 45 to 65 per cent premium.
