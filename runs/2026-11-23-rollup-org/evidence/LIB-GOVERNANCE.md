## What this adds up to

Inference, mine, not any source's.

1. Every source that addresses accountability puts it on a named human, including the advocates of autonomy. None shows a regulator accepting an agent or a system as the accountable party.
2. The approval dispute is narrower than it looks. Deel approves writes by "an owner or a policy". B33 hard-codes gates only on irreversible outward actions. N076's budget is itself a control. The real question is which actions get a person, and L015 gives the sorting rule: consequence, reversibility, and how soon the outcome can be checked. The Tamkin trial says that where a person is used, they should judge before seeing the agent's argument, or be someone other than the maker. No case tests one rule against the other.
3. Agent identity and permissions are organisation design. The one detailed account ended with a single agent inheriting the user's access, service accounts for scheduled work, and tagging by customer. A roll-up needs the same by client and by firm; the library holds no example.
4. On recording, the evidence favours capture as a by-product of work, and decisions made in writing, over blanket recording. O'Connor's costs are argued, not measured. Consent and confidentiality are named as conditions and nowhere examined.

## What is missing

- A case comparing per-step approval with limits and budgets on the same work.
- A tested remedy for deference.
- A real agent budget: who sets it, what happens at the limit. How an agent is reviewed or retired.
- Any division of decisions between a centre and acquired firms.
- Insurers' view of AI-produced work; any regulator's own text.
- Law on recording staff and clients.
- How a disagreement between a person and an agent is settled. B33's "sent back" and "held for a person" counts are the nearest thing.
- Services firms. Almost every agent-governance case is a software or AI company.
