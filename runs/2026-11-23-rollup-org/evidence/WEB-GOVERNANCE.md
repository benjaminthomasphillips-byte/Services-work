# WEB-GOVERNANCE: governance and management systems in multi-firm groups, and how they change with agents

Scope: outside research, done 23 November 2026. All pages were fetched and read as text by this agent unless a row says otherwise. Web search was not available (the run's shared search budget was already spent), so every source was reached by a known address or by following links on a fetched page. That biases the set towards well-known sources. "Constellation" is Constellation Software Inc. All its letters are by Mark Leonard, founder and president, to his own shareholders: an operator describing his own firm. Rows 06, 09, 19 and 24 were cut for length; their numbers are not reused.

## (a) How lasting groups divide decisions and run themselves

### R-WEB-GOVERNANCE-01
- Claim: At Constellation the layer above an operating firm may coach but may not give orders; its one hard power is to replace the firm's manager.
- Kind: company claim (operator's account of own practice)
- Quote: "They can nurture, goad and suggest, but they can’t order. No PM can personally know the customers, products, employees, and competitors sufficiently well across multiple BU’s ... to make the critical decisions required at the BU level. In the infrequent instances where the manager of a BU isn’t making the grade… then the PM needs to find a replacement for the BU manager."
- Where: https://www.csisoftware.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/presidents-letter-april-2018-final.pdf (letter dated 20 April 2018)
- Speaker and interest: Leonard, founder and president, to his own shareholders.
- Read: full
- Bears on: 3, 9; Q3, Q6
- Against Ben's starting positions: limits the "central platform" stance; a centre that pushes one operating system must give orders, which this long-lived group says it avoids.

## (b) How routines changed where agents do much of the work

## What this adds up to

Inference, not source statement.

1. The durable decentralised groups (Constellation, Berkshire, Halma) keep the same short list at the centre: capital, the choice and pay of each firm's head, audit, and a seat on the firm's board. Everything else sits in the firm. Their centres are tiny and resist standing reports. None of them rebuilds how the acquired firm does its work. Danaher does, and offers the only long-lived precedent for a shared operating system, but its public material says little about decision rights. So the AI roll-up has no clean precedent: it wants Constellation's autonomy and Danaher's intervention at once.
2. The one measured account of an agent-heavy organisation (Anthropic on itself) shows coordination work growing: more meetings, more agent-management, thousands of standing instructions, and attention as the limit. Block's flat design is a proposal. Budget for a management system that rations attention.
3. Regulators, a standards body and vendors agree on the mechanics: each agent has its own identity, a named business sponsor, system-enforced limits, approval only at significant or irreversible points, and audit of whether approvers actually check. Two results warn that human sign-off is weak as a check (rows 21, 26).
4. In licensed work the only authorised example still uses step approval.

## What is missing

- Any measured comparison of error rates under step approval against limits and budgets.
- Any account of monthly reviews, budgets or escalation inside an AI roll-up or a services firm with many agents. All agent-era evidence here is from software and technology firms.
- Monthly review packs and operating metrics of Danaher or Halma in their own words.
- Partnership governance in a mid-sized professional firm after outside investment.
- A practised scheme of money budgets for agents inside a firm. I found proposals only.
- Managers' span of control over people in an agent-heavy firm.
