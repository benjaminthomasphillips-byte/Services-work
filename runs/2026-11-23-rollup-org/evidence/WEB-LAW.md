# WEB-LAW: what may lawfully and contractually be recorded and delegated

Outside research, run 23 November 2026. UK and EU first, United States for comparison. All rows were read as retrieved passages around the quoted words ("part") unless marked otherwise. No row rests on a search snippet alone; where only commentary could be reached, the row says so. "Ben's positions" means: record full context (Q4), list and automate the work (Q1), the four requirements including a verifier (Q2), and the open vehicle question (part 2).

## (a) Monitoring and recording employees

### R-WEB-LAW-01
- Claim: UK law permits monitoring workers, but only by the least intrusive means that meets a stated purpose.
- Kind: rule or law (regulator guidance)
- Quote: "Data protection law does not prevent you from monitoring workers, but you must do so in a way which is compliant with data protection requirements." And: "You must be clear about your purpose and select the least intrusive means to achieve it."
- Where: https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/employment/monitoring-workers/data-protection-and-monitoring-workers/ (page undated; first issued October 2023 by my recollection; page says it is "under review" because of the Data (Use and Access) Act)
- Speaker and interest: ICO, the regulator; no commercial interest.
- Read: part
- Bears on: 7, 10, 11, 13; Q3, Q4
- Against Ben's starting positions: limits "full context". Recording must be tied to a purpose and be proportionate.

### R-WEB-LAW-02
- Claim: Employee consent is not a sound legal basis for recording, and legitimate interests weakens where staff would object.
- Kind: rule or law (regulator guidance)
- Quote: "consent is not usually appropriate in the employment context, due to the imbalance of power between you and your workers." And: "Legitimate interests may not be the most appropriate lawful basis if: you are monitoring in ways workers do not understand and would not reasonably expect; or it is likely some workers would object if you explained it to them."
- Where: same ICO page as R-01
- Speaker and interest: ICO, regulator.
- Read: part
- Bears on: 7, 10, 13; Q3, Q4
- Against Ben's starting positions: limits. An employee who "refuses to be recorded" cannot be answered with a consent form.

### R-WEB-LAW-03

- Kind: rule or law (regulator guidance)
- Quote: "Apart from in very exceptional circumstances where covert monitoring is justified, you must inform workers about any monitoring." And: "you should seek and document the views of your workers or their representatives (such as trade unions), unless there is a good reason not to."
- Where: same ICO page as R-01
- Speaker and interest: ICO, regulator.
- Read: part
- Bears on: 3, 7, 10; Q3, Q6
- Against Ben's starting positions: limits. Adds a consultation step to day one of any integration.

## (b) Client confidentiality and privilege

### R-WEB-LAW-12
- Claim: The SRA says client data must not be used to train AI models unless explicitly authorised, and that wrongful sharing can waive privilege for good.
- Kind: rule or law (regulator warning notice)
- Quote: "Firms and solicitors should satisfy themselves that client data remains within a secure environment, is not accessed by unauthorised third parties, is not used to train AI models except where explicitly authorised and appropriate to do so, and is not retained longer than necessary". It refers to "the permanent waiver of privilege that can occur".
- Where: SRA, Warning notice: Misuse of AI, 17 August 2026, https://www.sra.org.uk/solicitors/guidance/misuse-ai/ ; underlying rule, Code of Conduct para 6.3: "You keep the affairs of current and former clients confidential unless disclosure is required or permitted by law or the client consents."
- Speaker and interest: SRA, regulator.
- Read: part
- Bears on: 5, 10, 11, 13; Q4, Q5
- Against Ben's starting positions: limits. A playbook improved from one client's matter, then used across the group, needs explicit authority in the engagement terms. The notice speaks of training models; applying it to playbooks is my inference.

### R-WEB-LAW-13
- Claim: The Upper Tribunal said that putting client material into an open AI tool breaches confidentiality and waives privilege, and that closed tools avoid this.
- Kind: rule or law (judicial observation, not a ruling on privilege)
- Quote: "to put client letters and decision letters from the Home Office into an open source AI tool, such as ChatGPT, is to place this information on the internet in the public domain, and thus to breach client confidentiality and waive legal privilege ... Closed source AI tools which do not place information in the public domain, such as Microsoft Copilot, are available for tasks such as summarising without these risks."
- Where: UK and R (Munir) v SSHD [2026] UKUT 81 (IAC), para 21, https://caselaw.nationalarchives.gov.uk/ukut/iac/2026/81
- Speaker and interest: three-judge tribunal panel.
- Read: part
- Bears on: 11, 13; Q7
- Against Ben's starting positions: supports with a condition. Enterprise deployments with contractual confidentiality are treated as safe; consumer tools are not. Commentators (Osborne Clarke, not read) doubt the "public domain" reasoning.

### R-WEB-LAW-14
- Claim: A US federal court held that a client's own chats with a consumer AI tool were not privileged, because the tool's terms allowed disclosure and training.
- Kind: observed result (court ruling, as reported by a law firm)
- Quote: Court, as quoted: "[h]ad counsel directed Heppner to use Claude, Claude might arguably be said to have functioned in a manner akin to a highly trained professional who may act as a lawyer's agent". Proskauer's summary: "the tool's terms permitted the provider ... to use users' prompts and outputs for model training."
- Where: United States v. Heppner (S.D.N.Y., ruling 10 February 2026), reported by Proskauer, 18 February 2026, https://www.proskauer.com/alert/sdny-addresses-privilege-and-work-product-implications-of-using-unsecured-public-AI-tools
- Speaker and interest: law firm commentary; the opinion itself was not read.
- Read: part (of the commentary)
- Bears on: 5, 11, 13; Q5
- Against Ben's starting positions: not applicable to the firm's own tools; relevant to "the client has the same tools". R-13 and R-14 turn on the same point: the vendor's contract terms.

### R-WEB-LAW-15
- Claim: The ABA requires a client's informed consent before their information goes into a self-learning AI tool, even one used only inside the firm, and boilerplate consent does not count.
- Kind: rule or law (ethics opinion, read through a digest that quotes it)

- Where: ABA Formal Opinion 512, 29 July 2024, as quoted in The Bar Examiner, Fall 2024, https://thebarexaminer.ncbex.org/article/fall-2024/generative-artificial-intelligence-tools/ (the ABA's own PDF returned 403)
- Speaker and interest: ABA ethics committee; digest by a legal-ethics academic.
- Read: part (of the digest)
- Bears on: 5, 10, 13; Q4
- Against Ben's starting positions: limits strongly. This is the closest rule found to "using client work to improve the firm's playbooks". It names cross-client leakage inside one firm as the harm.

## (c) AI-produced work signed by a licensed person

## What this adds up to

Inference, mine.

1. Recording is lawful in the UK if announced, purposeful and proportionate. It cannot rest on consent. "Record everything" fails the least-intrusive test; "record these workflows, for these stated purposes, with staff consulted" passes. In Germany and the Netherlands the works council holds a veto, and a French court has shut tools off for lack of consultation. Capture should therefore be designed with staff from the start, and scoring of individuals kept out of it (EU high-risk; emotion inference banned).
2. Client material is the harder limit. The SRA, the ABA and two courts all turn on the same things: the vendor's contract terms, and explicit client authority for anything that learns across clients. Playbook improvement from client work needs an engagement-letter clause that is specific, not boilerplate.
3. No UK regulator requires approval at each step. All require a named licensed person who remains accountable, a tool vetted before use, and supervision scaled to risk; Mazur accepts sampling for routine work. Insurers may ask for more than the law does.
4. Ownership is open for law in England and Wales, for non-audit accountancy and for broking (with approval). It is closed for audit everywhere and for law in Germany. The US and UK answer is the same: a separate licensed entity served by an investor-owned services company. That favours a design where the centre owns platform and people and contracts with the licensed firm.
