# WEB-OWNERSHIP: ownership, capital and incentives in roll-ups of professional and local services firms

Outside research, run 23 November 2026. British spelling.

How to read this file:

- Row numbers have gaps (05, 07, 13, 16, 24, 29 were cut for length).
- The shared web-search allowance for the run was used up after five searches. Everything after that came from fetching known or linked URLs directly. Coverage is therefore uneven; see "What is missing".
- PDFs (FRC, CMA, Accountancy Europe, Knights) were converted to text and read directly. Quotes from them were checked against the text.
- HTML pages and SEC filings were read through a fetch tool that returns extracted passages. Quotes from those are as the tool returned them and should be re-checked before they are published. "Part" under Read means I saw extracted passages, not the whole page.
- Wikipedia rows are marked "summary only". They point to the underlying sources, which I did not open.
- Ben's starting positions concern how work is handed to AI and continual improvement (Q1 to Q4). Most ownership evidence does not bear on them directly, so most rows say "not applicable". Where a row limits the idea of an organisation that keeps improving for many years, it says so.

### R-WEB-OWNERSHIP-06
- Claim: Across Europe the standard private-equity structure splits the firm in two, with an investor-owned non-audit company supplying staff and services to an auditor-owned audit firm under an administrative services agreement; funds expect to leave in three to seven years.

## B. Wealth management

### R-WEB-OWNERSHIP-17
- Claim: The fullest disclosed seller-economics design buys only part of each firm's earnings, gives the buyer first claim on that part, and leaves the sellers the rest plus a share of growth through a management company they own.
- Kind: company claim about its own contracts (audited filing)
- Quote: "The selling principals form a management company." "In economic terms, we typically purchase 40% to 60% of the partner firm's EBPC." "Through the management agreement, we create downside earnings protection for ourselves by retaining a preferred position in Base Earnings." Worked example: target earnings $3.0 million, 60% bought. If earnings fall to $2.5 million the buyer still gets $1.8 million and the sellers' fee drops from $1.2 million to $0.7 million. If earnings rise to $3.5 million the buyer gets $2.1 million. Management agreements typically run six years; non-competes "typically have five-year terms".

## What this adds up to

Inference, not source statement.

1. Legal form follows the licence. Where a profession reserves control (audit, US law outside Arizona, US dentistry), groups split into a licensed firm owned by professionals and an investor-owned service company joined by a services agreement. That agreement is also how shared staff, premises and technology are charged. It is the only charging mechanism I found documented (R-06, R-09, R-27). Regulators now look through it (R-01, R-08).
2. Debt is where the regulators' findings concentrate. The FCA found regulated firms guaranteeing holding-company debt and groups solvent only on goodwill (R-19). CBIZ's interest bill tripled in one deal (R-10). The collapses on file (Tenon, Slater and Gordon, Ince) were listed, acquisitive and leveraged or over-paid (R-15, R-31, R-33).
3. Sellers are held by structure more than by price: a retained share of earnings above a preferred slice (R-17), shares paid over three years (R-10), earn-outs of one to three years (R-22, R-30), half the equity at practice level (R-27).
4. Fund ownership sets a clock of roughly four to seven years (R-06, R-26). "Permanent" is a claim the holder's own owners can undo (R-18, R-12).
5. In vets the roll-up gain came from price, and ended in binding remedies (R-25, R-26, R-28). A design whose return depends on raising prices at acquired firms has a documented regulatory ending.
6. Broad staff ownership is common in the brokers that have lasted (R-22, R-23). I found no evidence that it causes the lasting.
