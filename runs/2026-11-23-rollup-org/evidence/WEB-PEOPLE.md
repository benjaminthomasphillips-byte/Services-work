# WEB-PEOPLE: people in AI-heavy services firms

Outside research, run on 23 November 2026. 33 rows.

How these were read. Web pages were read through a fetch tool that returns extracts and quotations from the page, not the raw page. Quotes are as the tool returned them and some are sentence fragments. Four PDFs (NALP, KPMG forward-deployed paper, Hosseini and Lichtinger, Accenture transcript) were converted to text and read directly. "Read: full" means the whole page or the relevant section was fetched. "Part" means a paywall or a partial read. "Summary only" means I saw only a search-engine summary and did not open the source; treat those rows as leads.

## A. Junior hiring: what the figures show

### R-WEB-PEOPLE-01
- Claim: In US payroll data, employment of 22 to 25 year olds in highly AI-exposed occupations is about 19% below that of the same age group in less-exposed occupations, and the gap works through less hiring, not more leavers.
- Kind: observed result (descriptive; the authors say not causal)
- Quote: "Employment among workers ages 22–25 in highly AI-exposed occupations now stands about 19% below where it would be"; "The adjustment appears to operate primarily through reduced hiring of young workers rather than increased separations."; "descriptive patterns, not causal estimates of the effect of AI."
- Where: https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/news/canariesaug26/ (12 August 2026; revision of an August 2025 paper that reported 13%)
- Speaker and interest: Brynjolfsson, Chandar, Chen, Stanford Digital Economy Lab. Academics; data from ADP. No commercial position stated.
- Read: full (lab summary page; the paper itself not read)
- Bears on: 7, 15; Q6
- Against Ben's starting positions: supports "junior work goes first" (Q6). Limits any claim of wholesale displacement: "We do not see widespread, economy-wide job displacement".

### R-WEB-PEOPLE-02

- Where: https://raw.githack.com/s-mahdihosseini/Website/b99fa2962dcc4c165bbc3c41dd283afa99c5ccfb/GenAI_SenBTC_May_2026.pdf (version of 6 May 2026; first version 31 August 2025)

## What this adds up to

All of this section is inference.

1. Junior hiring fell where AI exposure is high, through fewer hires, not dismissals (rows 01, 02, 06, 08). The size is modest so far and causes are mixed with a weak economy, low attrition, apprenticeships and offshoring (rows 04, 07, 09, 10). A roll-up should plan for a thinner junior layer, not for none.
2. The only rebuilt routes found (rows 15, 16) share one design: teach the task by hand, then have the junior check the machine. None has results. Row 17 says half of juniors do not improve on the agent, so "a person checks it" needs a tested checker.
3. Incentives in the wild reward usage (row 19) or still reward hours (row 22). One scheme pays for reusable contributions (row 20). I found no scheme that shares the value of removed hours with the person whose know-how removed them. That part of the design rests on judgement.
4. Recording is the sharpest finding. Meta's attempt met organised resistance and forced a pause control and exemptions (rows 28, 29). A third of workers say they hold knowledge back (row 30).
5. In professional firms the measured complaint is slowness and shadow use, not refusal (rows 31, 32). Mandates look less needed than approved tools, credited time (row 21) and a stated use for saved hours (row 33).
6. Back-office roles are the ones firms have actually cut (rows 12, 13).
