# Review of draft v1 from the capability sceptic's seat

23 November 2026. Reviewer read only `design/draft-v1.md`. Seat: hold the design to what models demonstrably do in production in late 2026, not to forecasts.

## Verdict

The draft is honest that nothing is measured, but it still sizes the organisation (a resident, an engineer, three cases, a centre of five, a capacity sale, a job pledge) for a mode of working that has not been shown anywhere: agent as maker, with review time falling and errors flat. What is shown today is narrower: drafting with a person as maker, and unattended work only where an outside figure or deterministic code can confirm the result. The draft's own evidence (86% deference, 92.8% on designed traffic against 22.3% in production, changes that passed tests and degraded a live product for weeks, a convinced owner with "shockingly poor" results) describes failures the design would repeat, because its gates are scored on paper, its stop rule can be passed on the easiest case, its error measurement cannot see rare or late-surfacing errors, and its fallback to hand work disappears the moment the freed capacity is sold. The design should be rebuilt around "assist only, every unit fully reviewed" as the base case, with handover as an exception earned case by case on live traffic.

## Objections, ranked by how much they would change the design

### 1. Review time cannot fall under full review except by the deference the draft says it is fighting (Thesis "weakest premise"; Part 6 "The check, designed"; Part 8 dispute 1; Part 14 stop rules)

The draft says that at firm A every filed return is reviewed by a person and "the gain must come from faster, better-supported review". It also says reviewers follow the agent 86% of the time and no remedy is tested. These two cannot both hold. The "blind-first" screen hides the agent's reasoning but still shows the agent's output, which is the anchor. A review that is truly independent of the output is a re-performance, and costs what the work cost. So a fall in review minutes is as likely to be the failure as the success, and the first stop rule ("review time has not fallen on any case") rewards it. It can also be passed on VAT returns alone, the one case with an arithmetic tie-out, while accounts preparation and bookkeeping, where the hours are, show nothing.

What would have to change: make "assist only, full review, no review-time saving" the base case for the people plan, the centre size and the capacity sale. Restate the stop rule as hours-weighted across all three cases and conditional on an error measure with stated power (objection 4), not on "any case". Treat a fall in review minutes with no change in seeded-error catch rate as a warning, not a pass.

### 2. The "hand over with a designed check" bucket (52% and 49% of hours) assumes checks that mostly cannot be built (Thesis point 4; Part 6 item 4; worked example scores)

An independent check exists only where there is an outside ground truth (a bank balance, a registrar, a filed figure) or the task is deterministic enough for separate code to recompute it. Most of what goes wrong in accounts and bookkeeping is classification, omission and stale assumptions: the wrong VAT treatment, a missing invoice, capital booked as revenue. Recomputation by separate code reproduces those errors exactly, because it starts from the same classified inputs. A second model as checker is not independent; it shares the first one's blind spots. The draft admits a check exists today on 14 of 87 lines at firm A and that the score "ranks; it does not measure", then uses the 52% as the shape of the opportunity.

What would have to change: split the bucket in the inventory by type of check: (a) outside ground truth, (b) deterministic recomputation that covers the error classes that actually occur, (c) only a person or another model. Count only (a) and (b) as candidates for handover; move (c) to "assist only". Item 4 should score Y only when the check is shown to catch the real error classes found in the re-performance sample, not when a tie-out exists. Expect the handover share to shrink sharply, and re-run the economics on that.

### 3. Handover is decided by a desk score, not by performance on unfiltered live traffic (Part 6 task test and status rule; Part 10 "Full context")

The draft cites the 92.8% against 22.3% result and says an inventory "scores the line as designed, not the traffic as it arrives", then makes the handover decision from thirteen Y/P/N scores. The two items that address the known failure, complete inputs (5) and a test set from real traffic (9), are not gates. "Inputs usually arrive whole" is exactly the wording that produced the 22.3%. Worse, the draft's answer where state is in calls or memory is that "the agent keeps to the part of the case it can read". The recorded failure is that an agent with partial state does not know it is partial and completes the work confidently.

What would have to change: make items 5 and 9 hard gates. Replace "no N: hand over now" with a required shadow run on all traffic as it arrives, for a stated period, with a pass threshold written beforehand and reported separately for incomplete-input cases. Add an abstention measure: the share of deliberately incomplete or contradictory cases the agent stops on and routes, rather than finishes. No handover on a case where that has not been measured.

### 4. The error measurement cannot detect the errors that matter (Part 9 measure 2; Part 6 seeded files; Part 14 stop rules; experiment 2)

Three problems. First, base rates: if the true error rate on a line is a few per cent, showing that it "has not risen" needs hundreds of blind re-performed files per case per period, each costing as much as the original work. Nobody has budgeted this, and a 30-person firm may not have the volume in ninety days (VAT is quarterly: one return per client per test). Second, seeded errors are written by people and look like human slips; agent errors are fluent, plausible and of a different kind (invented figures, a rule applied to the wrong client, a prior-year value carried forward). A reviewer's catch rate on seeded files says little about catch rate on real agent errors. Third, the costly errors in tax and accounts surface in an enquiry years later. The month 12 to 18 reading cannot see them, so "errors reaching clients" will read as near zero either way.

What would have to change: state the sample size and cost of blind re-performance per case and put it in the P&L as a standing cost. Build the seeded set from the agent's own real errors harvested in shadow, refreshed each model change. Give no stop-rule pass on "errors did not rise" unless the sample could have detected a stated rise. Hold a reserve, and tell the insurer, for the tail that will not show for years.

### 5. Untrusted inbound content meets outward acts, and the first cases contradict the principles (Part 8 principle 3; worked example cases A1 and B1; Part 5 "an agent may book and chase")

Principle 3 says anything outward in the firm's name needs a person's approval each time. The first client case at firm A is an agent chasing clients for records under "written contact limits", and Part 5 lets agents "book and chase". Either each message is approved, and the saving is small, or the principle is already broken in case one. At firm B the repair case has an agent reading residents' and contractors' emails, photographs and invoices and then placing orders within a spend limit. Nothing in the design treats inbound content as hostile. Instructions planted in documents and emails remain an unsolved, routinely demonstrated failure of agents with tools; a contractor's invoice or a "resident's" email is the obvious route. Photo confirmation of completed work is trivially faked. And triage has an asymmetric error the dip sample finds too late: a gas, fire or damp report classed as routine.

What would have to change: add a hard gate to the task test: an agent that reads content from outside the firm has no tool with outward or money effect in the same run without a person between. Resolve the principle-3 contradiction in writing: say which templated outward messages may go unapproved and why. For triage, route anything safety-related or uncertain to a person at once and measure recall on emergencies, not average accuracy. Add an adversarial test (planted instructions, altered invoices, staged photos) to the pre-release review in Part 8.

### 6. Model churn is faster than the design's measurement cycle (Part 10 column 1; Part 11 "versions pinned"; Part 15 "As models improve")

The plan rests on pinned versions, a fixed test set and a quarterly or per-release re-test. Rented models are retired on roughly yearly notice, so the first firm's annual cycle will span at least one forced change; the month 12 to 18 reading compares different systems against one baseline. Behaviour also shifts without a named release: the draft's own source records changes that passed tests and degraded a live product for days to weeks. A test set of a few dozen past files per case cannot detect a drop of a few points, and outputs vary from run to run. The Part 15 language runs one way only: lines "move from assist to hand over". The draft's other source, a fall from 84% to 51% between versions, is filed under "what to expect" and has no mechanism attached.

What would have to change: make continuous sampling of live output the primary monitor, with re-testing on release as secondary. Set a minimum test-set size per case tied to the smallest regression the firm needs to see, and run each test several times. Write the downgrade path: what moves a case back from "hand over" to "assist", who decides, and within how many days. Plan the first cycle's measurement on the assumption of a model change in the middle.

### 7. The fallback to hand work is promised four times and removed by the business plan (Part 8 spend cap; Part 5 and principle 12 client opt-out; Part 13 vendor dependence; Part 10 "fall back to people"; Part 14 gains)

The design's safety rests on being able to do the work by hand: at the spend cap, on a model regression, on a signatory's stop, on a client's opt-out, or if the insurer says "all". Its return rests on selling the freed hours as more clients per person and on avoided hires. Once that is done there are no hours to fall back on, and the stops will bunch at the statutory peak, when a missed deadline is a penalty and a claim. Skill also decays: reviewers and bookkeepers who have not prepared a file for two cycles are worse at both doing and checking. The trainee route addresses new entrants, not the existing staff.

What would have to change: cap sold capacity at a stated fraction of freed hours until a peak season has been met with a deliberate outage drill (the workflow switched off for a week in the busy period). Price the unsold reserve as a standing cost in Part 14. Require every reviewer to prepare a share of files by hand each cycle, and count that time.

### 8. The build schedule contradicts the record the draft cites (Part 4 sequence; Part 7 centre table; Part 11 build list)

One practice engineer in year one is to build, with practitioners: three cases, their checks in separate code, test sets from past files, the per-client permission model (about 1.5 engineers over months in the one source), connectors to the firm's suite, a review queue that may need building, cost controls, and the quarterly re-tests. The sources say one tax domain took six weeks of engineering and practitioner review, the best-resourced roll-up's first deployment took more than a year, and a convinced owner got poor results. Three live cases by day 365 is a forecast that current tools make this fast; nothing on file shows it.

What would have to change: plan one case to live handover in year one, with the other two in shadow, or fund two engineers and say so in the centre cost. Buy, not build, the permission model or defer every case that needs it. Move the month-twelve review's test from "three cases live or stopped" to one case with a full measurement.

### 9. The measures are fed by the thing being measured (Part 9 measures 4 and 5 and "agents' part"; Part 6 item 13; Part 10 trace reader)

Agents "class the exceptions", prepare the weekly P&L and cash note, and "supply data" to the board. Recurrence, the draft's chosen proof that the loop works, depends on corrections being classed as error, preference or stale value by reviewers working against a timer, or by an agent. First-pass acceptance rising is what a nodding reviewer produces. A trace-reading agent proposes changes from traces whose quality nobody audits. The management system therefore reads well in exactly the cases where it has failed, and model-written summaries of figures are a known place for small invented or transposed numbers.

What would have to change: figures that reach the board come from the system of record by ordinary code, never from a model's summary. Exception and correction classes are audited by a person on a sample each month, with inter-rater agreement reported. Drop first-pass acceptance as a headline measure, or show it only beside the blind re-performance result.

### 10. Local acceptance and the portability test both overstate what carries (Part 3 shared-change row; Part 10 "One, five, thirty"; Part 15 firm-two test; experiment 5)

Experiment 5 runs firm one's check and playbook on firm two's past files. Past files are complete, cleaned and closed: this is the designed-traffic condition that scored 92.8% before production scored 22.3%. The test will pass more easily than the live reality and will then thicken the centre "on evidence". Likewise, local acceptance of a shared change against each firm's own small test set will not detect a fault of a few points, so the draft's named top risk at scale, a shared change wrong in many firms on one day, has no working defence; the draft says it "cannot show they are enough". A model-level fault is correlated across every firm regardless of local acceptance.

What would have to change: run the firm-two test on live incoming work in shadow for a full period, not on past files, and set its threshold on that. Stagger shared releases across firms by weeks with live sampling between stages, not by test-set acceptance. Do not let all firms sit on the same model version at the same time during a peak.
