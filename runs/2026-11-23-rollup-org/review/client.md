# Review from the client's seat

Reviewer seat: a client's finance director or general counsel deciding whether to keep buying, demand a share of the saving, or take the work in-house with the same tools; also the small-business owner (firm A) and the leaseholder (firm B). Reviewed: `design/draft-v1.md` only, read in full, 23 November 2026. Points of UK law marked "my knowledge" are not from the draft and need checking.

## Verdict

The draft designs the organisation for its owner, its sellers, its staff, its insurer and its regulator, and gives the client three things only: a notice, a named signatory and an opt-out. Everything the design gains is taken as volume at held prices, and everything it risks in the transition (a year of diverted managers, new error modes, a fallback to people whose hours have been sold to someone else, a thin firm-level insurance policy behind a ring-fenced parent) is carried by the existing client, who is not paid for carrying it. From this seat the disclosure the design rightly insists on is the evidence I need to ask for a lower fee, and the draft has no answer prepared except "the head decides". The leaseholder is worse placed: the design treats "the payer is not the buyer" as a reason the saving is kept longer, which is the same weakness the draft's own vets evidence shows ending in regulation. I would keep buying advice and accountability from a firm like this, ask for a written share of the saving on the three converted cases at my next renewal, and I could not be shown, from this document, why I should not get one. The design needs a client side: a stated proposition, a give-back rule, a service floor, a reserve of human capacity, consent before use rather than at renewal, and a claim route that reaches past a 30-person company's policy.

## Ranked objections

### 1. There is no client proposition, and the saving split is left to "the head decides" (Part 5; Part 14; Thesis, second-weakest premise; Part 15 Q5)

What is wrong. Part 5 sells "what the firm sells now, at the prices it charges now" and routes the gain to new clients. The existing client gets no faster turnaround, no service level, no fee change and no stated benefit, while being told in writing that AI now does the preparation. The draft's own evidence says one in five clients already use AI to check their accountant's work and that visible cost falls are followed by price or volume falls (chain 6). Disclosure plus held price is an invitation to renegotiate, and the only response designed is "if a client asks for a lower fee on evidence, the head decides". "The plan assumes some of the gain goes back at renewal" appears three times and is never sized, never put in Part 14, and has no rule. Thirty heads improvising thirty answers also means clients who ask get a cut and clients who do not ask subsidise them, which is the pattern that drew remedies in vets.

What would have to change. Part 5 needs a written client offer per converted case: what the client gets (turnaround, a fixed response time, a fee step, or a price lock for a stated term) and a give-back rule decided in advance, not on request. Part 14 needs a row for fee given back, with a scenario where the three converted cases reprice within two renewals. The second-weakest premise should be restated as "freed hours become sold capacity while existing clients on the same work are asking for the saving", because those happen in the same firm in the same year.

### 2. The leaseholder is the payer the design relies on not being able to object (Part 1 trade choice; Q8 table "payer-is-not-buyer … delays the question further"; firm B worked example)

What is wrong. The design counts the leaseholder's lack of a contract as a commercial advantage. But the leaseholder pays every pound of the management fee and every repair order, and is not powerless: service charges must be reasonable and can be challenged at tribunal, blocks can change agent through right to manage, and the draft itself records leasehold reform landing in 2027 and admits statutory regulation of managing agents was "not searched" (my knowledge on the first two; needs checking). Disclosure and the AI opt-out go to the client of record (the freeholder or residents' company board), so the people whose money, homes and personal data are being processed are told nothing and can object to nothing. The repair case lets an agent place orders "within the client's limit" with triage checked by dip sample: that is committing leaseholders' money without a person approving each act, which sits badly with principle 3 ("anything … that moves money"), and a mis-triaged leak, lift or fire-safety report is not reversible and is not something a sample catches. A run of within-limit orders on one building can also add up to works that require statutory consultation (my knowledge: the section 20 threshold; needs checking), and the limits as designed are per order, not cumulative.

What would have to change. Treat the leaseholder as a party in Parts 5, 8 and 13: notice to residents, a resident-facing route to a named person, and a rule that any safety-related report is triaged by a person. Limits in the repair case must be cumulative per building and per period, with a hard stop below the consultation threshold. Part 14 for firm B should assume the fee is contestable on reasonableness once AI use is disclosed, not that the question is "delayed". If the answer is that block management only works while leaseholders cannot see the saving, the trade should be dropped as a second trade.

### 3. The fallback is "people", and the people's hours have been sold (Part 5 "serve a client the old way"; Part 8 spend cap; Part 10 "fall back to people"; Part 13 vendor dependence; Part 14 stop rules)

What is wrong. Every failure mode in the design ends in the same sentence: the work falls back to people. A signatory stop, a spend cap reached, a pinned model withdrawn, a shared playbook rolled back, a client opt-out, and the month-twelve stop rule itself all assume spare human capacity that can do the work by hand. The whole economic plan is to sell that capacity to new clients and to move bookkeepers from making to rule-writing, with trainees learning on closed past files. In a deadline trade the stop will come in the peak month, and the cost of a late filing lands on the client as a penalty. Nothing in the draft says how much capacity is held back, whether anyone still practises the manual route, or who pays the client's penalty. "Stop building and run the firm as bought" is also not available after roles have changed and the engineer has gone: the draft does not say who keeps half-converted workflows running for clients in that case.

What would have to change. A reserve rule in Part 9 or Part 14: a stated share of freed hours that may not be sold until a manual fallback drill has been run in a peak period. A rehearsed hand-working drill per case, as principle 9 already demands for undo. A client-facing undertaking that penalties and interest caused by a stop or an agent error are met by the firm without the client having to claim. A wind-down plan for each case as part of the stop rules.

### 4. My claim stops at a small company's policy while the group that built the check stands behind a wall (Part 2 structure; Part 13 risk table; Part 10 thirty-firm note)

What is wrong. Liability is placed "in the firm and its signatory", the firm is a separate limited company with its own cover (£2m minimum, excess up to about £75,000 on the draft's model), no guarantees flow from the holding company, and the services company that writes shared checks is liable only "by contract with each firm". The draft notes that PI wordings commonly exclude cases where recovery rights were limited by contract and that this "bears on every contract in the chain", then leaves it. The design's own named worst case is a shared change "wrong in many firms on one day": that is many clients claiming against one aggregate limit at once, at a firm whose partners have been cashed out and put on salary. No insurer has been asked anything. As general counsel I would not accept this without knowing whether the limit is per claim or in aggregate, whether AI-prepared work is covered at all, what run-off cover exists if the firm is closed or sold, and whether the parent stands behind it.

What would have to change. Part 13 needs a client-side row: what a client can recover, from whom, in a correlated error. Either a holding-company guarantee of client claims arising from shared items, or group excess cover sized to the correlated case, or a rule that no shared item runs until the firm's insurer has confirmed cover in writing. The engagement terms must not cap the firm's liability in a way that voids its own cover. Run-off cover should be a non-negotiable.

### 5. AI use begins before the client is told, and client files are used for purposes the client never agreed to (Part 4 sequence; Part 5 "at the next renewal"; Part 6 item 9; Part 10 "what may be shared"; principle 7)

What is wrong. Ownership is disclosed on day one, but the AI clause goes into engagement terms "at each renewal", while the three cases (records chase, VAT returns, bookkeeping) go live in days 100 to 365. For any client whose renewal falls later, their material is in the tools before they have been told or given the chance to object. That contradicts principle 12 and, for firm B, the RICS requirement the draft quotes: "in writing and in advance". Separately, test sets are built "from the firm's own past files" and playbooks are improved from corrections on live client work, while principle 7 says client material "serves that client only". The draft admits the lawfulness of even own-firm use "is argued by analogy". Existing engagement letters and data-protection terms will not name model suppliers as sub-processors; the only data-protection assessment in the plan is for staff logging. Nothing says what happens to my material in test sets when I leave.

What would have to change. No client's material enters any AI tool until that client has had written notice and a period to object; enforce it as a per-client flag at the gateway, not as a renewal clause. A client-data assessment and updated processing terms in days 1 to 100, before the rehearsal. An explicit ask, separate from the AI notice, for use of past files in test sets, with removal on exit. Until that is done, the firm-level test sets should be on the same "consented or synthetic" footing the draft already applies to cross-firm sets, and the build timetable should be lengthened to match.

### 6. A prospective buyer and an unowned firm are given my files (Part 4 diligence test 3; experiment 1; experiment 5; Part 2 "who owns the know-how"; events table)

What is wrong. Diligence includes "re-performance of a sample of past files" and a practice-system export, done by "the resident-to-be" before exchange, when the buyer is a stranger to every client. Experiment 1 does the same at "a willing firm" before anything is bought. Experiment 5 runs firm one's playbook over an unowned firm's past client files "with consent", without saying whose: the firm's consent is not the client's. From this seat that is my accounts, payroll and tax affairs shown to an investor's staff under no duty to me. Separately, the draft says the firm owns "its client-specific rules" and that when a partner leaves with clients "client rule files stay with the firm". Those rule files are a written description of how my business runs, built partly from my staff's answers. I get no copy and no portability, which turns my own operating knowledge into the firm's hold over me.

What would have to change. Diligence on the work must run on anonymised or client-consented files, or be done by the firm's own staff under the buyer's instructions with only results passed over; say which. Experiment 5 needs client-level consent or synthetic files stated as a condition, not left to "legal advice". Client rule files should be the client's to take on leaving, in a readable form, and the engagement terms should say so. This weakens the "what is left" list in Q5 and should be recorded there.

### 7. The three cases chosen to convert first are the three most easily taken in-house or bought from the software vendor (Part 15 Q5 closing paragraph; firm A worked example; Part 5 "when the client has the same tools")

What is wrong. The draft's answer to in-house substitution is that small clients "do not want to", backed by one line about AT&T and volunteer directors. That covers the leaseholder board. It does not cover the firm A client with a finance director or a competent bookkeeper, and the draft's own finding is that nothing outside the audit opinion is reserved by law. Records chase, VAT returns and bank-feed bookkeeping are the work the client's own ledger software is already automating, and the planned capacity sale, quarterly reporting, is more of the same. "Offer it first where it helps … and hold the accountable part" is not priced: if I take the portal and the live ledger, what am I still paying a fixed bundle fee for? The draft names "a cheaper rival or the software vendor" as the nearer threat and then designs nothing against it. The only measure that would show it is fee retention, which lags by a renewal cycle.

What would have to change. The light inventory in diligence should score each line for how easily the client or the client's software can do it, alongside the task test, and the client book should be segmented by who has the means to insource. Part 5 needs a price path per service that separates preparation (which will reprice) from sign-off, agent status and advice (which may hold). Part 14 should carry a case where the converted lines lose a stated share of fee while the accountable lines hold, and the case selection in Part 6 should be tested against it: the draft may be spending its first year building on the revenue least likely to stay.

### 8. Year one is paid for in client service, and nothing measures service as the client sees it (Part 9 eight measures; "What each party would be agreeing to"; Part 14 stop rules; events table "an agent's error reaches a client")

What is wrong. The draft says year one costs "a year of extra effort from managers and seniors", moves partners to selling, makes managers workflow owners and has bookkeepers writing rules. Those are the people who answer my calls. The eight measures contain errors, complaints and retention, all of which report after the damage; none measures turnaround, response time or deadlines met, and the ninth-measure rule blocks adding one. The draft itself warns that faster preparation with unchanged review "moves the queue to managers", which the client experiences as later accounts. The stop rules are all the investor's: review time, capacity sold, conversion cost. Client loss "clearly above the firm's own history" proves the design wrong in one table but triggers nothing. When an error reaches a client, the signatory "tells the client and corrects it"; nothing says other clients touched by the same fault are told, or who pays.

What would have to change. Swap a measure or split one so that turnaround and deadlines met are reported monthly per case, baselined in days 1 to 100. Add a client stop rule: handovers pause if turnaround or client losses pass a threshold set beforehand. In the error event, require the re-review "of work done since" to end with notice to every affected client and a stated remedy.

### 9. "A named person reviews and answers for it" is promised to every client while the plan depends on that person spending less time, and the opt-out is hollow (Part 5 "what clients are told"; Part 3 last row; Part 6 "the check, designed"; Part 8 dispute on sampling; Part 7 reviewer pay)

What is wrong. The client notice says a named person reviews the work. At firm B the design samples volume outputs, so for most items that statement is untrue as worded. At firm A every return is reviewed "until the insurer and the institute say otherwise", and the success test of the whole design is review minutes per unit falling while each person serves more clients. I am told nothing about whether my file was reviewed in full, sampled, or reviewed in a timed queue. VAT returns are "filed only on client approval": a small-business owner cannot check a return, so that approval moves comfort to the firm without moving any assurance to me. The opt-out ("a client may ask for their work to be done without AI") has no price, no service standard and no protection: the firm decides disengagement and pricing, so an opted-out client can simply be repriced or dropped, and the ability to do the work by hand decays as objection 3 describes. Seeded known-error files are also not stated to be kept out of the live filing path.

What would have to change. The notice must describe the actual control per service: reviewed in full, sampled at a stated rate, or client-approved, and say what the client's approval does and does not mean. State the opt-out terms: same fee and service for a fixed period, no disengagement for opting out, and an honest statement if the firm will stop offering it. Keep seeded files in shadow or mark them so they cannot be filed or sent.

### 10. Ownership and group conflicts are disclosed in form but managed for the owner (Part 5 brand; Part 3 "conflict veto"; Part 12 thirty-firm note; Thesis decision 1; Part 1 horizon)

What is wrong. The stated aim is that the group name "should carry weight with sellers and none with clients", which is disclosure designed not to register; the draft's own vets evidence is about clients being unable to tell who owns their provider. Clients are told of the sale on day one, after it has happened, with no right to leave without penalty and no word on onward sale, while the capital decision (long hold or a fund with a clock) is still open and the draft says that with a clock "sellers and staff should be told" but not clients. The centre holds a "conflict veto" over client acceptance, so it must hold a cross-firm list of clients, which sits oddly with "the centre never contacts a client" and with no client being told their identity is held at group level. At thirty firms the draft imagines a group accountancy firm "doing the books of the property firms": for a leaseholder that is a sister company preparing or examining the accounts of the agent that holds my money.

What would have to change. Give clients a penalty-free exit window on a change of control, including any later sale of the group, and tell them the owner's horizon. Say what client information the centre holds and why. Bar group firms from acting as accountant, examiner or auditor on any sister firm's client-money or service-charge accounts, and require related-party disclosure to the paying party for any intra-group supply charged to clients or to service charges.

## What I did not test

I read only the draft. I did not check the cited evidence rows, the inventories or the rival designs, and the points of law marked "my knowledge" (reasonableness of service charges, right to manage, the consultation threshold) need checking against statute before they are relied on.
