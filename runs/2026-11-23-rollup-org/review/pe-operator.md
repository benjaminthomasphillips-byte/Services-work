# Review of draft v1 from the seat of a private-equity operating partner

23 November 2026. Reviewer read only `design/draft-v1.md`. All pound figures marked "my estimate" are the reviewer's own rough arithmetic on the draft's ranges, with assumptions stated. They are not evidence and should be replaced by real numbers.

## Verdict

I would not take this to an investment committee as it stands. The draft asks for a decision on "the size of the bet" (Thesis, decision 3) and then leaves every amount open: price, centre cost, tools, profit forgone, the size of the gain. On my own rough arithmetic the centre costs more each year than the group's share of the first firm's real profit, the first cash arrives no earlier than the second peak season, and the only gains the draft allows itself to count (avoided hires and sold capacity) are too small at a 30-person firm to pay for the centre even if everything works. The design has no comparison against simply buying the same firm at the same price and running it properly, and several of its own rules (no profit-linked pay for the head, held prices, credited non-chargeable time, a job pledge) make the acquired firm a worse ordinary investment than it was on the day of purchase. The honest reading is that this is a research programme with a firm attached. That may still be worth funding, but then it should be sized, sequenced and governed as a research programme: prove the review-time premise under a paid mandate before buying anything, and buy only when the purchase stands up as an ordinary deal.

## Objections, ranked by how much they would change the design

### 1. There is no return case, and the arithmetic the draft avoids looks bad (Part 14; Thesis decision 3; "What each party would be agreeing to")

What is wrong. Part 14 leaves every "after" figure open "on purpose" and the agreement table lists purchase price, centre cost, tool spend and year-one profit forgone as "all open". An investment committee cannot approve an unsized bet. The draft's own ranges allow a first pass, and it is not encouraging:

- Firm A profit is £0.6m to £1.0m before partners' pay. Put three partners on market salaries (my assumption: about £120,000 each, loaded) and real profit is roughly £0.25m to £0.65m. The group's share of that, at a 60% to 75% majority, is about £0.15m to £0.5m a year, before the year-one dip the draft plans for.
- Price at 0.8 to 1.3 times fees of £2.2m to £2.8m is £1.8m to £3.6m for the whole firm; the majority cheque is about £1.1m to £2.7m (my estimate).
- A centre of a managing partner, a resident, an engineer and two part-timers, with tools and advisers, is plausibly £0.5m to £0.8m a year (my estimate), so £0.75m to £1.6m over the 18 to 24 months asked for. That is between about a third of the purchase cheque and more than all of it, and it exceeds the group's share of the firm's profit in every case.
- The countable gain is avoided hires and sold capacity. At a 30-person firm an avoided hire is worth perhaps £35,000 to £50,000. Twenty per cent more clients at held prices is perhaps £0.5m of fees and £0.25m to £0.3m of contribution (my estimate). Neither covers the centre.

What would have to change. Part 14 needs a model with three cases (nothing works, modest, good), in pounds, by quarter, for firm one and for the group at one, three and five firms: cash in, cash out, cumulative cash, and the return on all capital including the centre. It needs a stated hurdle. And it needs the one number that decides everything: how many avoided hires or how much sold capacity per firm per year is required for the group to cover its centre, set against how many hours the "hand over with a designed check" band could plausibly free.

### 2. No comparison against buying well and running the firm properly (Part 14; Part 4 sequence; worked example, firm A)

What is wrong. The draft's own first-hundred-days list is ordinary good ownership: monthly accounts, weekly cash, time recording, stopping unbilled out-of-scope work, chasing clients earlier, a 105-day lock-up to bring down. Part 10 says the first improvements "need no model". None of that needs a resident, an engineer, a head of quality or a register. So the true question is what the AI programme adds on top of competent ownership, and the draft has no arm that measures it. Worse, the design removes ordinary levers: prices are held as a principle (Part 5), the head is never paid on profit in the transition years (Part 7), and there is no mention of the standard capacity answers in a capacity-short accountancy firm (outsourced or offshore bookkeeping, repricing the bottom of the client list, dropping unprofitable clients). The stop rule's fallback, "run the firm as bought", is that control case, reached two years late and after the centre's cost is sunk.

What would have to change. Add a conventional value plan for firm one with its own numbers (billing leakage, lock-up, repricing of under-priced clients at renewal, hiring or outsourcing for capacity) and make it the base case. The AI programme is then judged on its increment over that base, net of its cost. If the conventional plan gives most of the return, the centre should be one operating partner and a part-time controller, and the engineer a contractor hired per case.

### 3. The sequence spends the money before testing the premise the draft itself calls weakest (Thesis, "The weakest premise"; Roadmap; Part 4)

What is wrong. The draft says everything depends on review time falling without errors rising, that tax guidance reads as review of every deliverable, and that no insurer has been asked. It also says experiment 1 can be run before buying, and experiment 5 shows that the check can be run in shadow at a firm the group does not own. Yet decision 3 asks for a majority purchase and a centre for 18 to 24 months first, with the reading on review time at month 15 to 18. That is a multi-million-pound way to run a test that a paid mandate could run for a small fraction of the money.

What would have to change. Make experiments 1 and 2, and the insurer's and institute's written answers, conditions before any purchase. Run them at one or two friendly firms under a paid baseline arrangement, on past files, with a single engineer and the managing partner. Buy only if review minutes fall with errors held on at least one case, and the insurer does not require review of all outputs at a cost that removes the gain. This changes Part 4's order, Part 7's centre at "one firm", and the size of the first cheque.

### 4. The centre never clearly covers itself at the reference firm size and the chosen pace (Part 14 "The centre"; Part 7 table; Part 1 "One, five, thirty")

What is wrong. The draft concedes one firm cannot carry the centre and that nine to eleven people are "tight" on £12m to £17m of revenue at five firms. On my estimate a centre of that size costs £1.2m to £1.8m a year, which is roughly 8% to 12% of group revenue, against the Constellation figures the draft quotes of 3% falling to 0.5%. The group's share of five firms' real profit after partners' salaries is perhaps £0.75m to £2.5m (my estimate), so the centre takes half or more of it. At one purchase a year, five firms is year five or six and thirty is "a decade or more". "At thirty it works if the cap holds" is an assertion with no number behind it. A subscale overhead carried for ten years is not patience; it is the largest cost in the plan.

What would have to change. One of three things, chosen and stated: buy larger firms (fees of perhaps £5m to £10m) so that each carries more centre; or cut the standing centre to two people and make residents and engineers a per-deal cost that ends at month 18; or raise the pace after the first firm with a clear rule for when. Whichever is chosen, Part 14 must give the break-even number of firms and the year it is reached.

### 5. "No debt" does not remove the transition-year problem; it hides it in deferred consideration and fixed costs (Part 2 "Debt" and "Sellers"; Part 14)

What is wrong. Three points.

- Deferred consideration over about three years, paid on retention and milestones "not profit", is a fixed obligation falling due in exactly the years the draft plans for lower profit. Nobody says whether the firm or the holding company pays it, or from what. It behaves like debt without being called debt.
- Year one adds fixed cost inside the firm: partners move to salary, pay steps for owners and reviewers, credited non-chargeable time, training as an explicit line, tools. There is no transition-year cash-flow showing the firm can meet those, the deferred payments and a dividend to a minority holder who used to live on drawings.
- The policy itself is inconsistent: "None on any firm" and, in the next sentence, "the first firm carries none" because transition-year capacity is unknown. An all-equity purchase of a low-growth asset at a fair price gives a modest yield. If the long-run plan involves holding-company debt, say so and say when; if it never does, the return case in objection 1 has to work on yield and growth alone.

What would have to change. Part 2 needs a stated debt policy for the group over time, a schedule and funding source for deferred consideration, a floor on cash cover for it, and a 24-month cash-flow for firm one with the year-one dip in it. Deferred consideration should be counted as leverage when testing that cash-flow.

### 6. Pay and equity are cut loose from profit, and the equity has no route to cash (Part 7 pay table; Part 2 "Sellers"; Thesis decision 1)

What is wrong. The head and signatories are "never" paid on firm profit in the transition years. Deferred consideration pays on milestones such as "three cases attempted" and "baselines taken", which is payment for activity. So the people who run the firm have been paid cash, hold a minority, and for three years bear no consequence if profit falls. Meanwhile workflow owners, authors and centre staff are paid in "group units held about four years" in a company with "no planned exit". Nothing says how a unit is valued, who buys it back, from what cash, or what a leaver gets. The same gap applies to the sellers' minority: no valuation method, no dividend policy, no good-leaver and bad-leaver terms, no put or call. Equity that cannot be turned into money will not hold anyone, and a buy-back promise is a cash call on the group that the model does not show.

What would have to change. Add a profit floor to deferred consideration (measured before group-borne costs, so the head is not paying for the experiment). Write the minority's terms: dividend policy, valuation formula, leaver terms, a put and call after a set period. For group units, either define a yearly internal market with a formula price and a funded buy-back budget, or replace them with cash profit share. Put the cost of all of it in Part 14.

### 7. Stop rules have no thresholds, and there is no downside value or way out (Part 14 "Stop rules"; Part 1 "No planned exit"; Roadmap)

What is wrong. The draft says thresholds are "fixed in writing beforehand; I have not invented them", and the roadmap says the same of every test. A stop rule without a number is a discussion, and the people holding the discussion at month 12 will be the people whose jobs depend on continuing. "Has not fallen on any case" is also a very low bar: one case improving by a trivial amount keeps the whole programme alive. And "stop and run the firm as bought" is not costless: by then the firm has higher fixed pay, a job pledge in writing, an AI-disclosure clause in its terms and a minority shareholder with no agreed way to part. With no planned exit there is also no mark: the committee has no method to say what the holding is worth in year three.

What would have to change. Propose thresholds, even as placeholders to be argued over (for example a minimum percentage fall in review minutes on at least two of three cases with sampled errors no worse; a cap on cumulative centre spend before a pass). Give the decision to someone outside the centre. State the downside case: what firm one is worth, and to whom it could be sold, if the programme stops at month 18, and which of the day-one promises survive a stop. Add a yearly valuation method so "long-hold" still has a scorecard.

### 8. The plan takes the scarcest people out of a firm chosen because it is short of capacity (Part 1 screen; Part 4 days 100 to 365; Part 7; worked example, firm A)

What is wrong. The screen selects firms short of capacity. The programme then asks four managers to become workflow owners, bookkeepers to write client rules, seniors to do blind-first review and seeded-error tests, and partners to sell, all in the first year, around a statutory deadline calendar, with a new quarterly reporting duty landing at the same time. The draft notes "a year of extra effort from managers and seniors" and counts authors' time as chargeable, which means it is not being charged to clients. No practitioner-hours budget is given, no backfill is funded, and the cost appears nowhere. In a capacity-short firm the likely results are missed deadlines, lost clients or burnt-out managers, which are the three things the deferred consideration is meant to prevent. The draft also plans to sell new capacity (the quarterly reporting work) before any capacity has been freed.

What would have to change. Budget practitioner-weeks per case before go-live and fund backfill (contractors or outsourced preparation) as a deal cost in Part 14. Start with one case, not three, outside peak season. Do not commit the firm to new work lines on the strength of capacity that month 15 to 18 has not yet confirmed.

### 9. The deal terms may not win deals, and the price marker is not a price (Part 4 "Sourcing" and "Pricing"; Part 2; "What each party would be agreeing to")

What is wrong. The only price evidence is brokers' marketing at 0.8 to 1.3 times recurring fees, for whole small practices. The draft buys a majority, not the whole, and in a market it describes as crowded with private-equity buyers who use debt and pay earn-outs on profit. Against that, this buyer offers: part cash, deferred payment on milestones, no auction, a resident in the building, a monthly board, disclosure of AI to every client, loss of the audit book, and a minority stake with no exit. Nothing shows a seller would choose that at a price that "works with no AI gain". There is also no normalised-earnings work: the firm's profit is quoted before partners' pay, the audit book leaves (about 5% of hours, plus whatever other work those clients take with them), and the value of the "Chartered Accountants" title is at risk in 2027. "A003's three scenarios" are cited for pricing and never shown.

What would have to change. State the price as a multiple of profit after market partner salaries and after the audit book leaves. Say what majority percentage and what cash-to-deferred split is assumed. Test the offer with three or four real sellers or a broker before the design is fixed, and record what competing buyers are paying. Show the three pricing scenarios in the document.

### 10. Firm B and the second trade should be dropped from the plan, and the dated regulatory hits priced into firm A (Part 14 firm B table; worked example firm B; Part 1 "Which trade first")

What is wrong. On the draft's own account firm B has an 8% to 15% margin on weak markers, loses insurance commission recovery on 1 November 2027, faces leasehold reform across at least eight lines, has 22% of hours that no system can reach, a lettings half where "no gain is kept", and an unknown write path on which "most of the plan" depends. The draft says the reform "matters more to the P&L than any agent". That is a firm an ordinary buyer would pass on, and keeping it as the second trade doubles the trade templates, suppliers and regulatory reading for a centre that is already subscale. Separately, firm A's known dated items (title consultation result in 2027; the quarterly reporting duty as both demand and unpaid workload) are listed as things to "watch" and not priced.

What would have to change. Remove block management from the base plan and keep it as a note for later. Hold to one trade until the centre covers its cost. Put a pound estimate on each dated regulatory item in the firm A case.

## Numbers the draft needs and does not have

- Purchase price, as a multiple of profit after market partner salaries; majority percentage; cash and deferred split and schedule.
- Centre cost in pounds per year at one, three and five firms; UK cost of a practice engineer; tools and model spend per firm.
- Year-one profit dip at firm one, in pounds; practitioner-hours diverted; backfill cost.
- Deal costs, tax and structuring costs; working capital at completion; the size of the one-off lock-up release.
- Fees and contribution lost with the audit book and with clients who follow it.
- Change in professional indemnity premium and excess under AI-assisted work.
- Client and staff loss after a sale, as a planning assumption with a range.
- The size of the prize: hours that could be freed per case, the share that becomes avoided hires or sold work, and the pounds per year.
- Break-even number of firms for the centre and the year it is reached.
- Hurdle rate, a yearly valuation method, and thresholds for every stop rule and every experiment.
- Value and buyer for firm one if the programme stops at month 18.
