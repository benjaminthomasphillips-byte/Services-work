## 1. General, conditional, drop or merge, missing

**General (hold in all or nearly all seven).**
- **G3 and G6 hold in all seven.** They are the architecture's strongest claims.
- **G4** holds in six; it bends only in home care, where half the outcome is not a condition.
- **G1, G2, G8 and G10** hold in five or six. G1 bends where the record is a liability (legal) or its owner dies (probate). G8 bends where the opponent, not the producer, is the threat (legal, claims). They are general, but G1 and G8 need restating for adversarial services.
- **C1 and C2 are triggered in all seven and hold in six.** They are effectively general. Their conditions ("controls or can price"; "several parties rely") were met everywhere, so they should move into the core.

**General in intent, wrong as stated.**
- **G7** bends in five of seven. The duties idea is general. The revenue clause ("rises only when the outcome improves") fails for subscription, per-commitment and per-employee pricing, and the controls needed (firewalls, scope separation, conflicts, privilege) are not stated.
- **G5** holds in six and breaks in home care. It is general for information products, not services whose product is human work.
- **G9** bends in four. It mixes two things: workers who produce the service (lettings, claims, home care, where it holds), and the firm's own pipeline of judges (accountancy, legal, probate, claims), which it does not cover.
- **G11** bends in three. Accountancy flattens demand rather than shaping capacity to it, legal demand is lumpy, and probate's time shape is decades. It should cover the long tail as well as peaks.

**Conditional, correctly.** C4 (holds wherever triggered; correctly off in legal), C10 (holds in home care), C11 (holds in home care), C12 (holds in four of five triggered), C9 (holds in probate and home care).

**Conditions wrongly stated.**
- **C7** is too narrow. Lettings (one boiler model, a bad winter) and payroll (correlated client failure) both face correlated demand that is not physical, and both meet it with capital, not crews. It should read "correlated demand or loss, physical or financial", sized for a bad year.
- **C8** is too narrow. Accountancy's analogue is licensing to peer firms. Probate's is the family's own AI. Where the condition was met, the firm usually worked *inside* the client's function or sold layers, rather than licensing (lettings, claims, home care).
- **C9** is too broad. "Acts on a person's life" triggers in legal (dismissals), payroll and claims, where the firm cannot or should not hand pace to that person. It should apply where the person is the principal and in crisis or decline.
- **C10**'s title says "public" but its condition says "the payer gains from less service". The insurer in claims meets the condition. Drop "public".
- **C2**'s rule "platform company only when the record has one owner" is ambiguous where each record has one owner but the platform serves many (accountancy).

**Drop or merge.**
- Merge **C10 into G7**. Separating finding need from paying is a control on parties with opposed interests, as the claims firewall shows.
- Merge **C4 into G8** as an evidence rule: pool only what cannot be traced.
- Merge **G11 with C7** into one element on demand in time: peaks, seasons, correlated events, long tail.
- Restate **C5** (section 3). Triggered seven times, it holds only twice.
- Promote **C1 and C2** to core.

**Missing (needed by several designs, covered by no element).**
1. **Adversarial design.** This means counterparties, opponents and fraudsters as designed-for parties, and an adversarial edge as a permanent kind of human need. Legal (the counterparty as growth channel, imposed paper), claims (fraud, credit hire, claimant's agent) and payroll (siding with the employee against the client) all need it.
2. **The record as a liability.** This means a privileged or protected layer, and rules on what the operational record may say given compelled disclosure. Legal (two-layer record) and claims (baseline-use rule) need it, and so do probate and home care where beneficiaries or payers read.
3. **Succession of judgement.** Agents close the school where judges learned. Accountancy, legal, claims and probate each build a training route (blind cases, writing tests, exception books), and home care builds an academy.
4. **Capital for physical and credit promises, and correlated loss.** Lettings, claims and payroll each need a balance sheet, a reserve and an insurer from day one. C1 covers only guarantees.
5. **The review rule.** Whether insurers, regulators and courts accept sampling plus independent recomputation decides headcount in all seven designs. It is a structural dependency, not a detail.
6. **Structural separation of the firm from its own platform or software.** Accountancy splits the platform company from the firm, home care spins out a neutral operating system, and legal has the endpoint versus the standard. Conflicts with peers and procurement drive it.
7. **Law reform as part of the product.** This appears in probate (statutory nominations, a will proved in life), home care (capitated contracts), accountancy (trusted status with HMRC) and payroll (agreements as code with social partners).

## 2. Does the two-part reach claim hold?

**The claim:** the architecture is the whole design where the product is information, a decision, money or legal status, and only the support layer where the product is a human relationship.

**First part: holds with qualification.** Accountancy (10 H, 5 B), payroll (15 H, 4 B) and probate (14 H, 4 B) are designed almost entirely by the architecture. Legal, the purest "decision and legal status" product, bends most of the four (7 bends). It bends on G1, G7 and G8, the elements that assume a cooperative world, and it needs privilege and counterparty design the architecture lacks. So "whole design" is too strong where the product is legal status contested by an opponent. The revenue clause of G7 also fails even in information services.

**A third category is missing.** Lettings and claims are neither pure information nor relationship. Their products are a physical thing kept right: a home, a restored house or car. The architecture holds 13 times in each, about as well as for information services. But the physical edge becomes owned core (technicians, turn crews, drying crews), and the firm needs a balance sheet. The claim says nothing about such services.

**Second part: half right, and understated.** In home care the architecture does not produce the service: G5 breaks, and G2 and G4 bend. But it is more than a "support layer". With its conditionals (C9, C10, C11, C12) it held 14 times. It shapes commissioning (independent band review), pricing (per person-week), workforce (G9), observation rules (G8), the market (G10) and the daily curve (G11). What it does not design is the relational act itself. A fair statement is that the architecture is the whole *governance and economics* of a relational service but not its *production*.

**Restated reach claim:** "Whole design where the product is information, money or uncontested status; whole design plus owned physical capacity and capital where the product is a physical thing kept right; whole design minus adversarial and privilege rules where status is contested; and the governance, payment and workforce design, but not the production, where the product is human care."

## 3. The three weakest elements, and how I would restate them

### 1. G7 Duties to every party, and controls on the firm (bends in 5 of 7)

**Why it is weak.** "Revenue rises only when the client's outcome improves" is not true of any design's main price: tiers, per commitment, per employee-month, per policy-year. The designs replace it with narrower bans: no float, no less care, no income from declines, form-neutral pricing. The element also says nothing about the controls the designs actually needed when the firm both judges and earns: claims' scope separation and fairness firewall, legal's conflicts and privilege, accountancy's platform separation. And it treats non-payers as secondary where home care makes the non-payer the principal.

**Restated:** "The firm names every party the service affects and the duty owed to each (agent, employer, neutral, advocate, opponent), ranked by law where law ranks them; where the person served is not the payer, the person is the principal. Where the firm both judges and earns from the result, the judging is separated from the earning and sampled independently. No price rewards what harms the client or a ranked party: no income from holding money, from less care, from declines, from the client's exit or from keeping it in an unsuitable form. Outcome-linked pay is used only for what the firm controls."

### 2. C5 Mandate chain with portable proof of authority (triggered 7 times, holds 2)

**Why it is weak.** It is built on probate's grant, and it holds only there and in payroll's employer transfers. Elsewhere the designs needed authority as *bounds* (legal's policy envelope, investor consents), *delegation* (claims' delegated authority, home care's council and nurse delegation) or *capacity* (home care, probate attorneys). "Chain" and "portable" fit none of these. Its condition triggers almost everywhere, so the element is general in need but narrow in form.

**Restated, and moved to core:** "Authority as a record. Who may act for the client, on what, within what bounds, granted by whom and until when, is held in the shared record and signed by whoever can grant it: the client, an attorney, a payer delegating assessment, a principal delegating claims authority, the members of a company. Counterparties can rely on it without a fresh paper token. Where they will not yet, the firm buys them the same assurance (an indemnity) and argues for the law to recognise it."

### 3. G9 The workforce as a designed condition (bends in 4 of 7)

**Why it is weak.** It is core but worded as a condition ("where workers sit on the client's critical path"). It is about the wrong workforce in half the designs. Accountancy, legal, probate and claims all had to design the succession of their *own* judges, because agents close the training ground. That is general, and G9 does not cover it. Where workers do produce the service (lettings, claims, home care) G9 holds, but it is conditional.

**Restated as two elements:**
- **G9 (core), Succession of judgement.** "Agents remove the work through which judges were trained. The firm designs and funds a route to judgement (blind cases, writing tests, resolving maker and verifier disagreements, a small book of exceptions under supervision) in every landscape, and agrees with the professional bodies that it counts."
- **C-new (conditional, where production is human or physical), Producing workforce.** "Supply, contracts, pay and skill are designed to the shape of demand, and owned where speed or continuity decides the outcome."

**Runners-up.** G5, which breaks once, needs the qualifier "where the product is not itself human work". C8's condition misses peer firms and clients' AI, and the designs work inside clients' functions more often than they license.
