# Counter-review: the view from an independent estate and lettings agency

**Reviewer:** owner of an independent UK estate and lettings agency, four branches, about 60 staff, Propertymark member. I have traded through the online-agent wave, the portal years and the Renters' Rights changes.

**Reviewed:** `step-1-the-engine.md` and `step-2-what-is-left.md`, 24 November 2026.

I am not an AI person. I read these as someone who has watched three waves of "this will kill the high-street agent" come through. Two of them did real damage and one fizzled. Where I say something is from experience, I mean I have seen it in my own office or my competitors'. Where I say "from memory", I have not checked the figure. The only figures I did check were the Renters' Rights dates and Rightmove's 2025 numbers.

## Where they are right

More of it rings true than I expected.

- **Carrying the risk while controlling the work (Q4)** is the best idea in the papers, and my industry has run on it for decades without naming it. "No sale, no fee" is a warranty: we carry the risk that the deal dies. We price that risk from our own judgement of which buyers, chains and solicitors will get there. The online agents broke this bundle. They took an upfront fee whether or not the house sold, so the vendor carried the risk. In my experience that was why vendors in a slow market came back to us. The papers predict this would fail, and in my patch it did.
- **The robo-advice counter (step 2, section 3).** The incumbent with distribution adopts the technology and wins. That is the Purplebricks story, more or less. A lot of national advertising bought market share, and the share stalled. From memory, the business was sold to Strike for a nominal sum in 2023. Meanwhile, the rest of us took up the useful parts: online booking, instant valuations, digital ID checks.
- **Subscriptions to maintained conditions.** Lettings management is that already. It is the part of my business I would never sell cheaply, because it is recurring, it is sticky, and it does not depend on the housing market.
- **Correlated error (step 1, section 6).** One bad template across thousands of listings or tenancy files is exactly how agents get into trouble. I come back to this below.

## Where it does not survive contact with my industry

### 1. "Clients' AI attacks diagnosis first." We have had that experiment for over a decade.

In agency, the "diagnosis" is the valuation. The credence problem is overvaluing: an agent quotes high to win the instruction, then "reduces" the price six weeks later. That is real, and it is the trade's worst habit. But vendors have had free automated valuations from the portals and others for well over ten years, and most of them check sold prices before we arrive. It has not stopped them picking the agent with the highest number. They are not short of information. They want to hear good news about their biggest asset. The papers assume a second opinion reduces over-servicing. In my experience a buyer of a credence good who is emotionally invested will often use the checker to justify the answer they wanted. Where client AI does change things is the fee conversation, not the valuation one. Vendors arrive knowing what three other agents charge.

### 2. The portals already are the "supplier of the checker", and the papers barely mention them.

Q3 treats the new gatekeeper as something that may emerge. In property it emerged twenty years ago. Rightmove's own 2025 results put average revenue per agency advertiser at about £1,530 a month, up 6% on the year. That bill rises every year whatever the market does, and every agent I know resents it and pays it. Portals do not just carry listings. They sell us valuation leads, they are building AI valuation and search tools, and they will be what buyers' agents query first. Any AI-native agency that sells homes pays this toll. The papers list "portals" once, in the Q7 table, as a distribution owner taking a share of commodity services. In my world, the portal is where most of the margin from the efficiency gains will end up, unless something displaces it. Several well-funded attempts have failed so far: OnTheMarket made the most serious try. Any plan for estate agency has to start with how it deals with the portals.

### 3. The physical and emotional work is not a parameter to add at the end.

"The share of coordination and physical work" appears as the seventh item in a list in step 2, section 4. In agency it is most of the job: viewings, keys, the valuation visit, the inventory, the boiler that fails on a Friday night. Above all, it is sales progression. Getting a chain of five households, five solicitors and three lenders to exchange on the same day is coordination under uncertainty, with people crying on the phone. From memory, something like a quarter to a third of agreed sales fall through. That is the real cost in my business, because we carry it under "no sale, no fee". An AI that chased solicitors, read searches and flagged a weak link early would be worth more to me than one that writes listings. Listings are already a solved problem and nobody pays for them.

### 4. The outcome signals are faster than they think, but the counterfactual is the trap.

Step 1 worries that services lack fast signals. Agency has plenty: viewing booked, offer, sale agreed, fall-through, exchange, completion. Lettings has arrears, voids and deposit disputes. All of them arrive within weeks or months, so we are quite checkable. What we never see is the counterfactual: would the house have sold for £15,000 more with a different price strategy? The engine will learn to get sales agreed quickly, which is the measure that is easy to see. Quick sales are easy to buy by underpricing, which suits the agent and the buyer at the vendor's expense. P1's warning about optimising the proxy is correct, and in my trade it has a direction: towards the agent's interest and away from the vendor's.

### 5. Regulation and redress are missing as a force that shapes the market.

The papers talk about licences in general. Here is what actually shapes my firm:
- compulsory redress schemes and client money protection;
- anti-money-laundering supervision by HMRC;
- Trading Standards enforcement of material information in listings;
- consumer protection law, which since the 2024 consumer act lets the CMA fine directly (from memory, not checked for agents specifically);
- the Renters' Rights Act. Section 21 ended on 1 May 2026, the transitional window closed at the end of July, and the private rented sector database and landlord ombudsman come in the next phase.

Sales agents are still not licensed. The 2019 working group on regulating property agents recommended it, and as far as I know it has never been implemented. If it ever is, step 2's argument that "licences become less scarce" works the other way in my sector: a licence would create a qualified tier that does not exist today. That is a policy bet the investor should watch rather than assume.

Two more points follow from this. Error is visible: an AI-written listing that misdescribes tenure or flood risk is a Trading Standards matter with my name on it. And in lettings since May, a correlated compliance error across a managed portfolio means rent repayment orders and fines across hundreds of tenancies at once. That is the scandal step 1 predicts. It will happen in lettings first.

### 6. Home-buying reform is the "state and software" case they want, and they missed it.

Q5 uses tax as its example. Property has its own case. Upfront material information is already required in listings. Industry schemes for digital property packs and shared data standards are building up, and the government has consulted on reforming home buying and selling: more information up front, possibly binding early commitments. I have not checked where that consultation stands. If it goes through, much of what conveyancers and agents do in sales progression becomes structured data that software providers and the Land Registry handle. That shrinks the pool for agents and conveyancers alike. It also creates exactly the "view above the systems" (step 2, point 4) that nobody holds today: the whole chain's status in one place.

### 7. The talent window looks different here.

Agency staff are mostly paid on commission, and staff turnover is high. When the mid-sized chains cut back, experienced negotiators do not queue for jobs at a new firm. They go self-employed on one of the hybrid "agent platform" models, keeping a large share of the fee. That is step 1's "engine hosts independent professionals", and it has been tried at scale in the UK for several years with mixed results, from what I have seen. They are cheap to start and hard to keep consistent, and quality control is the weak point. The papers should study it as a test that has already run.

## Where we will be in 2035, and whether the framework predicts it

This is my guess, not knowledge.

- **Branches.** Fewer high-street branches, but not none. Local presence still wins instructions, and somebody has to hold the keys.
- **Sales fees.** Squeezed more slowly than the framework implies. Transactions are lumpy, emotional and infrequent, so vendors do not build the comparison habit that drives Q1. The framework gets the direction right and the speed wrong.
- **Lettings.** Consolidated much further. Renters' Rights compliance is pushing small landlords out and turning management into a regulated compliance service. Lettings books will trade at multiples of recurring income, as they already do. The framework predicts this well: maintained conditions, risk bundled with operations.
- **Value capture.** The portals and the conveyancing and data platforms take more of it, unless reform moves it to the state. The framework points that way through Q3 and Q5 but does not name the actors. In property they are already known.

## What I would back

I would not build a new consumer-facing AI estate agency. It would fight the portals, the local brands and the hybrid platforms, and the online-agent wave showed that cheap marketing does not win instructions.

I would back three things:

1. **A lettings management and compliance operator, built by buying books.** Buy lettings books from retiring independents. There will be many, as Renters' Rights pushes them out. Run the compliance work on software: certificates, deposits, database registration, possession grounds, repair timetables under the new standards. Then sell landlords a warranty covering compliance and rent, priced from your own arrears and dispute data. This is step 1's P3 and step 2's Q4 in a market where it fits.
2. **A sales progression service sold to agents and conveyancers, not to the public.** It would hold the chain's status, chase every party and predict fall-throughs. It takes the risk we carry and makes it smaller. I would pay for it per completion. Many independents would, and it does not compete with the portals.
3. **Hold off on sales agency itself** until agent licensing and home-buying reform are settled. Both could rewrite who carries accountability in a sale.
