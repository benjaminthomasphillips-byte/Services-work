# Counter-review: the view from a freight forwarder

**Reviewer:** commercial director of a mid-sized European freight forwarder (about 300 staff; air, ocean and road; customs brokerage). I have run tenders against the global forwarders, watched the digital forwarders raise money and then cut back, and paid for AI pilots on quoting and document capture. Where I am speaking from experience I say so. Where I am guessing, or quoting figures from memory, I say that too.

---

## Where you are right

I will start with the parts I agree with, because they are better than most of what I am pitched.

Your own freight example is the most honest line in either paper: a quoting workflow that scored 92.8% on synthetic tests and 22.3% on real requests. That matches what I have seen. Our quoting pilot did well on clean requests and fell over on the real ones. A real request reads like "same as last time but 3 pallets, ready Thursday-ish, can you do DAP Lyon". There is no HS code. The weight is gross on one line and net on another, and the incoterm is wrong. The client being a co-producer (step 1, section 5) is not a side issue. It is most of the job. Half of what my customer service team does is pulling a complete instruction out of a shipper who does not know what one looks like.

You are also right that the middle gets squeezed, that the hard tail concentrates on the remaining people, and that errors become correlated. A bad classification rule applied to every line of a big importer's catalogue is exactly the scandal I would expect. Q5 is right that the state takes value in mandated work. That is happening to customs right now (see below). And the warning about robo-advice, where incumbents with distribution adopted the technology and won, is the most important sentence in step 2 for my industry.

## Where it does not survive contact with freight

**1. You assume the firm sells work. A forwarder mostly sells access to capacity it does not own.**
Your model is a firm that does cases and is paid for them, by the hour, by the unit or for an outcome. Most of a forwarder's gross profit is the spread between what we buy from carriers and what we sell to shippers. The rest is ancillary charges and customs entries. That spread is set by the carrier market, not by our efficiency. In 2021–22, when ocean rates went through the roof, forwarders made record profits with no change in how well we worked. When rates fell back, the profits went with them. This is my experience, and the listed forwarders' published results showed the same pattern. An engine that lowers my cost per shipment by 30% matters less to my P&L than one bad year in the carrier cycle. Your framework has no cycle in it. Any strategy for my industry that ignores capacity cycles will be wrong about margin.

**2. "Carry the risk and control the work" breaks, because we do not control the work.**
Q4 says the margin is in bundling risk with control of the operation. In freight, the carrier sails the ship, the airline flies the plane, the haulier drives the truck, and the port, the weather and the customs authority decide the rest. A forwarder coordinates other people's assets. Our liability is limited on purpose. On road it is capped under CMR at 8.33 SDR per kilogram. Air and sea have their own convention limits, and our own trading conditions sit on top. That is from memory, but any forwarder will confirm the structure. Cargo insurance is a separate, commoditised product, and we mostly resell it. If I "warranted" transit times, I would be underwriting carrier schedule reliability, which I cannot drive down with any engine. The parties who can bundle risk with control are the asset owners. That is why the carriers have been buying their way into logistics. From memory: CMA CGM bought CEVA and later Bolloré's logistics arm, MSC bought Bolloré Africa Logistics, and Maersk pushed end-to-end logistics. Your Q4 predicts the right winner for the wrong reason. It is the operator with assets, not the operator with an engine.

**3. Concentration in freight is driven by buying power and network, not by the fixed cost of evaluation.**
P4 says evaluation is a fixed cost that favours scale. That may be true, but it is not why my industry consolidates. The big forwarders win tenders because they buy carrier capacity at volume, have their own offices at both ends of most lanes, and can offer one global contract to a multinational's procurement team. DSV's purchase of DB Schenker, which I believe closed in 2025, is the latest step. Nobody in that deal was buying test suites. If you back an AI-native forwarder on the theory that evaluation scale makes winners, you will lose to a company that simply has more boxes on more ships.

**4. Digital forwarders have already tested your thesis, and the result was sobering.**
Flexport raised billions and was valued at about $8 billion in 2022. Public reporting has it cutting about 20% of staff in late 2023 and about 15% in early 2024, with the founder talking about getting back to profitability. Others in the European digital-forwarder cohort restructured or were absorbed. My read, which is opinion: better software did not change the unit economics, because the spread is set by carriers and shippers. Shippers bought on price in calm markets and moved when service failed in rough ones. Paying below cost to win customers did not hold them. Your Strategy A, pricing routine work near cost to win records, is exactly what was tried. A shipper's records are not hard to move: the bookings live in their ERP and the carriers' systems as much as in ours.

**5. The hard cases arrive in waves, not as a steady stream.**
Step 1 treats exceptions as a measured flow you can learn from case type by case type. In freight, the hard tail is bursty and everything goes wrong at once: Suez in 2021, the Covid congestion, the Red Sea diversions from early 2024, tariff shocks. In those weeks the work is allocating scarce space, rerouting, and phoning carrier contacts who owe you favours. A holdout set built in a calm quarter tells you nothing about a crisis quarter. Crisis performance is also when shippers decide who keeps the contract at the next tender. Your evaluation design needs a crisis regime, not just more holdouts.

**6. You miss working capital, and in my business that is the money.**
We pay carriers, hauliers and often duty and import VAT up front, through deferment accounts and customs guarantees. Shippers pay us at 60 to 90 days, and procurement teams push for longer terms in every tender. Credit risk, customs guarantees and cash are a large part of what a forwarder's balance sheet is for. A new entrant needs capital for this long before it needs capital to back warranties. Payment terms are a weapon in tenders, and a well-funded entrant could use them. That is a more realistic use of investor capital than underwriting outcomes.

**7. The "point of need" and the "live records" are already owned, by the software vendor.**
Q7 and strategy point 4 talk about holding the client's live records. In forwarding, the operating system for a very large share of the industry is one vendor's: WiseTech's CargoWise. It holds the shipment records, the customs filings and the accounting for us and for many of our competitors, and it sets its own prices. Its pricing changes have caused open complaints from forwarders. Your framework does predict this, under rule-required software and distribution owners. It should go further: for anyone building in my industry, the first strategic question is your relationship with that vendor, not your engine.

## Customs: where your framework fits best

Customs brokerage is the part of my business that looks most like your accountancy examples, and here I think you are broadly right.

- **The routine tier is collapsing on schedule.** A standard import entry is already priced as a commodity. The EU customs reform, finally approved this year, sets up a single EU customs data hub. It starts with e-commerce in 2028 and is due to cover all goods by 2034, from the published timetable. E-commerce platforms become the "deemed importer", and the €150 duty exemption for low-value parcels ended in July 2026. That is your Q5 exactly: the state collects the data upstream and the filing step loses value.
- **The accountable layer is real.** When we act as indirect representative we are jointly liable for the duty. Authorised Economic Operator status, classification, preferential origin, and now CBAM, sanctions and dual-use screening are where judgement and liability sit. New mandated work is arriving faster than the state automates the old work, which is your own counter-argument in Q5, and in customs it is winning.
- **Your outcome signals are weak here, as you warn.** A customs audit can come three years after the entry. "No query" is the absence you describe in step 1. You will learn far more from post-clearance audits and binding rulings than from accepted declarations.

## Where we will be in 2035 (my guess)

- Quoting, booking, tracking and document capture are fully automated and cost almost nothing to provide. Nobody earns a margin on them.
- A handful of global forwarders and carrier-owned logistics arms hold most of the contract freight of large shippers.
- Mid-sized forwarders survive only as specialists: perishables, pharma, project cargo, particular trade lanes, or customs-heavy work. Many are bought for their client books, as Q9 says. I agree they should be priced as customer acquisition.
- Customs brokerage splits. Filing is folded into the data hub, the platforms and the software. A smaller, better-paid trade compliance profession carries liability for classification, origin and sanctions.
- People: fewer clerks, and more experienced operators and customs specialists who are genuinely scarce. Your talent window (Q8) matches what I see. Good customs people are already hard to hire.

Your framework would get the squeeze and the customs story right. It would get the winners wrong, because it does not weight asset ownership, buying power and the capacity cycle.

## What I would build if I were the investor

1. **Not a new forwarder.** The spread belongs to carriers and the big networks.
2. **An AI-native trade compliance operator.** It would act as indirect and fiscal representative for mid-sized importers and for the e-commerce platforms that are about to become deemed importers. It would hold AEO status and carry the liability, priced from its own error and audit data. This is your Strategy B, the accountable partner behind the owner of distribution, and the 2028 data-hub deadline gives it a real starting date. The risk is that platforms take it in-house. The defence is that platforms do not want joint liability for duty across 27 member states.
3. **Buy mid-sized customs brokers for their client books and their licences and guarantees**, then move their filing onto the new system.
4. **Possibly an exceptions desk sold to mid-sized forwarders**, but they will not hand shipment data to anyone who might become a competitor, so it has to be credibly neutral.

For diligence on any forwarder or broker, I would add to your three tests: gross profit per shipment across a full cycle, quote win rate and turnaround, days sales outstanding, claims and customs-penalty history, and performance in the last disruption.

Everything else is from my experience or memory, and is marked as such.*
