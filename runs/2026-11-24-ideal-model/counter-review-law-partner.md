# Counter-review: a senior partner at a large UK commercial law firm

*About 25 years in practice; head of a practice group; on the management board. I deal every week with general counsel who are building their own AI teams. I have sat through the LPO wave, "NewLaw", the listed-firm experiment and two or three rounds of "the robots are coming". Where I rely on memory rather than a source I say so. I did no fresh research for this.*

---

## Where they are right

The three-product split in step 2 is the most useful thing in either paper. Routine execution, diagnosis and residual judgement really do behave differently in law, and the authors are right that the judgement premium survives because someone credible carries the consequence, not because the client can't see the work. The point that the cost of the check sets the floor (step 1, section 4) is exactly what we see: on AI-assisted due diligence, the cost that remains is the senior lawyer reading the exceptions and signing the report. The warning about correlated error is right and underrated. And the remark in step 2 that incumbents with distribution may simply adopt the engine and win (the robo-advice point) is the one I would underline for any investor. That is what is happening in my part of the market now.

## Where it does not survive contact with how law actually works

**1. The buyer already diagnoses.** Q2 says the client's AI attacks diagnosis first, so less work gets bought. In consumer and small-business law, perhaps. In commercial law the buyer is a general counsel, who is a lawyer and who has always decided what work goes out. Over-servicing exists, but it shows up as too many associates on the file and too many turns of the document, which the GC already polices through budgets, panel terms and write-downs. The real threat to us isn't a second opinion. It is insourcing: GCs now have their own AI tools and are keeping the middle of the work, such as first drafts, contract review, routine regulatory questions and first-pass disclosure. Step 2 mentions AT&T in passing. In my world it is the main event, and the framework ought to treat the in-house team as the main competitor, not as a buyer with a checker.

**2. Nobody in commercial law wants a warranty, and the bundle of risk plus work already exists, held by insurers.** P3 and Q4 propose guaranteeing outcomes, priced from the firm's own error data. Three problems. First, a contested legal outcome can't be warranted: it depends on a counterparty, a judge or a regulator. Second, what clients buy from us is not indemnity but cover. "We took advice from a leading firm" protects the board and the GC's career. That is reputational and social, and no balance sheet replaces it. Third, where transactional risk is underwritten, it is done by warranty and indemnity insurers, placed by brokers, priced from the diligence report that the law firm writes. The law firm supplies the evidence. It does not carry the risk, and it does not get the premium. Conveyancing is similar: title indemnity policies are sold by insurers. So in law, the "integrated operator" of Q4 has largely already been built, and it is an insurer using lawyers as suppliers. The authors should look at that before assuming the services firm captures the bundle.

**3. The one place law does bundle risk and work is litigation, and they miss it.** Conditional and damages-based fee agreements, after-the-event insurance and third-party litigation funding are exactly "carry the consequence, priced from the best data". It is also politically fragile: from memory, the Supreme Court's PACCAR decision in 2023 threw funding agreements into doubt, and the Civil Justice Council recommended legislation to reverse it in 2025. I don't know whether that has passed. If I were applying the authors' P3 to law, this is where I would look: claims assessment and funding at volume, not warranties on advice.

**4. Cross-client learning runs straight into confidentiality and conflicts.** P7 flags privilege and data protection, which is good, but understates it. Every large client's outside-counsel guidelines I have seen in the last two years restrict using their material to train or improve tools, and some ban it outright. More important, we act for competitors, and sometimes for both sides of a market. An engine that learns from Client A's negotiating positions and applies them on Client B's deal against A is a conflicts and confidentiality problem under the regulator's code, not just a contract point. Information barriers exist for exactly this. The "owned process data" in P7 has to be stripped of anything that reveals a client's position, and in high-end work that leaves less than the authors think.

**5. Volume is thin where the money is, and the volume work has been tried.** P1 says the engine needs many cases per case type. Commercial law has a few high-volume streams: NDAs, lease reporting, KYC, disclosure, and remediation projects (LIBOR transition, Brexit contract repapering). Those went to legal process outsourcers, alternative providers and firms' own lower-cost centres in Belfast, Manchester and elsewhere fifteen years ago. That moved the low-margin work to a cheaper cost base; the profit stayed with the partners running the complex matters. The authors' P3 predicts that result correctly. But it also means an investor building the engine in commercial law is entering the lowest-margin corner of an already industrialised market.

**6. The regulator decides who may do what, and the reserved activities matter more than "sign-off".** P6 imagines a licensed signatory signing for a system. In England and Wales some activities are reserved, including conducting litigation. As I understand it, the High Court's 2025 decision in *Mazur* held that unauthorised staff cannot conduct litigation even under supervision. It was under appeal when I last looked, and it caused real alarm in volume litigation firms. If supervised paralegals can't do it, an AI system certainly can't conduct litigation on its own account. The courts have also shown their teeth over AI-generated fake citations (the Divisional Court's *Ayinde* judgment in 2025, from memory). The direction of travel is towards more personal accountability for each output, not less. Step 2's "compressed accountability" (one signature for a whole system) is the opposite of what I see.

**7. Published outcome records won't happen in commercial law.** Step 1 and Q3 both rely on firms publishing agent-readable track records. Win rates are meaningless because firms choose their cases and most disputes settle confidentially. Deal outcomes are confidential by contract. The certification layer the authors predict already exists: the legal directories, panel reviews and GCs' networks. That layer is subjective, slow and enormously powerful. AI may make panel reviews more data-driven, but the gatekeeper will be the client's procurement function, not a public ratings agent.

**8. The partnership's economics are the real story, and neither paper engages with them.** Our profit comes from leverage: partners' margin on associates' hours. AI hits leverage, not price. So far, in my experience, AI gains have shown up first as better realisation: work that we used to write off now gets billed at its true value. Hourly rates have kept rising. "Gains pass to clients under hourly pricing" is true eventually, but the lag is longer than the authors think, because GCs buy on relationships at panel-review cycles, not file by file. And partnerships pay out almost all their profit every year. We do not keep the capital needed for the fixed-cost evaluation engine in P4, and we certainly don't have a balance sheet to warrant anything. That is the opening for an outside investor. It is also why the history of outside capital is poor: from memory, the listed claimant firm Slater & Gordon collapsed after an aggressive UK acquisition, DWF went from listing to a private-equity take-private, and the Big Four's legal arms have grown much more slowly than predicted for twenty years.

## What they miss entirely

- **The US.** The biggest legal market does not allow non-lawyer ownership outside a few states (Arizona and Utah are the usual examples). A 2035 "market leader" in law built on outside capital is mostly a UK, Australia and selected-states story. Q9 says licences cap scale; in law the cap is ownership, which is harder.
- **Professional indemnity insurance.** It is compulsory for solicitors, renewed every year, and the insurer will want to understand any system that produces advice at scale. A correlated error across thousands of matters raises awkward questions about how claims are aggregated under the policy. The insurer, not the regulator, may be the first to set limits on autonomy.
- **Supply of work caused by AI and regulation.** The authors size the pool shrinking. In my practice, AI regulation, data disputes, AI-related product liability and the employment consequences of automation are already growing. Q6's elasticity point applies: some legal demand grows as a direct result of the technology.

## Where commercial law will be in 2035

This is a guess, from experience. The elite firms are still there, more profitable, with fewer associates per partner and pay for the top people even higher. The squeezed middle has been bought up, partly by private equity and partly by accountancy-led groups, and sells fixed-fee packages. Volume consumer and SME work (conveyancing, debt, simple employment, wills) is mostly done by technology-led firms with a few authorised people, plus insurers and banks embedding it. In-house teams are larger and more technical, and buy specialist advice and managed services rather than a generalist panel. Legal publishers and AI tool vendors hold a large slice of the value. The framework predicts the middle and the volume tier correctly. It underestimates how much the top end is protected by social cover, and it overestimates how far warranties and published outcomes will spread.

## What I would back

1. **Not a challenger to the elite firms.** You can't buy the cover they sell.
2. **A managed legal operation sold to in-house teams on subscription**, structured as an alternative business structure: it runs the GC's contract estate, regulatory tracking and routine advice through an engine, with authorised lawyers on the judgement desk. It sits inside the insourcing trend instead of fighting it, and the subscription gives it the "do less work for the same outcome" incentive the authors rightly favour.
3. **Claims assessment and funding at volume**, paired with a partner insurer and funder: the one place in law where carrying the risk and controlling the work already make money together.
4. **Property transactions and lender work** in partnership with title and indemnity insurers, where the outcome is checkable (registration complete, no claim) and volume is high.

In each, I would hire experienced lawyers from squeezed mid-sized firms, as Q8 suggests. That part of the talent argument is right. I would also bet that the shortage of trained judgement in the 2030s will be real, because juniors learn by doing exactly the diligence work that is going away.
