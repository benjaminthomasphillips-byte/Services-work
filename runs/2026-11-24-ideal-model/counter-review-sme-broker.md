# Counter-review: the managing director of an independent UK SME insurance broker

*About 40 staff. Commercial combined, fleet, professional indemnity and cyber for small and mid-sized businesses. FCA-authorised. I have had more than one approach from a consolidator and turned them down, so far.*

Read on 24 November 2026. Where I say "I know", it comes from running a brokerage. Where I say "I think" or "I guess", it is opinion. I have not quoted market statistics I can't source. One regulatory point I checked: in December 2025 the FCA's policy statement PS25/21 redrew the line for which commercial customers get ICOBS, PROD and Consumer Duty protection. It now uses the DISP thresholds for SME eligible complainants, so most of my clients are still inside the Duty.

---

## First, what they get right

A lot of this reads like someone has sat in a broker's back office. In order of importance:

- **Correlated error is the scandal of the decade (step 1, section 6).** My industry has already lived through it. When COVID hit, one family of business-interruption wordings went wrong for thousands of policyholders at once. It needed the FCA's test case to sort out. One wording, copied across a market, failed everywhere at the same moment. If an AI-native broker pushes one flawed "recommended cover" playbook to 20,000 SMEs, that is the same event again. The authors' remedies are the right ones: staged releases, limits on how many clients a change reaches, and a second verifier on anything signed.
- **Carrying risk on its own earns the cost of capital, and the margin is in the bundle (Q4).** That is the history of my market over the last ten years. The money has gone to managing general agents (MGAs) and to brokers who own them. They control underwriting and distribution and rent the capital. Without naming it, the authors have described the MGA model.
- **Conformance is not quality (P1).** A renewal can pass every internal file check and still leave a client underinsured for years. The real outcome shows up only at a claim, and for most SMEs that is rare.
- **Slow-checked layers keep the margin (P3).** Yes. Claims advocacy and getting a client fairly presented to insurers are where we earn our keep. The certificate and the mid-term change are not.
- **The incumbent with distribution may win (step 2, section 3).** I'd put it more strongly, below.

## Where it doesn't survive contact with how broking works

**1. The papers assume the firm sets the price. In broking the insurer does, and the client never sees our cost.**
Most of my revenue is commission, a percentage of the premium, paid by the insurer. Add some fees, premium-finance income and, for some, profit share on a scheme. The client sees one number: the premium. If AI cuts my cost of handling a renewal by half, the premium doesn't move, because my cost isn't in it in any visible way. Q1's mechanism, where cost falls and comparing buyers turn that into a lower price, is muffled twice in my world. Insurers set the commission rate. The insurance cycle moves premiums far more than broker efficiency ever will. My revenue rose in the hard market of recent years without my doing any more work. In a soft market it falls without my doing any less. A framework that tracks cost per case will misread my economics. Revenue follows rates and retention, not effort.
*What would change:* model revenue as premium × commission rate × retention, with the cycle as the main variable. Treat cost per case as margin, not price.

**2. In insurance the credence risk is under-buying, not over-servicing (Q2).**
The authors' story is that a client's AI asks "do I really need this?" and less work is bought. In my world that is the dangerous direction. The commonest harm I see is the SME that bought too little. The sum insured is too low, so average applies at a claim. Business interruption is under-indemnified. Cyber is missing. Something wasn't disclosed and the claim is cut back. An AI second opinion trained on "typical practice", which is the conformance trap the authors themselves describe, will tend to cut cover to save premium. The broker who resists that is doing the job properly. The regulator agrees. Under the Consumer Duty the test is whether the product meets the client's needs and gives fair value, not whether it is the cheapest.
*What would change:* split "diagnosis" into over-supply credence goods (repairs, litigation) and under-supply credence goods (insurance, pensions, compliance). In the second kind, the client's AI creates demand for an adviser rather than destroying it.

**3. "Buy squeezed mid-sized firms for their clients, priced as customer acquisition" (Q9) is not the market I live in.**
I know this from the approaches I've had. Private-equity-backed consolidators have been competing hard for books like mine for over a decade, and they pay full multiples of earnings, not distressed prices. I'm not going to quote multiples from memory. The point is that brokers are not "assets whose value is melting". They are some of the most fought-over small companies in UK financial services, because commission income renews with high retention and the consolidator can push it into its own MGAs and facilities. An investor arriving in 2027 to buy books cheaply will be bidding against people with more capital, better panel terms and fifteen years' practice at integration.
*What would change:* the buy-books strategy needs a reason a broker owner would sell to you rather than to the consolidator: price, autonomy, or an equity story. It also needs an admission that you won't get them cheap.

**4. The firm cannot "warrant outcomes" here. The route is capacity, and capacity can be withdrawn.**
P3 and Q4 talk about the firm guaranteeing outcomes, backed by capital or a partner insurer. In my world that means running a delegated authority: an MGA or a binder with capacity from an insurer or Lloyd's. I know two things about this. Capacity providers audit you, cap you and pull capacity when the loss ratio turns. Lots of MGAs that were promised years of capacity lost it after one bad underwriting year. And when a broker places clients into its own MGA, the conflict of interest draws regulatory scrutiny and client suspicion. The "integrated operator" is real, but it holds its licence to trade at the pleasure of whoever supplies the capital, not of the engine.
*What would change:* make capacity security, from several carriers on multi-year terms, a named precondition for strategy B and the bundled-risk thesis. Put the conflict-of-interest governance in from day one.

**5. The evaluation problem is even worse than step 1 says, and the counterparty holds the data.**
Step 1 says many case types have too few cases to see a signal. For an SME broker, the outcome that matters is a disputed or declined claim. For any single client that happens perhaps once in many years. The insurer sees the claims experience across its whole book. Acturis, the dominant broker platform, sees how much of the market trades. I see a sliver. P7 concedes this. I'd go further: in broking, the firm with the best outcome data is the insurer, by a wide margin. So "price the slow layer from your own fast data" largely means "price it from data the insurer already has more of".
*What would change:* the outcome-data asset in broking has to be negotiated with carriers, through claims-data sharing written into the agency agreement or the binder. A new entrant can't build it alone.

**6. Clients don't buy the way a "client's agent compares outcome records" implies, but micro-SMEs already do.**
I know my clients buy on trust, through referral from their accountant or a trade body, and they decide at renewal. They stay because we fought a claim for them. Step 2's segment point (Q1) is right, but it understates how far the split has already gone. The micro end, sole traders and small trades buying public liability and van cover, went online years ago through digital brokers and direct writers. That tier is effectively commoditised already, and the AI-native newcomer is late to it. The mid-sized SME with a fleet, property, employees and a cyber exposure still wants a human who will turn up when the warehouse floods.
*What would change:* treat micro-SME as a market already won by digital distribution and embedded insurance (banking apps, accounting software). Treat the advised SME as where an AI-native operator could still change the economics.

## What the papers miss

- **Fair presentation of the risk.** Under the Insurance Act 2015 a commercial client must make a fair presentation of the risk to the insurer. Gathering that information, and making sure it is right, is the most valuable and most labour-intensive thing we do. It is also where AI helps most: pulling data from accounts, telematics, Companies House and asset registers into a risk presentation that is complete and checked. The authors' "clients as co-producers" and "missing inputs" points (step 1, section 5) are exactly this, but they don't see that in insurance it carries legal weight. A bad presentation voids cover.
- **The regulator's focus on commission and fair value.** The FCA's interest in how intermediaries are paid has been growing: GI pricing practices, the fair value work under the Duty, premium-finance charges. The motor finance commission affair showed that a commission model can become a liability across a whole market. An AI-native broker that cuts its cost and keeps the same commission will be asked, quite rightly, whether its fee is fair value. That pushes towards the fee- or subscription-based models the authors favour, though for a regulatory reason they don't mention.
- **The broker's own PI insurer.** My errors and omissions cover is priced by underwriters who will ask how AI-made recommendations are controlled. Who insures the AI-native broker, at what deductible, will shape what it can automate. That may matter more than the FCA does.
- **Price walking was banned for home and motor, not commercial.** Step 2 cites the 2022 ban as a comparison. It applied to personal lines. Commercial renewal pricing has no such rule, which is one reason inertia margins survive in my market.

## Where I think SME broking is in 2035, and whether the framework predicts it

This is my guess. Micro-SME cover is almost wholly embedded or bought digitally, often inside the bank or accounting app, with the platform taking the distribution share. That is Q7's "distribution owners" row, and the framework gets it right. The advised SME market is more concentrated: a handful of consolidators and perhaps one or two AI-native players. Each account handler runs several times today's number of accounts, because renewal data gathering, market submissions, documents and mid-term changes are automated. Most surviving independents are niche specialists. A larger share of premium goes through MGAs and broker facilities. Commission is under fair-value pressure and partly replaced by fees.

The framework predicts the shape: routine work collapses, margin moves to bundled risk and control, distribution owners win the micro end. It would get the cause of the profit wrong. In 2035, broking profit will still come mainly from the insurance cycle, retention and control of capacity, not from the engine's learning curve. And it would miss that the incumbents, the consolidators, are best placed to install the engine. They have the volume, the carrier relationships and the PE money. The robo-advice comparison at the end of step 2 is the most important sentence in either paper for my industry.

## What I would back if I were the investor

1. **A specialist MGA where risk can be checked continuously, run by the engine.** Fleet, with telematics and dashcams, and cyber, with external attack-surface scanning, are the two lines where P2's "checkability as a design choice" is already true. Some cyber MGAs and insurers already sell scanning and warnings alongside the policy. That is the authors' fast-engine-feeds-slow-business idea in working form. I'd back a UK operator in one such line with secure multi-carrier capacity, and I'd sell through brokers rather than against them at first.
2. **Software that does the fair presentation and the renewal for advised brokers.** Don't buy the brokers. Sell the engine to the forty-person firms like mine, which can't build it and don't want to sell to a consolidator. That turns the independents into your distribution and your data feed. It is the strongest version of "partner with whoever holds the volume".
3. **Claims advocacy as a paid service.** It is the slowest-checked, most trusted layer, and the one clients remember. It is under-supplied for SMEs and hard for a bank app to provide.

I would not back an AI-native broker that tries to out-acquire the consolidators, or a direct-to-micro-SME play. One is too expensive and the other is already done.
