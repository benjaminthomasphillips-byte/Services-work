# Review of the lettings worked example, by an operations director

Reviewer: written in character as the operations director of a large English lettings and management business. 24 November 2026. Review of `example-lettings.md`.

Labels: **[experience]** what I know from running the work; **[seen]** exists today; **[reasoned]** follows from logic; **[conjecture]** my guess towards 2030 to 2035.

## My overall view

The shape is right. The obligation ledger, "the law is mostly clocks", and the reach limit on templates are the best ideas here. I would build all three. But the example models the firm as a set of cases. My job is mostly a set of people who do not reply. The bottleneck is rarely the tenant or the paperwork. It is the landlord who will not approve a quote, the contractor who will not come, and the key nobody can find. The example under-weights all three.

## 1. Where agents could not do what it says

**Landlord approvals stall the repair line.** [experience] Above-limit works wait on landlords for days. Many never answer. Some dispute the invoice after the work. Agents can chase perfectly. Chasing is not the problem. The answer is structural: a float per home, a pre-agreed spend matrix by repair class, and a firm right to act. Without that, the repair clock in the promise cannot be met.

**Contractor supply, not dispatch, is the constraint.** [experience] Good plumbers and Gas Safe engineers are overbooked. They choose agents who pay fast and send tidy jobs. A text offer with a photo checklist works for the middle of the market [seen: repair platforms]. It does not create capacity. "Three no-shows and suspend" empties a rural panel in a winter. The network is the asset, and it needs people to build it.

**Tenant confirmation is a weak verifier.** [experience] Tenants rarely confirm a fix. They report again, or they do not. Under-reporting of recurrence will flatter the 90-day check. Treat silence as unknown, not as success.

**Money is messier than "same day".** [experience] Universal Credit arrives late and in odd amounts. Payments lack references. One payer covers two tenancies. Landlord statements raise queries about fees and works. Reconciliation by agents is credible now [reasoned]. Allocation of unidentified receipts and statement disputes will still need a person in 2027.

## 2. Where it is too cautious

- **Viewings and inspections.** Self-guided viewings with smart locks exist in the US [seen]. Video-led tenant self-inspection is credible now [reasoned]. Half the 15,000 visits could go by 2030 [conjecture].
- **Repair triage.** From my own desk, a fifth or more of reports are fixed by guidance: boiler pressure, a tripped switch, a blocked trap [experience]. A video triage agent should resolve these with no visit.
- **Voice.** Voice agents already make collection calls [seen]. Early arrears, access booking and certificate reminders by phone are ready now. The tenants on "old phones" answer calls.
- **Tax and data.** The example misses the Non-Resident Landlord Scheme. The agent must withhold tax unless HMRC approves gross payment, and is liable if it gets it wrong [experience]. Making Tax Digital for landlords is starting [seen]. Both are pure clocks and records. This is a product, not a risk.
- **Damp.** Humidity sensors are already used by social landlords [seen]. Readings beat photos as a verifier and would shorten the 12-month outcome signal.

## 3. What it has missed

- **Keys and access.** Most of my missed appointments are access failures, not no-shows [experience]. Key logs, key safes and smart locks need their own case type.
- **Leasehold flats.** Many managed homes are flats. Roof leaks, communal damp, fire safety and building insurance sit with a freeholder or managing agent. The firm cannot dispatch. Clocks run while a third party sleeps.
- **Insurance claims.** Escape of water to the flat below brings a neighbour, a block insurer and a loss adjuster. These run for months.
- **Fraud.** Forged payslips are rising, and generated documents will make it worse [conjecture]. Title fraud and rent-to-rent operators let homes they do not control. Payout and deposit refund bank details are the main target. "No agent changes bank details" is right, but someone must, with call-back checks.
- **Landlord events.** A landlord dies and executors need authority. A lender appoints receivers. A landlord sells with the tenant in place. A landlord leaves mid-tenancy and the deposit must transfer.
- **Council enforcement.** Improvement notices, licence conditions and civil penalties, now higher under the Renters' Rights Act [seen]. Councils want a named person.
- **Voids.** Frozen pipes, unoccupancy clauses in insurance and council tax all need managing.
- **Neighbours and behaviour.** Anti-social behaviour, subletting, cannabis farms and police warrants.

## 4. Volumes, roles and headcount

The volumes look inconsistent. 4,400 move-ins on 7,500 homes is nearly 60% turnover. Managed tenancies turn over more like 25 to 35% a year [experience], and periodic tenancies may change that either way [conjecture]. Unless let-only clients are included, the physical and onboarding volumes are inflated.

The physical network is undercounted. 30,000 viewings at about an hour with travel is 18 FTE alone. Inspections and check-ins add about 30 more [reasoned]. Either commit to self-guided viewings and tenant capture, or count about 50.

Inside the firm, 55 in 2027 is too low by about 25 [experience, with conjecture on the size]. Missing roles:

- contractor network managers, by region (4 to 6);
- client accounts and landlord tax (4 to 6);
- keys, access and voids coordination (2 to 3);
- complaints handling to the ombudsman's standard (2 to 3);
- landlord acquisition and valuation (6 to 10, given churn of perhaps 10 to 15% a year as landlords sell).

The judgement desk of 14 also looks light against 30,000 jobs, many above limit, plus 2,500 damp cases. I would plan about 80 in 2027 and 45 to 55 in 2030. One thing I would never remove is the night on-call person. It costs under £100,000 a year and caps the worst outcome. Some scaffolding is too cheap to take down.

## 5. Economics

Revenue is understated in today's model. Traditional firms also earn maintenance commission, interest on client money and referral fees [experience]. Renewal fees fall away under periodic tenancies [reasoned]. Costs are overstated: £55,000 loaded is high for property managers, who are nearer £35,000 to £45,000 [experience]. So the traditional margin is higher, and the gap smaller, than shown.

The agent-first costs are plausible line by line. The missing line is acquisition. Books trade at a multiple of fee income [experience]. Low-fee online managers have mostly retreated to let-only or left the market [experience]. Landlords choose on trust and on reaching a person when something breaks. A £65 subscription will be bought by portfolio landlords, not accidental ones.

## 6. What I would build

1. **Buy a book in one dense region, and migrate it.** Run in parallel for a year. Real data beats scaled guesses.
2. **Make the contractor network the first product.** Pay in 48 hours on evidence. Give them clean jobs and access. Capacity follows payment.
3. **Fix the landlord approval problem in the contract.** Float, spend matrix, right to act.
4. **Start with portfolio and build-to-rent clients.** They want data, accept rules and value the interface.
5. **Keep a named patch lead per 400 to 600 landlords.** A person for escalation and retention, not a property manager.
6. **Build access, fraud controls and leasehold liaison as case types from day one.**
7. **Sell the compliance guarantee first.** It is the cleanest case type and the strongest trust signal.
