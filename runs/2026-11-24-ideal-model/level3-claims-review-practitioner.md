# Review of the level-3 claims redesign, by a buying claims director

Review of `level3-claims.md`, 24 November 2026. Written in character: a claims director at a UK commercial and household insurer who buys outsourced handling and adjusting.

Labels: **[experience]** what I have seen in buying and running claims, not checked at source; **[seen]** exists today, with source; **[reasoned]** follows from logic; **[conjecture]** plausible towards 2035.

---

## My overall view

This is the first paper on outsourced claims I have read that starts in the right place. A loss adjuster is an answer to an information gap. Close the gap before the loss and most of the adjuster goes. I agree. I would buy parts of this in 2027. But the paper is too kind to itself in three places that decide whether it works: reductions, the baseline and the restoration network. It is also too cautious about the product itself.

## 1. The authority rule breaks on reductions, not declines

The paper says agents may pay but may not decline or reduce without a person. Declines are a minority. Reductions are on almost every claim: the excess, underinsurance (average), betterment, wear and tear on part of the damage, limits [experience]. If every reduction needs a named person, the judgement desk scales with volume, not with declines. The headcount table then falls apart.

The fix is a redesign, not a retreat. Reductions that were computed from rules the customer saw at bind (excess, a declared sum insured, a stated limit) can be automated. The customer gets the reason in plain words and a one-tap route to a person within a day [reasoned]. Discretionary reductions (betterment, partial wear and tear, causation) stay human. Write that split into the constitution.

The paper also says saying yes is "cheap to reverse". It is not. Once paid, money is almost never recovered from a policyholder [experience]. Automatic payment thresholds attract clusters of claims just below them [experience]. The payment breaker needs to watch for threshold gaming, not just volume.

## 2. The baseline cuts both ways

A pre-loss record is a good idea. But better evidence will often show a slow leak or old damage. That means more declines under gradual-damage and wear-and-tear exclusions [reasoned]. Home acceptance is already the FCA's worry [seen, as cited in the paper]. An insurer that instruments homes and then declines more will be on the front page. The design needs a rule: the baseline may be used to pay faster, and to decline only where the policyholder was warned at the time. A sensor alert that the customer ignored is fair to use. A reading they never saw is not [reasoned].

Adoption is optimistic. Take-up of free leak devices is modest, and many households never install what is posted to them [experience]. Renters, landlords and older customers are hard. Seventy per cent of losses with born evidence by 2035 is a stretch for household; motor will get there sooner through the car itself [conjecture]. Nobody pays for the devices in section 7. Someone must.

## 3. Restoration is the profit pool and the complaint pool

I agree that scarce trades are where the margin sits. But in my experience the worst complaints come from network repairs: snagging, missed dates, poor finish, and customers who wanted their own builder [experience]. Fixed price with a date puts all of that on the firm. It needs a workmanship guarantee, a capped exposure per event, and a fair cash-settlement option at a rate the Ombudsman will accept, not the network's discounted rate [experience; the FCA has looked at cash settlement fairness, reasoned from its 2025 work]. Carrying repair variance across a storm season also needs a balance sheet. "Take advice" is not enough; this is the firm's biggest risk.

## 4. The buyers will not behave as drawn

- **MGAs do not choose freely.** Their capacity provider approves the claims handler and often dictates it [experience]. The real buyer for the wedge is the syndicate or insurer behind the MGA. Sell to them.
- **The savings share will be fought over.** Every outsourcer has shown me savings. Counterfactuals are always contested. A matched-sample audit is right, but agree the method before the contract, or the share becomes an annual argument [experience].
- **A 44% margin will not survive a tender.** Procurement benchmarks. Expect it to fall to the mid-20s by the second renewal [conjecture].
- **"Reserves certified quarterly" overreaches.** The firm sets case reserves. Actuaries set the book's reserves. Reinsurers will not take an outsourcer's certificate in place of the insurer's [experience].

## 5. Counterparties and surge

Credit hire is not won on the first call. The not-at-fault driver often calls their own insurer or broker first, and the referral is sold before anyone else rings [experience]. Third-party capture already exists and only gets some of them [experience]. The paper should look at what is upstream: the referral itself.

The catastrophe reserve of 150 to 300 adjusters on retainer is the same people on every firm's list [experience]. In a big event, the constraint is trades, dryers and scaffolding. Contract priority capacity, with penalties, rather than names on a list.

## 7. Where it is too cautious

- **The product, not just the handling.** If cover is compiled at bind and restoration is fixed-price, the firm should co-design a policy that promises restoration by a date, not indemnity. That removes most quantum disputes at source [reasoned]. The paper stops at "wording review".
- **The indemnity prize.** Three to five per cent feels low for escape of water if mitigation reaches homes within hours. Water damage grows fast in the first two days [experience]. Test for more.
- **Declines forever human.** For simple, rule-based declines (no cover in force, an excluded peril the customer named), a fast human appeal may satisfy the Ombudsman better than a slow human first decision [conjecture]. Measure it in the wedge rather than assume it.

## 8. What I would do in 2027

Buy it as a pilot on escape of water for one MGA book, with the capacity provider in the room. Agree the savings audit method first. Measure three things in the first winter: device take-up, days to restoration and complaint rate on network repairs. Those three decide whether the rest is worth having.
