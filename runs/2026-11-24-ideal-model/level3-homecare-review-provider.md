# Review of the level-3 home care redesign: a provider's view

Written in character, 24 November 2026, as the director of an independent home care provider in England with about 150 care workers, rated Good or Outstanding. This is a composite practitioner's view, not a real firm's data.

Labels: **[experience]** what running a provider teaches (in character, unsourced); **[seen]** exists today, with source; **[reasoned]** follows from logic; **[conjecture]** plausible towards 2035.

---

## My overall view

This is the first redesign I have read that starts in the right place. "Nobody wants a visit" is true. The 15-minute call, minute-billing and zero-hours turnover do come from how care is bought, not from bad managers [experience]. Putting the worker at the centre and agents at the edge is right. I would sign up to the constitution clauses tomorrow.

But the paper is a design for the hours of 10am to 4pm. Home care is not. And it trusts the per-person-week price more than I would.

## What will not work as written

**1. Demand has peaks, and salaried teams do not.** Almost everyone wants to get up between 7 and 10, eat at midday, and go to bed between 7 and 10. Seven days a week. That is why split shifts exist [experience]. A team of ten on 37.5-hour salaries serving 45 people will be overloaded at breakfast and idle at 2pm. The "week as the unit" does not move breakfast. So the 80% contact share is not credible unless the middle of the day is filled with paid work that counts: reablement sessions, lunch with someone, social trips, training [reasoned]. Add the daily huddle, weekly planning, monthly reviews and ward visits, and I would put a realistic ceiling at 70–72% [reasoned]. That alone adds about a tenth to the 2035 workforce.

**2. The density figure needs checking.** About 500,000 people get regulated home care in England [seen: DHSC statistics, cited in the paper]. That is roughly one in 110 people. "A few streets" might hold 2,000 people, so perhaps 18 clients across every provider [reasoned]. To serve 45 in a walkable patch you need about 5,000 residents and almost all the market. That means a patch monopoly. Councils have tried zones; families still want choice, and direct-payment holders can go elsewhere [experience]. Density is possible in towns. It is not a given.

**3. "No call monitoring" contradicts "a guaranteed floor of hours".** If the council pays per week and the firm promises a floor, the council will want proof the floor was met. That is call monitoring by another name [reasoned]. Worse, per-week pricing pays the firm more for delivering less. The floor is the only guard. I would keep verified time records, owned by the person and visible to the council. Do not pretend they vanish.

**4. The price.** £390 a week for 11 hours is about £35.45 an hour. Average council rates are reported around £25 [seen, secondary, in the paper]. No council I deal with will pay 40% above its going rate for a pilot [experience]. The paper knows this. It should say plainly that 2027 runs on private clients and NHS money, not council contracts.

**5. Discharge is a risky first wedge.** Discharge money has come in short-term pots and dried up mid-year [experience]. Volumes swing week to week. Salaried staff cannot be laid off when beds empty. Use discharge as a second line. Lead with self-funders in one patch, where per-week pricing and a known team sell themselves [reasoned].

**6. Continuity has a dark side.** A known face is the best safeguard and the worst one. Closed, self-managing teams can normalise poor practice, and abuse is easier where nobody else visits [experience]. The paper's pattern-watch agent is good. Add a planned second pair of eyes: a senior from another team visits each person every month or so [reasoned].

**7. Families.** A daily summary will increase calls at first, not reduce them [experience]. Consent scoping will be hardest where the person has dementia and two children disagree. That is the normal case, not the edge case. The paper should design for it.

**8. Building it.** Six builders for 300 people, and a 40% loss in year one, is a software company's plan. Good care-management systems already exist [seen, general knowledge]. Buy them and build only the person agent and the week agent [reasoned].

## Where it is too cautious

- **Assessment.** The paper leaves assessment with the council. Trusted-assessor schemes already let providers assess at discharge [seen, general knowledge]. By 2035 the team should assess and set the band, with the council auditing a sample [conjecture]. That is the real cut in delay.
- **Hours per person.** A fall from 11 to 10 hours is timid if reablement is the default. Good reablement takes a share of people off care altogether [experience]. I would model a wider range.
- **Unpaid carers and neighbours.** The family "inside the team" is still unpaid. Why not pay family members and trained neighbours as part-time team members, under the firm's supervision [conjecture]? That is the only big new source of labour.
- **Night.** The paper barely mentions it. Night is where sensors plus a mobile response team could change the most, and it is the shift I find hardest to staff [experience].
- **Flexible hours.** Many of my best carers are parents who want school hours. Offer guaranteed contracted part-time, not only full salaries [experience].

The proposed G9, the workforce as a designed condition, is the most important line in the paper. I would add a G10: **time of day as a constraint**. Care demand has a shape, and the design must fit it.

## Stress test: CQC, councils and money

CQC will welcome continuous evidence, but I do not expect inspection to become "reading a live view" by 2035. Its capacity has been the problem, not our data [experience; conjecture for 2035]. One registered manager per 480 people is workable if seniors carry real delegated authority [reasoned].

The Fair Pay Agreement helps a salaried model. It also removes part of the pay advantage the paper relies on for recruitment [seen, in the paper; reasoned]. Then the advantage must come from the team, the hours and the ladder. I think it can. My lowest turnover has always been in teams that work the same patch with the same manager [experience].

My verdict: build it, but solve the 8am problem first.
