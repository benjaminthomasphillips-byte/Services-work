# Review of the level-3 legal redesign, by a co-founder of an AI-native firm

Written by Claude in character, 24 November 2026. I play a co-founder of an AI-native law firm built by lawyers and engineers together, selling speed and fixed prices to fast-growing companies. Labels: **[seen]** exists today, with source; **[reasoned]** follows from logic; **[conjecture]** plausible towards 2035; **[experience]** what my character would say from running such a firm. Experience here is role-played judgement. It is not evidence.

---

## My verdict

The diagnosis is right. Legal risk is born when someone commits, and the firm belongs at that moment. The draft is not timid about the end state. It is timid about the path. It also sells the wrong thing first, and it keeps a law firm's org chart under new names.

## 1. It wins clients the wrong way

The wedge is "the always-ready company": 30 days of record-building, sold through investors. That is a consulting project with a subscription attached. Founders do not buy prevention. They buy unblocking. [experience]

Firms like ours win with one small unit delivered absurdly fast at a known price. Crosby sells contract review at a fixed fee per document, reportedly around $400, with lawyers checking agents' work [seen, Contrary Research and press reports]. Cursor reportedly sent it about 2,000 contracts by March 2026 [seen, press reports]. Crosby raised a Series A in October 2025 and a $60m Series B since [seen, Artificial Lawyer; Law360]. The lesson: land on the deal that is stuck today, then widen.

So flip the order. Start with "send us the contract blocking your quarter; signed today". Every contract handled adds to the record. Readiness becomes a by-product of moments handled, not a project bought up front [reasoned]. The investor channel still works, but as a perk the fund pays for ("your companies get same-day contracts"), not as a readiness audit.

## 2. It misses the growth engine: the counterparty

The draft treats the other side as a problem to manage. It is the best channel the firm has. Every contract we send on the open standard lands in another company's inbox. That company sees the terms come back in minutes and asks who did it [reasoned]. Each deal is an advert to exactly the right buyer.

Conflicts limit this. We cannot advise the other side on that deal. But we can offer them their own agent for every other deal [reasoned]. Design the onboarding so the counterparty can join in one click after signing. That is a loop no traditional firm can copy, because their paper carries no product [conjecture].

## 3. It still thinks in documents

Section 3 lists today's documents and gives each a fate. That is the automation frame the client rejected. By 2035 most routine commitments need not be documents at all [conjecture]. A company publishes its policy envelope: caps, data terms, governing law. A counterparty's agent queries it, finds the overlap and binds both sides, logging the terms. The "contract" is a record of a handshake between two policies.

That changes the firm's job. It stops drafting paper and starts **writing and defending policies**: what each client will accept, and why. Policy design for a founder who has never stated a risk appetite is real legal work. The draft assumes policies exist (E3). In my experience founders cannot state them. The firm must infer them from past decisions and confirm them in a short conversation [experience].

## 4. The org chart is a law firm with new labels

"Judgement desk", "named counsel" and "deal leads" are associates, partners and transaction teams renamed. The headcount reaches about 9 clients per person in 2035. Too cautious [reasoned].

Organise by flow instead. Run small pods of one lawyer and one engineer, each owning a type of commitment end to end: sales contracts, hiring, financing. The pod's job is to make its flow need fewer humans each quarter. A lawyer who closes an exception also fixes the policy or test, so it never recurs [experience]. Under that rule the judgement desk shrinks every year, not just with model releases. I would plan for 15–20 clients per person in landscape A [conjecture]. The adversarial edge stays human, but it shrinks too once the other side also negotiates through agents.

## 5. Pricing: the right principle, the wrong unit

"Price the condition, never the hour" is right. But £1,750–1,900 a month is a retainer, and early-stage founders cancel retainers they do not feel [experience]. Price per commitment instead: per contract signed, per hire onboarded, per grant issued, with a monthly cap. Revenue then tracks the client's growth. The price also feels like infrastructure, not a bill [reasoned]. Keep fixed-price moments, and back them with a service guarantee: if a standard round takes more than ten working days, part of the fee comes back. That guarantee is credible only to a firm that knows its cycle times [reasoned].

## 6. The certificate is the boldest idea, but in the wrong wrapper

A readiness certificate relied on by investors' counsel is the right goal. A capped reliance letter from the firm puts the risk on our indemnity cover and invites aggregated claims (risk 7). Better: give warranty insurers the record and let them price the policy. Later, co-own a small insurance vehicle with one of them [conjecture]. Our record is the underwriting data. Their balance sheet carries the risk.

## 7. The architecture verdicts

- **E1 BENDS:** right. Positions with evidence is the correct reframe.
- **E2 BREAKS:** too harsh. The draft's own fix, "the flow where the client's acts take effect", is E2 restated more generally. That is a bend, not a break. Law also touches money more than it admits: subscription monies at a round, option exercises, settlements. An escrow partner puts the firm beside those flows without holding client money [reasoned]. Verdict: **BENDS**.
- **E3 HOLDS:** too generous. Policies are the heart of the design, but they do not exist at most small clients and must be made (point 3). **HOLDS, with a policy-design cost.**
- **E4 BENDS:** right.
- **E5 BENDS:** about right, but too harsh on guarantees. Legal outcomes cannot be guaranteed. Time, price and completeness can, with refunds [reasoned].
- **E6 BENDS:** right on the neutral standard. Too harsh on what the firm can own. It can own the best endpoint on that standard, the agent other companies' agents prefer to deal with, without owning a platform [conjecture]. That softens the landscape D downside.
- **E7 HOLDS:** right. The "adversarial edge" is a useful new kind of need.
- **E8 HOLDS:** too generous. Agents in HR, chat and sales systems are the hardest thing to sell; the draft lists it as risk 4. Start where work already happens without integration: email, chat and the e-signature tool. Go deeper later. **HOLDS, with an adoption ramp.**

## 8. Smaller points on scaling

- **Do not buy a firm in 2027.** Hire authorised people instead. A bought book brings hourly habits and clients who expect the old service [experience]. Apply for authorisation now: the SRA took about eight months to authorise an AI-driven firm [seen, SRA; Law Gazette].
- **Pick one segment first.** Three segments at 120 clients is too many. Start with venture-backed companies; their counterparties are their peers, which feeds point 2 [reasoned].
- **The training route is right,** and it is the firm's best recruiting pitch. Say so loudly.

## What I would keep

The commitment gate. One side only. No learning against a client. Privilege as a reason to use the firm. The neutral standard body. Supervision of the client's own AI. These are sound, and most rivals have not thought of them [reasoned].
