# Review of the level-3 legal redesign: a general counsel's view

Written in character, 24 November 2026. I am the general counsel of a 300-person UK scale-up. I buy from four firms and run a team of five lawyers who use AI daily. Labels: **[experience]** is my practitioner judgement in this role, not a sourced fact; **[seen]** exists today, with source; **[reasoned]** follows from logic; **[conjecture]** is plausible towards 2035.

## My overall view

Legal risk is born when someone commits, not when a lawyer looks. The "commitment flow" is a better idea than "the money flow" for law. The employment wedge is timely and real. But the paper assumes the hard part is technology and standards. In my job the hard part is people who commit outside any system, and records that can be used against us. Some of it is too generous. A few parts are too timid.

## Where it will not work as drawn

**1. The signature is not the chokepoint.** Most of our commitments are not signed in DocuSign. They are accepted in a customer's procurement portal, ticked in a vendor's click-through, agreed on a call and confirmed by email, or made in a side letter a sales director sends at 11pm on the last day of the quarter [experience]. On the sell side, roughly two-thirds of our revenue by value is on the customer's paper. On the buy side, we sign vendors' standard terms we cannot change. A gate on e-signature sees the tidy minority. The real gates are the purchase order, the portal and the CRM [reasoned]. The paper's 50–70% "on a standard by 2035" is too generous for any company that sells to banks, retailers or government [conjecture]. Standards will win in the middle: NDAs, DPAs, SaaS between peers.

**2. The friction monitor creates evidence against the client.** This is the gap I would raise first. A system that scores "this employee may bring a claim" or "this customer may sue" writes a record. In a tribunal or a subject access request, that record is likely disclosable. Litigation privilege needs litigation in reasonable contemplation and a dominant purpose. An automated risk score built from operational data may fail both [reasoned]. "We knew in March and did nothing" is the worst sentence in a tribunal bundle [experience]. The paper treats privilege as a reason to route work through the firm. It also needs to treat the live record itself as a liability. Design for it: keep scores inside a privileged channel, as advice to counsel, with retention limits.

**3. I would not hand the record or the gate to an outside firm.** For a company with a GC, the record of what we promised is the core of my function. My board, my auditors and my security team would not let a law firm's agents run inside our HR system and chat [experience]. The paper half-sees this ("a layer under the GC"). But its economics still count 400 scale-ups paying £60–96k a year for Standing. For GC-led companies I would pay for moments, specialists, capacity and a name. I would pay for standing work only where I have no lawyer: a new jurisdiction, say [experience].

**4. Supervising my AI is not a product I would buy as drawn.** I would pay for good test suites and benchmarks. I would not pay a firm to "answer for outputs" unless it truly took the liability. No firm's insurer will let it accept liability for a tool it does not control, on terms I would value [reasoned]. What I would buy is narrower: a firm's tested playbooks and clause positions, loaded into my tools, updated when the law changes. That is a licence, not supervision [conjecture].

**5. The readiness certificate is less new than claimed, and fits exits better than rounds.** Vendor due diligence reports with reliance letters to buyers and insurers already exist in UK private equity sales [experience]. But venture rounds are not slow because of diligence. Rounds are slow because of investment committees, existing investors' consents, pre-emption waivers and arguments about a few rights [experience]. Standard documents already exist (the BVCA models) and rounds still take weeks. "A round in days" needs the investors' process redesigned, not just the company's record [reasoned].

**6. Nobody will opt in to share term data.** What our customers accept is commercial intelligence. I would not let it be pooled, even anonymised, because in a small market it is easy to trace [experience]. Public ratings of vendor contracts are a likelier route than pooling from clients [conjecture]. The paper is right to ration it, but should not count on it.

## Where it is too cautious

**Large counterparties may move first, not last.** The paper treats banks and retailers as the obstacle. Their procurement teams hate negotiation volume more than anyone does [experience]. They are the likeliest to deploy negotiating agents early, but with their own envelope, not a neutral standard. So the firm's bolder bet is not owning a standard. It is being the best at negotiating against another party's agent: knowing its envelope, finding where it bends, and escalating to a person at the right moment [conjecture].

**Legal spend is a flow it ignores.** I manage a panel, e-billing, matter budgets and outside counsel guidelines. A firm that helped me run all of my external spend, including on rivals, and showed outcomes per pound would sit in a real flow I control [reasoned]. That is closer to E2 than the paper allows.

**Rule-change mapping could be sold to GCs as a product.** The paper says not to compete for the GC's routine work. But I would pay for "here is every contract and process this new rule touches, with the fix" today [experience].

## The E1–E8 verdicts

| | Paper | My view |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **E1** record | Bends | **Agree**, and add: the record is also a disclosure risk. It holds positions, but it must be designed for what a hostile reader can obtain |
| **E2** money flow | Breaks | **Slightly too harsh.** Client money, yes, breaks. But spend management is a money flow the firm could sit in. The "commitment flow" restatement is right |
| **E3** policies | Holds | **Too generous.** Playbooks exist, but they are overridden at quarter end by people senior to legal [experience]. The firm must not own risk appetite; the CFO and CRO set it. Holds for envelopes, bends on enforcement |
| **E4** conditions | Bends | **Agree.** Conditions to shorten moments is the right bridge |
| **E5** pricing | Bends | **Agree, slightly generous.** Fixed fees exist today. Cycle time is driven by the other side as much as by readiness, so pooled data predicts less than claimed |
| **E6** standard | Bends | **Agree.** Neutral body is right. Expect rival standards from large buyers |
| **E7** people at edges | Holds | **Agree.** The adversarial edge is the right addition |
| **E8** capability in place | Holds | **Too generous. Bends by segment.** Holds below 150 staff with no lawyer. For GC-led companies, access to HR, chat and sales systems will be refused or slow, and embedding raises disclosure as well as privilege questions |

## What I would actually buy in 2027

The employment re-papering, at a fixed price, now [experience]. Vendor due diligence with reliance before a sale. A rule-change map for our contracts. Named specialists on call for disputes and regulators. I would not buy the gate or the friction monitor from a firm. If a firm shows me the friction monitor can be built so it is privileged and not disclosable, I would reconsider.
