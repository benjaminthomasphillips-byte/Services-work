# Review of the level-3 lettings redesign: a builder's view

Written in character, 24 November 2026. I play a founder of an AI-native lettings operator. It buys agencies and runs them on its own agents, in the spirit of Dwelly, and has automated more than half the steps in letting a home.

Labels: **[seen]** exists today, with a source. **[reasoned]** follows from logic. **[conjecture]** plausible towards 2035. **[experience]** is what I would say as an operator who has run acquired books. It is judgement given in role, not measured data, so treat it as one step weaker than [reasoned].

---

## My verdict

This is the best paper in the set. Its central move is right: make the home the unit, set policy once, and let work disappear instead of getting faster. But it still sells a better version of what landlords buy today: management plus maintenance, now at a fixed price. It stops one step short of the real redesign. In 2035 the landlord is not buying "management". He is buying a **net yield on a home he never has to think about, or a clean way out**. The tenant is not renting a flat from a stranger. She is a **member of a network of homes**. Build for those two things and most of the paper falls into place. Some of it changes.

## 1. Sell the yield and the exit, not a cheaper bundle

The "fixed-cost home" is today's bundle at a lower and smoother price. That is still level 1's question with a better answer. I would sell **a net yield contract**: rent assurance, maintenance, compliance and voids folded into one monthly payment to the landlord. The landlord's question becomes "what do I get each month?", not "what do I pay?" [reasoned]. The paper avoids this because of insurance law. That is a fair stress-test point. It is not a reason to design small. Start with an insurer carrying the tail from day one, not from 2031 [reasoned].

The bigger miss is the exit. A quarter to a third of landlords say they plan to cut back or sell, and many single-home owners plan to leave entirely [seen: English Private Landlord Survey 2024, 31% reducing, as reported in search results and not checked at source; [NRLA Q2 2026](https://www.nrla.org.uk/news/more-than-quarter-landlords-selling-as-perfect-storm-hits)]. The paper treats this as one line in section 9.3. I would make it a main product. The verified home record makes a single tenanted home **investible** for a fund, because the fund can price it without a survey. So the firm becomes the bridge: buy homes with tenants in place for a capital partner, keep running them, and keep the tenant's home. Exiting landlords become a source of homes, not lost revenue [conjecture]. By 2035 I would expect institutional mandates to be a bigger share of homes than the paper's "few dozen" [conjecture].

## 2. The tenant is the distribution, not the landlord

The paper calls tenants "a second customer". In my world they are the first. Under periodic tenancies tenants move more freely, and landlords cannot pick from bidding wars [seen: Renters' Rights Act phase 1]. So a home's value to a landlord depends on how fast a good tenant fills it. The firm that holds a pool of verified, loyal renters controls supply for landlords [reasoned]. I would build **a renter membership**: no deposit, a rent history that counts as credit, priority on any home in the network, and a move between our homes in days. Landlords would join to reach those tenants, not the other way round [conjecture]. In my books the cheapest landlord we ever won was referred by a tenant who liked us [experience].

## 3. Plan for agents on both sides of the letting

By 2035 most searches will be run by a tenant's own AI agent, not by a person scrolling a portal [conjecture]. That agent wants machine-readable homes: verified condition, energy, running costs, rules on pets, and a way to make a binding offer. The paper puts "tenants' agents" in 2034. I would build the interface in 2028. Portals earn their toll by owning attention. Agents do not need attention. They need data they can trust [reasoned]. The firm with the best-verified listings wins the agent's choice. That is a better hedge against portals than an in-house tenant pool alone.

## 4. Data at the source: use what is already in the home

The paper wants sensors, smart locks and shut-off valves in each home. From buying books, I know devices are the part that breaks: flat batteries, Wi-Fi changes, tenants unplugging hubs, and landlords refusing the cost [experience]. Much of the data already exists. About two-thirds of domestic meters were smart and working in smart mode at the end of 2025 [seen: [DESNZ smart meter statistics](https://www.gov.uk/government/collections/smart-meters-statistics)]. Newer boilers report faults themselves. Bank feeds show payment patterns. The tenant's phone has a camera. So I would **read before I install**: use meter data (with consent) to infer damp risk and heating faults, use connected boilers, and pay tenants small credits for a 60-second video each quarter. Add sensors only where the risk model says they pay [reasoned]. This also eases the privacy problem the paper flags.

## 5. Own the flows, give the record away

"Own the record" is the wrong instinct. A record that nobody else reads has no value, and the state's landlord database will hold the obligations layer anyway [reasoned]. Power sits in **the flows**: rent passing through the wallet, and work orders passing through the contractor network. I would publish the home record as an **open standard** and lobby lenders, insurers and conveyancers to accept it. Then I would win by being the cheapest and most trusted operator of that standard [reasoned]. The paper half-says this ("open export"). I would go further and make the schema public in 2027.

## 6. The wedge: the annual gas check is too weak

Gas checks are a commodity, bought on price, and they will shrink as heat pumps spread [reasoned; pace is conjecture]. Better wedges are **moments when a landlord must decide**: the EPC C deadline in 2030, registration on the landlord database, a remortgage, a sale. Each has a natural partner, such as a buy-to-let lender or broker [reasoned]. I would sell "EPC C done, financed from the rent, with a guaranteed yield after" through lenders. Pay for upgrades from the wallet over time. The lender gets a better asset, and we get the home [conjecture].

## 7. Acquisitions work differently from the paper

The paper says buy books and convert each home at its next annual visit. I agree on not migrating old processes. But most landlord churn after a deal comes in the first 90 days, when landlords notice the change of owner [experience]. Conversion must be fast and must start with something the landlord gains, such as a faster payout or a cleared compliance gap. It must not wait for a calendar event. Also, employed multi-skilled technicians with gas and electrical tickets are scarce [experience]. Budget for an apprenticeship pipeline or buy a heating firm. Do not assume you can hire 16 of them.

## 8. Still too cautious on 2035 headcount

Twenty-six inside people for 7,500 homes in landscape A is still a 2030 number. The paper itself says the floor is set by accountability, not by work. With the yield contract, renter membership and agents on both sides, I would expect the inside floor nearer 12 to 18, with the field as the real cost line [conjecture]. The measure that matters by then is not staff per home. It is **margin per home on the risk the firm carries**.

## What I would build first

1. A renter membership and verified listing interface, in 2027.
2. A net-yield contract with an insurer behind it, on one acquired book.
3. An open home-record schema, filled from meters, boilers, bank feeds and tenant video before any new device.
4. An exit route for landlords leaving, with a capital partner, by 2028.
