# Review of the level 3 lettings redesign: an operations director's view

Review of `level3-lettings.md`, 24 November 2026. Written in character as the operations director of a large build-to-rent and lettings operator in England.

Labels: **[experience]** is what I would expect from running homes. It is a practitioner's judgement, not checked data. **[seen]** exists today, with source. **[reasoned]** follows from logic. **[conjecture]** is plausible towards 2035.

---

## My verdict

This is the right question. Making the home the unit of work, not the case, is correct. The home record, standing policy and the annual visit are sound. But the paper is strongest where it removes admin and weakest where it meets people, money and bricks. Three things decide whether it works. Can you price the repair risk? Does the physical round work in scattered stock? Do the exceptions shrink as much as the table says? I think the first is harder, the second is fine with changes, and the third is over-claimed.

## 1. The risk pool is the business, and adverse selection will find it

The fixed-cost home at one price is the boldest idea and the most dangerous. The landlords who sign up first will be the ones with 1970s boilers and flat roofs [experience]. Single-home stock varies far more than a build-to-rent block. One price for all will lose money on the worst third.

Fix: underwrite each home at entry. Use the first annual visit as the survey. Price by component age and building type. Exclude known defects or put them in the capital plan [reasoned]. The paper already has the data to do this. It just does not use it at the door.

The 25% maintenance fall is a guess, as the paper admits. In my experience about half of reactive spend is driven by tenant turnover, white goods and misuse, not by fabric decay [experience]. Sensors do little for that half. I would plan on 10 to 15% and treat more as upside [conjecture].

## 2. Rent assurance is underpriced for a post-section 21 world

Section 21 has gone. Possession now rests on section 8 grounds and on county courts that are already slow [seen: Renters' Rights Act roadmap]. One bad tenancy can mean many months of lost rent plus legal cost [experience]. At 3 to 4% of rent the paper is pricing a pre-2026 market [reasoned]. The good news is the paper's own instinct: the firm wants tenancies to last. Lead with household support and early contact, not with cover.

## 3. Referencing is not gone. It becomes a fairness decision

We use open-banking referencing now. It is faster and harder to forge [experience]. But the hard cases remain: self-employed, students, new arrivals, guarantors, people on Universal Credit. Fraud moves to borrowed and mule accounts [experience]. And landlords can no longer ask for large rent in advance, and blanket bans on families and benefit claimants are outlawed [seen: Renters' Rights Act]. So affordability judgement matters more, and it must be explainable. Call it "changed", not "gone". Keep a fairness lead close to it.

## 4. The physical world: keys, turns and access

- **Keys are not "mostly gone".** Smart locks work in our build-to-rent blocks because we own the door and the entry system [experience]. In scattered houses, tenants change locks, freeholders control communal doors, and the law still needs 24 hours' notice for access [seen: Landlord and Tenant Act 1985, s.11]. Contractors still need someone to let them in.
- **The void is the turn, not the search.** With two months' tenant notice, re-letting inside notice is already normal for good operators [experience]. The real void is the turn: clean, paint, carpet, snag. Seven to fourteen days is common [experience]. The paper never designs the turn. It should. Own a turn crew; plan works from the record before the keys come back.
- **Check-out still needs eyes.** Sensors do not see a stained carpet or a cigarette burn. Tenant move-in videos are often poor [experience]. The continuous record shrinks disputes; it does not end the visit.

## 5. The annual visit works, with two changes

A gas check can be done up to two months early and keep its anniversary date [seen: Gas Safety (Installation and Use) (Amendment) Regulations 2018]. That makes planned rounds feasible. The electrical check is five-yearly and needs a qualified electrician, so the "one technician" needs dual-qualified staff or a paired visit [seen: Electrical Safety Standards regulations 2020; reasoned]. Gas-and-electric multi-skilled engineers are scarce and poached [experience]. Budget for training your own.

Failed first appointments are the hidden cost [experience]. Build in evening and Saturday slots.

Sixteen technicians for 7,500 homes can cover the annual round. They cannot also take much of the reactive work [reasoned]. The paper slightly double-counts them.

## 6. Devices: the problem is uptime, not consent

The paper worries that tenants will refuse sensors. In my experience few do, when it is framed as damp and leak protection [experience]. Social landlords have seen the same [seen in sector reports, general knowledge]. The bigger problem is devices going quiet: dead batteries, unplugged hubs, no landlord broadband in scattered homes [experience]. Plan for cellular or low-power network devices, and an "offline" state that triggers a visit. Also plan for alarm fatigue. Every flat breaches a humidity band in January. Someone must decide which alerts mean action.

## 7. The people the paper under-counts

- **Councils.** Selective and additional licensing differ by borough. Enforcement notices, HMO rules and Awaab's Law timeframes all need named people who know the local officers [experience]. Council liaison barely appears.
- **Complaints and redress.** The new ombudsman will raise volume, not cut it [reasoned]. One complaints person in 2035 is too few. Keep three, and treat complaints as the best quality data you have.
- **The compliance warranty.** Indemnifying a landlord's civil penalty or a rent repayment order may not be insurable in the normal way [reasoned; needs legal advice]. Price the warranty as "we prevent it and pay the costs", not "we pay any fine".

## 8. Where the paper is too cautious

- **Start in build-to-rent, not just scattered stock.** Density, owned doors, one building system and one owner already exist there [experience]. The home record, policy engine and sensor-led damp control can run at full strength in year one. Use that as the proof, then take it to single homes.
- **Standing authority is not a leap.** Most management agreements already carry a spend limit [experience]. Landlords will accept policy. What they resist is cash held back. Sell the repair reserve as "your money, your account, visible", not as a deduction.
- **The tenant can be a bigger customer.** Periodic tenancies mean tenants move more freely. A tenant who stays with the firm across homes is a lower void and lower risk. Offer movers priority and a carried-over deposit [conjecture]. That is a moat portals cannot copy.
- **The record should go to lenders sooner.** Buy-to-let lenders need EPC C evidence for 2030 [reasoned]. Go to two of them in 2027, not 2031.

## What I would do first

Run the annual service in one dense region and one build-to-rent scheme. Underwrite every home at the first visit. Measure three numbers for two years: reactive spend per home, device uptime, and turn days. If reactive spend does not fall by 10% in that time, sell the record and the service, and leave the risk to insurers.
