# Review of the level-3 payroll redesign: a practitioner's view

Reviewer: head of global payroll and employment compliance, 3,000 staff in 25 countries (in character). Written 24 November 2026. I read `level3-payroll.md` in full.

Labels: **[seen]** exists today, with source; **[reasoned]** follows from logic; **[conjecture]** plausible towards 2035; **[experience]** what I see running payroll today. Experience is a practitioner's judgement. It is not checked against sources and it is not a forecast. Where I cite a rule without a check, I say "general knowledge, not checked".

---

## My overall view

I would buy most of this. The core idea is right: I want states that are true, not runs. The money section is the best thing in it. The control result, though, is stated too kindly. And the paper misses the two things that eat most of my week: collective agreements and people who move.

## 1. The unit of rules is wrong

The paper treats "a country's rule pack" as the unit. In my world it is not [experience]. In Belgium pay depends on the joint committee for the sector. In France it is the *convention collective*. In Germany it is the *Tarifvertrag*, then the works agreement. In Italy it is the CCNL. Each sets grades, allowances, extra months and notice [general knowledge, not checked]. The real unit is **country × sector agreement × company agreement**.

This matters for headcount. Twenty standard-setters for 40 countries is far too few if each country hides dozens of agreements [reasoned]. It also matters for the architecture (see 7).

## 2. EOR is often agency work in law, and that has a price

The paper sees Germany's permit and 18-month cap. It misses the bigger point. Across much of the EU, the agency-work rules require **equal treatment with the client's own comparable staff** [general knowledge: EU Temporary Agency Work Directive 2008/104; the Dutch payrolling rules since 2020; German equal pay after nine months; not checked]. So the EOR contract cannot be "generated from policy". It must mirror the client's terms, which the firm does not hold. In France, lending staff for profit can be unlawful outright [general knowledge, not checked].

So the dial is not a convenience. In many EU countries it is the only lawful product [reasoned]. The paper half sees this in 11.3. It should lead with it.

## 3. Errors: the design is too clever about prevention and too quiet about correction

Most of my pain is late facts, not wrong maths: backdated rises, late sickness notes, unreported leavers [experience]. Continuous computation helps. But authorities close periods. UK tax is cumulative across the year. Corrections to filed periods follow each state's own process [general knowledge]. The "run" disappears as work. It does not disappear as a legal unit [reasoned].

Here the paper is **too cautious**, not too bold. It should state an error policy the employee can see: underpayments paid the same day; small overpayments caused by the firm never clawed back; every correction explained in the live statement [reasoned]. The penalty guarantee covers the authority. Nothing covers the employee's trust, which is what they remember [experience].

## 4. Money: right instinct, wrong timing

Debiting the client "on payday morning" will not work as stated [experience]. Many corporate treasuries block third-party debits. Payroll in seven currencies needs FX settled before payday. Banks have cut-offs, and payment returns from wrong accounts arrive days later. Employees want the money at the start of payday, not by the end.

The fix is bolder, not more cautious: **make the credit facility the default**. The firm pays on time from its own funding line and collects afterwards [reasoned]. That kills the deposit, which is the thing I actually resent. EOR deposits of one to three months' cost tie up real cash [experience]. "No float" I barely notice. "No deposit" I would switch for. Sell that.

No FX spread is genuinely valuable. Hidden spreads are the line my CFO asks about [experience].

## 5. People who move

The paper mentions equity for movers. That is a small part of it. My daily mess is staff working from a country where we are not registered: social-security certificates, the 183-day test, shadow payroll, permanent-establishment risk [experience]. Remote work made this the fastest-growing source of risk. A firm that holds the employment record across borders should treat **location as an event**, like a pay rise, and price the result live [reasoned]. This is the biggest missing product.

## 6. Employee experience and the portable record

The live pay statement is right. The portable record is good but placed wrongly. A mortgage lender will trust the tax authority's data more than the employer's [reasoned]. The record should link to authority data and to state identity wallets, not compete with them [conjecture].

Support headcount is too lean. Twenty people supporting 150,000 employees in 40 countries cannot cover the languages and time zones for illness and bereavement [reasoned]. Agents will handle "why is my pay different?". People will still be needed when someone is ill [experience].

## 7. The architecture verdicts

**G2 and C3 bends: fair.** The agent-or-principal finding is the paper's best insight. I agree with it.

**Too generous:**
- **G8, evidence rules (HOLDS).** "Payroll data never used against the employee" cannot hold. Law requires the employer to recover overpayments, apply court attachment orders and report to authorities [general knowledge]. This should be **BENDS**. The fix: data used against the employee only where law compels it, and the employee is told.
- **C2, the standard (HOLDS, with a clarification).** The paper says it holds "if a state counts as the neutral body". That condition is doing all the work. Much of the standard is set by **unions and employers' associations**, not states (point 1). I would score **BENDS**.
- **G3, policy as work (HOLDS).** The client does not write all the policy. Social partners co-write it, and works councils must agree some systems. **Bends mildly.** With C2, this points to a second missing parameter: **who writes the rules: client, state or social partners**.
- **C10, rationed public payer (not switched on).** Wrong. Statutory sick and parental pay often run through state funds, with employer reclaim or subrogation [general knowledge, not checked]. The firm deals with rationed public payers every month. **Switched on.**
- **G10, survives the firm (HOLDS, weighted heavily).** Transferring employees to a step-in employer is governed by transfer-of-undertaking law and, in some countries, employee consent [general knowledge]. It holds on paper. I would mark it **untested**.

**Too harsh:** none that I can see.

**Is the control honestly stated?** Not quite. One author designed and scored the paper. My tally is about **17 holds, 5 bends, 0 breaks, one untested** [reasoned]. The architecture still survives. No element breaks. But "nearly clean" overstates it. The honest finding is two missing parameters, not one.

## 8. What buyers really pay for

I pay for four things [experience]: one accountable party when it goes wrong; independent controls evidence my auditors accept; local-language support for my staff; and errors fixed fast and without fuss. I do not pay for architecture. The paper's pricing logic ("price the condition and the accountability") matches this. Its sales story should too.

I would still keep the float rule. Clients like me will pay a little to see no hidden income [experience].

## Where it is too cautious, in short

- Make credit the default and abolish deposits.
- Publish an employee-first error policy.
- Treat location as a live event and own mobility.
- Lead with lawful agency-work status across the EU, rather than treating Germany as the exception.
- Ask authorities to read the event stream from day one in the four real-time countries, not in 2031.
