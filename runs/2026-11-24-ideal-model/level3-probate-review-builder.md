# Review of the level-3 probate redesign, by a builder

Written in character as a founder of an online wills and probate service, 24 November 2026. Labels: **[seen]** exists today, with source; **[reasoned]** follows from logic; **[conjecture]** plausible towards 2035; **[experience]** what I say as an operator. Experience is practitioner judgement voiced in character. It is not a checked source and should not be quoted as evidence.

---

## My verdict in one line

This is the best paper I have read on our market. But it still treats probate as a project to run faster. The bolder move is to make most estates **never need probate at all**.

## 1. Where it still automates the old service

The design keeps the old shape: death, then grant, then collection, then distribution. It makes each step quicker and bridges the waits with loans and insurance. That is a better probate firm. It is not a new service.

Ask the first-principles question. Why does a grant exist? To prove who may take money out of frozen accounts. Many assets already pass without one: joint accounts, pension nominations, life policies written in trust, small balances under bank limits [seen, general knowledge]. So the readiness membership should not aim at "estate known". It should aim at **"estate pre-routed"**. Every asset should have a named destination that works at death without a grant. Use nominations, trusts for the house where sensible, joint ownership where the person wants it, and payable-on-death instructions wherever an institution offers them [reasoned].

The readiness score then measures one thing: **the share of the estate that will move without a grant.** If a member reaches 90%, the "settlement" is a week of notifications. The wallet, bridging and much of the guarantee shrink to a residue [reasoned]. By 2035 I would expect banks to offer death-transfer instructions widely, because they want to stop freezing accounts. Grieving customers leave them [conjecture].

The paper does see the risk that this shrinks its own fee base. I would take that risk. Whoever gets to zero-probate estates first wins the member, and the member's children.

## 2. How firms like ours actually win clients

Here the paper is weakest.

- **Monthly readiness subscriptions will not sell.** People do not pay £6 a month for a thing they want to forget. In our experience they pay once, at a trigger, and then disappear [experience]. Make readiness free to the person. Have it paid for by someone with a lasting reason to pay: employers, banks, pension schemes, and above all **charities**.
- **Charity-funded wills are the missing channel.** Charities pay for free wills because some people leave them a gift [seen, general knowledge: free-will schemes and charity partnerships are common]. That has been the biggest acquisition engine in online wills [experience]. The paper never mentions it. It fits the design well, because the wishes record can hold the gift and the reasons for it.
- **Buying will banks is overrated.** A will stored in 2004 belongs to someone who has moved twice and ignores email. Conversion to membership is low [experience]. What has real value is the **probate instruction at death**, and that goes to whoever the family trusts that week.
- **The real flywheel runs through children.** The paper says this in section 8.2 but does not build on it. Every settlement puts us in front of three to five adult children in their fifties, at the one moment they all think about their own wills [experience]. That is the cheapest acquisition we have. It should be the core of the growth model, not a footnote.
- **Cold estates fund the firm.** About half of adults have no will, and most deaths for decades will be non-members [seen, general knowledge]. Intestate and cold estates are the volume. The paper treats them as a cost problem. They are the business until about 2032 [reasoned].

## 3. The wedge is wrong in order, not in kind

The April 2027 pension change is real [seen, HMRC technical note]. But it pulls a young firm straight into the most liability-heavy work, with an untested tax engine. The paper itself names "a correlated error" as a way to fail. Pension providers are also slow, cautious partners; expect 18 months to sign one [experience].

Start where volume teaches operations: standard cold estates, sold direct at a fixed price. Add the pension-aware product in 2028, once the engine has been tested against a few hundred real estates [reasoned].

## 4. Too cautious about 2035

- **Headcount.** About 230 people for 12,000 estates is cautious. In our experience a small team runs thousands of estates a year, and most of its time goes on chasing institutions [experience]. The redesign deletes that chasing. I would model 2035 A nearer 150 people, with the guide line as the only large one [conjecture].
- **The house.** It is the longest wait and the main cause of fights, and the paper leaves it as a "remains". Be bolder. Offer a guaranteed sale or purchase through a neutral partner, at an independent valuation, with the price and fee shown. Or offer a lifetime arrangement that passes the house to a named beneficiary without a sale [conjecture]. The constitution's ban on commissions can stay. A transparent, priced option is not a kickback.
- **Digital life.** Photos, subscriptions, social accounts and crypto are a high-volume, low-skill job that families hate. It is a good cheap first product for non-members [reasoned].

## 5. The E1 to E8 verdicts

| # | Paper's verdict | My view |
| --- | --- | --- |
| E1 | Bends | **Agree.** Add that the record should hold destinations, not just balances [reasoned] |
| E2 | Bends | **Agree, but the timeline is too generous.** Before the grant, banks hold the money. "Funeral money same day" relies on banks paying invoices directly, which many already do [seen, general knowledge]. The wallet starts later than implied |
| E3 | Holds strongly | **Too generous.** Wills are full of ambiguity: chattels, "equally between my children who survive me", lapse, missing gifts. Residue computes. Everything else needs judgement [experience]. Say **holds** |
| E4 | Bends | **Agree.** Pre-routing makes the condition do more of the work, which strengthens E4 [reasoned] |
| E5 | Holds | **Too generous.** Insurers will want years of loss data before they price executor indemnities at a sensible cost. The paper names this risk in section 10 but still scores E5 as holding. Say **bends until about 2030** [reasoned] |
| E6 | Bends | **Too generous on feasibility.** A shared UK finance standard tends to need a regulator's or the state's push. Open banking needed a competition order [seen, CMA 2017]. A start-up cannot co-found it. It can lobby and publish a schema [reasoned] |
| E7 | Bends | **Too harsh; it breaks here.** The guide is the product the family buys. Agents are the factory behind them. Call it a break and restate the element [reasoned] |
| E8 | Bends | **Agree, with a warning.** Embedding in hospices and care homes looks like referral-farming at the deathbed. Regulators and the press will treat it that way [experience]. Embed in employers, banks and charities instead |

E9 (mandate chain) and E10 (custody beyond the firm) are right. They are the paper's best contribution.

## 6. What I would change first

1. Replace "estate known" with "estate pre-routed" as the target of readiness.
2. Make readiness free to the person, paid for by charities, employers and banks.
3. Put the children's flywheel at the centre of the growth model.
4. Lead with cold standard estates; add the pension product in 2028.
5. Rescore E3 and E5 down, and E7 to a break.
