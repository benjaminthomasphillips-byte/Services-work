# Review of the level-3 probate redesign: a practitioner's view

Reviewer: a senior private client solicitor and STEP member who has run probate departments. Written in character, 24 November 2026. I read the draft in full.

Labels: **[seen, practice]** is my own experience of how estates actually run. **[seen]** is a published fact. **[reasoned]** follows from logic. **[conjecture]** is plausible towards 2035.

---

## My overall view

This is the best redesign of probate I have read. Its central insight is right. Most of our work is rebuilding a dead person's financial life from outside, by letter, months later. Closing that gap before death is the right answer, not faster letter-writing [seen, practice]. The pension wedge is well timed. Refusing percentage fees and funeral commissions is right, and clients will notice.

But it has been written by someone who has not sat at the kitchen table. In places it is too smooth about counterparties and the law. In other places it is too timid about changing them.

## Where it will not work as written

**1. Authority before the grant is the real bottleneck, and the paper underplays it.** Executors named in a will have authority from death in law. In practice, banks, registrars of shares and the Land Registry ask for the grant before releasing anything above their small-estate limits [seen, practice]. Administrators under an intestacy have no authority at all until the grant [seen]. About half of adults have no will, so most cold estates are intestate. An "estate wallet in the executors' name" before the grant is hard to open with most banks today [seen, practice]. The whole timeline depends on the wallet filling early. It will not, unless the partner bank accepts something short of a grant.

**2. The timeline for taxable estates is wrong.** Where an IHT400 is needed, the probate application cannot go in until HMRC has had the return for 20 working days [seen, HMRC guidance]. That is a floor of about four weeks after filing, not "1–2 weeks to tax paid and probate applied for". Excepted estates, which are most estates, avoid this [seen]. The paper should split the timeline by tax status.

**3. "Full estate position known in hours" confuses assets with liabilities.** The assets may be known in hours for a member. The liabilities are not. DWP benefit overpayment checks routinely hold up distribution for months, and we tell executors not to distribute until they clear [seen, practice]. Add care-home arrears, last-year income tax, personal guarantees and the occasional unknown creditor. The record in life sees money in. It rarely sees contingent claims.

**4. The executor choice belongs to the executors, not the firm.** "Let this firm act for my executors" is a wish, not a binding instruction. Executors can instruct whoever they like [seen, practice]. The only binding route is appointing the firm as professional executor. That route has a poor reputation, because will writers have used it to lock in fees that families later resent [seen, practice]. The paper must show executors why they would choose to keep the firm, not assume they will. The guarantee is that reason. Say so.

**5. Insurance will be adverse-selected.** Missing-beneficiary and late-creditor cover exists and is cheap [seen, practice]. Cover for family provision claims under the 1975 Act is a different matter. An insurer will price the calm estate and exclude the one with an estranged child or a cohabitant left out [reasoned]. Those are exactly the estates where early distribution matters. Expect early pay-out of the residue to be limited to low-risk estates for years.

**6. The wishes record will help, but less than the paper hopes.** In *Ilott v The Blue Cross* [2017] UKSC 17 the mother left a letter explaining why her daughter got nothing. The daughter still received an award [seen]. Reasons persuade families more often than they persuade judges. Also, anyone can enter a caveat for a small fee and block the grant for six months, renewable [seen]. Transparency does not remove that weapon. My guess is a modest fall in disputes, perhaps a quarter, not a half [conjecture].

**7. The "executable will" beside the signed will is a hazard.** Two sources of truth breed error. The structured rules will miss lapse, abatement, ademption, survivorship and construction points. When they differ from the signed will, the signed will governs, and the firm has distributed wrongly [reasoned]. Generate the rules from the will under a lawyer's check, and never let them stand apart from it.

**8. Embedding in hospices and care homes will be resisted.** Hospices guard their families fiercely. Care homes are often creditors of the estate. Bank bereavement teams already sell their own executor services [seen, practice]. Expect gatekeepers to refuse, or to demand fees that breach the paper's own no-commission clause [reasoned].

## Where it is too cautious

- **It accepts the grant as permanent.** The bold move is a "pre-validated estate". Some US states allow a will to be validated by a court in the testator's lifetime: Ohio, Arkansas, North Dakota and Alaska among them [seen, general knowledge; not checked]. Combine that with the capacity evidence the firm already collects. A member's will could be proved in life, with death triggering only identity and death checks. That removes capacity disputes, much of the caveat risk and most of the registry wait [conjecture]. It needs legislation. So does everything worth having here.
- **It ignores nominations.** Transfer-on-death designations let assets pass outside probate in the US [seen]. Pensions already work this way here through expression of wishes [seen]. The firm should press banks and platforms to offer nominations for members. Each nominated asset is one the grant no longer gates [reasoned].
- **The attorney's account is undersold.** Millions of lasting powers are registered [seen, general knowledge; figure not checked], and attorney abuse is a daily safeguarding concern [seen, practice]. This could be the largest product by 2035, not a £5 add-on. It also solves the readiness sales problem: the family is already engaged and already worried [reasoned].
- **Guide hours are probably too high in later years.** I agree families want a person. In my experience most want one decisive conversation and a reliable update, not twelve hours of contact [seen, practice]. Make the time available. Do not budget for every family to use it.

## The E1–E8 verdicts

- **E1 BENDS:** agree. Add that the record must hold liabilities and contingent claims, not only assets.
- **E2 BENDS:** agree. Holding no client money is right and wise. The paper may be too harsh in life: an attorney's account is in the money during life. Say so plainly.
- **E3 HOLDS, strongly:** **too generous.** A will is a standing policy written once, read literally and often wrongly. Half of deaths have no will at all. Construction, rectification and family provision claims override it. I would call this BENDS.
- **E4 BENDS:** agree.
- **E5 HOLDS:** **slightly generous.** It holds for process risk: missing beneficiaries, creditors, errors. It does not hold for family risk, which is the tail that matters. HOLDS for process, BENDS for outcomes.
- **E6 BENDS:** agree, but the chance of a small firm co-founding the standard is low. UK Finance, the Death Notification Service and government own this ground [seen]. The firm can contribute, not lead.
- **E7 BENDS:** agree, and well put. "The person is the front door" is right.
- **E8 BENDS:** agree on the logic. Too optimistic on access, for the reasons in point 8.

**Missing element:** I would add **authority and proof**. The architecture assumes the firm can act once the client says so. Here every counterparty waits for a court-issued token. A design that does not plan for that token, or plan to change it, is incomplete.

## Economics

The fixed fees are credible for clean estates. They will not survive houses with subsidence, sitting tenants, hoarding or a sibling living there rent-free [seen, practice]. Price those as exceptions, from the record, before the guarantee is given. Otherwise the guarantee will be paid for by the clean estates, and good clients will leave.
