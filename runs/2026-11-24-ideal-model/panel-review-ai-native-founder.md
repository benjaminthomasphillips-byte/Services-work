# Panel review: co-founder of an AI-native professional services firm

*Reviewer's seat: co-founder of a regulated, AI-native firm started within the last two years. Licensed professionals and engineers built it together, it sells at fixed prices, and it competes on speed. I mark what I know from running one as **[experience]** and what I am projecting as **[conjecture]**.*

## Verdict in one paragraph

This is a competent, tidy operating model. A good share of it I would sign up to tomorrow. But it is the operating model of the firm I am running now, written up neatly. It is not yet the design of the firm that leads in 2035. It is strong on the inside of the firm: pods, rhythm, principles, the engine. It is thin on the four things that actually decided whether we lived or died: liability, distribution, pricing as underwriting, and legal structure. Those four are also where the boldest moves to 2035 sit. Its claim to "win in all three landscapes" is a hedge that reads as caution. It ends by deferring the big choice, operator or platform or supplier, to 2033. By then the entity structure, the cap table and the insurance programme will long since have made that choice.

## Where it is wrong or would not survive contact

**1. Regulation and insurance sit under "weakest". They should be in the first ninety days.** [experience] Getting a firm authorised and getting cover for agent-made work took more founder time in our first year than building the product did. The insurer is not a constraint you discover late. It is a design partner. Your evaluation data, meaning holdouts, error rates and audit trails, is underwriting evidence. Public reports this year say Crosby's agent-specific cover, reportedly $5m, let it take lawyer review off roughly a fifth of its tasks. That is the right shape: autonomy is unlocked by a policy wording, not only by a model release. The draft treats the autonomy ladder as an engineering matter. In practice each rung is a negotiation with an insurer and, in some jurisdictions, a regulator. The quality function should own that relationship.

**2. "Win on experience, not price" is half right and misses the real job of the price.** [experience] Speed and live status win the first meeting. Fixed prices win the purchase. But a fixed price makes you an underwriter of scope. Your margin lives or dies on the tail cases a fixed price quietly absorbs. In our first year the main thing destroying margin was not model cost. It was the 8–10% of matters that turned out to be three matters. The draft lists "fixed per item" as a line in a customer-experience table. It needs a pricing and risk function from day one that prices the case mix, sets scope triggers and learns from outcomes. Step 1's best idea, using fast-check data to price the slow, high-margin layers, should be put to work here. Instead it vanishes from step 3.

**3. The pod assumes the scarce person is the engineer. It is the licensed expert.** [experience] Good engineers who like messy domains are hirable. Senior professionals who have judgement, a reputation, a willingness to sign off on machine work and the patience to teach it are rare. Sign-off capacity is your real throughput limit, and your regulatory key-person risk. Two more frictions. First, pods organised by type of work give a growing client four faces, when clients want one. Second, "no separate managers" breaks at around 25 to 30 people. I would organise pods by client segment, with work types as shared capabilities. I would also make a "principal pipeline" the single most important hiring plan in the document.

**4. Apprentices in year one are a luxury.** [experience] In 2027 you cannot afford the supervision, and regulators still decide what counts as qualifying experience. Hire experienced people squeezed out of mid-sized firms, as the draft half-says. **[conjecture]** By 2030 the profession's pipeline will be visibly broken, because nobody is paying juniors to do the work that trained them. The bold version is not "we have apprentices". It is "we become the training route": simulation, assessment and supervised live cases, accredited as a qualification route, then licensed to other firms. Whoever trains the next generation of signatories controls the scarcest input in 2035.

## What is missing that a firm like this must decide

**5. Distribution.** There is almost nothing on how clients are found. [experience] It is the hardest problem we have, and it is not solved by a better portal. The firms that grow fast are embedded in someone else's flow: investors' portfolio companies, banks, accounting and payroll platforms, marketplaces. **[conjecture]** By 2030 a large share of routine buying will be done by clients' agents comparing published offers. That makes landscape C a distribution question, not an API feature. The draft's choice is right: machine-readable service levels and verifiable outcomes. But it should become the main go-to-market thesis, not a row in a table marked "conjecture on timing".

**6. The competitor in 2035 is not the incumbent firm. It is the client's own agent plus software plus a hired signature.** **[conjecture]** In landscape A, much checkable work is done in-house by the client's agents. What clients buy from outside is accountability, insurance-backed assurance, and the hard tail of cases. The draft's three landscapes never name this substitution. Step 1 already saw it, noting that quick-to-check work becomes cheap or free. The design should answer it directly: sell assurance over work the client's agents do, not only work you do yourself.

**7. Legal structure and capital.** Nothing on entity design, ownership rules or how much to raise. [experience] This is the first fork. In England, an alternative business structure lets engineers and investors hold equity. Most US states still require a structure with management services organisation (MSO) and professional corporation (PC) entities, which complicates "broad equity". It constrains the 2033 end-forms the draft lists: a platform hosting independent experts and a supplier of the engine are different legal animals. It also sets your investors' expectations. A firm funded with venture money is pushed to look like software, and the pressure to cut the cost of checks is exactly how the "correlated error" in step 1 happens. The draft should state target gross margins, the share of revenue spent on tokens, insurance and checks, and what multiple the firm is built to earn.

**8. Incumbents will not stand still.** The phrase "incumbents cannot match without rebuilding themselves" is dated. [experience] Large firms are standing up AI-native subsidiaries, and private equity is buying books of clients and putting them onto platforms. Some of those platforms are better funded than we are. The draft's idea of buying the books of retiring owners is right, but buying a book is now a contested auction, and the firms are hard to integrate. [experience] The clients you buy are loyal to the old partner, not to your portal.

## Where it is too generic

Sections 6 and 7, the rhythm and the principles, are sound and interchangeable. "Write it down", "anyone can stop the line" and "earn autonomy with evidence" would fit any well-run software company. The ten design choices mostly describe how to run well, not where to stand. The citations from the sweep, such as Bird, FINN and Lexware, show that small teams work in software. They do not show the hard parts of services. Cut half of this and spend the words on liability, pricing and distribution.

The "wins in all three landscapes" stance is the clearest case of caution. You cannot be optimal in all three. A leader picks a primary bet and builds options for the others. **[conjecture]** I would bet on a mix of A and C: autonomy keeps rising and buying goes through agents. Then I would design to profit most if that happens and survive if B holds.

## The strongest ideas, to keep

- **The promise as specification, marketing and quality bar at once.** [experience] This is exactly how it works. Our promise became the backlog.
- **Quality and assurance independent of the pods, reporting to the board.** Make it stronger: it should own the insurer and regulator relationships.
- **The back office as the engine's first client.** It is cheap, safe and convincing in diligence.
- **Rent the intelligence, own the learning, with a model-upgrade harness.** Correct, and most firms do it badly.
- **Autonomy as reversible dials set case type by case type**, with lowering it after a failure treated as normal. This is the cultural point that matters most.
- **Agents with owners, budgets and records, and token spend budgeted next to payroll.**
- **Contribution pay judged by peers, and nobody paid on volume they also check.**

## Bold ideas it is missing

1. **Sell outcomes backed by a balance sheet.** Offer warranties or guarantees on results, for example "if this filing is rejected, we pay". Fund them from the error-rate data, and later through a captive insurer. **[conjecture]** By 2035 the leading firm looks partly like an insurer: its edge is pricing the risk of its own work better than anyone else.
2. **Assurance over other people's agents.** Sign off, audit and insure work done by clients' own agents. That is the human-accountability layer sold separately.
3. **One firm holding several licences.** Law, accounting, tax and property advice under one regulated roof, where rules allow it. **[conjecture]** Client problems do not respect the boundaries between professions, and agents erase the cost of crossing them.
4. **Write the standard.** Publish the evaluation methods and service-level schema, and help regulators and insurers adopt them. Whoever defines "good" for machine-delivered professional work sets the rules competitors are audited against.
5. **Own the training route** for the next generation of signatories, as above.
6. **Represent the client's agent.** **[conjecture]** In landscape C, deals and disputes increasingly run between agents. The firm becomes the accredited counterparty that negotiates, escrows and certifies on the client's behalf.
7. **Contracts that give the firm rights to learn from client data**, settled from the first contract, because P7 in step 1 depends on it and it is very hard to add later.
8. **Design partners who hold equity**, so the first clients become distribution.

## What I would do differently

I would open with four decisions before the pod chart:

- the entity and licence structure;
- the insurer partnership and how autonomy gets underwritten;
- the pricing and risk function;
- the distribution channel.

I would name a primary 2035 bet. And I would treat the human signature, backed by insurance, as the product the firm is really selling. Everything in the current draft can then sit underneath that as the way to produce it cheaply.
