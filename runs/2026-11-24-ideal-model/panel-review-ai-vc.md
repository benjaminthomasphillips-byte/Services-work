# Panel review: early-stage investor in AI applications and "services as software"

Reviewing step 3, "How we would build an AI-native services firm", draft of 24 November 2026.

I mark what I know from sitting through pitches, board meetings and follow-on rounds as **[experience]**, and what I am projecting as **[conjecture]**.

## Verdict in one paragraph

This is a well-made operating manual for a good services firm. It is not yet a design for a market leader, and it would not get through my partnership meeting in its current form. It describes how the firm works (pods, rhythm, principles, agents on the organisation chart). It says almost nothing about how the firm makes money, how it is financed, how it gets its clients, or what it is worth. Those questions decide the shape of every other choice. Worse, the sharpest idea in the whole series, step 1's point that the engine wins where margin disappears so profit has to move to the slow, risky layers, has dropped out of step 3. The draft is cautious exactly where 2035 calls for boldness. On price collapse, risk-bearing, the frontier labs as competitors, and capital structure, it either says nothing or defers the question to "later steps". Keep the bones. Rebuild the strategy layer on top of them.

## Where it is wrong, naive or would not survive contact

**1. There is no economic model, so there is no way to tell if the design works.** [experience] The first thing I ask any AI-native services founder for is three numbers by case type: gross margin after human review, revenue per head, and how both move over time. Services firms are valued at roughly one to three times revenue. Software-like businesses are valued at many times that. The whole venture case rests on showing that, as volume grows, headcount grows much more slowly than revenue and margin rises. The draft says pods grow "with little added headcount" in landscape A. That is a hope. It should be a target with a dashboard: people per £1m of revenue, review minutes per case, tokens as a share of revenue, and the share of cases closed with no human touch. Without these numbers the "monthly economics" meeting has nothing to steer by.

**2. "Win on experience, not price" is half right, and it is the wrong half to lead with.** [experience] The companies I see growing fastest sell into a budget line that already exists. They price at a clear discount to what the client already spends, and they win on speed as well. Experience gets you a trial. A cheaper line in an existing budget gets you the renewal and the expansion. Enterprise buyers also buy reduced risk, and that is a third thing again. More important, step 1 says quick-to-check work tends towards zero. So a firm that wins on experience in routine work is winning a race whose prize is shrinking. The wedge should be chosen for where it leads: towards work where the firm can price risk, outcomes and accountability.

**3. The draft avoids the greenfield-or-roll-up choice, and that choice now dominates the market.** [experience] Since 2024 much of the capital in this category has gone into buying traditional firms and putting AI into them. General Catalyst set aside a reported $1.5bn for the strategy, and Thrive, Lightspeed and 8VC have followed. Eudia's Series A was largely conditional on its making acquisitions. The draft mentions buying books of clients in 2028 as an "option". That is backwards. Whether you build from scratch, buy, or do both decides your cap table, your culture, your licences and your speed to scale in 2027. My view: greenfield firms have better engines and worse distribution. Roll-ups have the clients and licences but old habits that are hard to shift, and integration has gone more slowly than the pitch decks claimed. The best answer I have seen is to build the engine on a greenfield wedge for 12 to 18 months, then buy firms for their clients and licences, never for their methods. The draft should make this choice explicitly.

**4. The 2033 "choose the end form" is a decision that gets made in 2027 whether you like it or not.** [experience] An operator, a platform for independent experts and an engine supplier to other firms each need different entities, different investors and different hires, and they conflict with each other. If you sell your engine to other firms, they become your competitors. The legal structure (in England an alternative business structure, in the US a separate professional corporation alongside a management company) and the first priced round fix most of this. Pick the main end form now and keep the others as options. Do not leave them as a menu for 2033.

## What is missing, especially looking to 2035

**5. A fourth landscape: work gets so cheap that revenue shrinks while volume grows.** [conjecture, grounded in step 1] The three landscapes describe how capable the models become. None describes what happens to price. If routine legal, accounting and compliance work falls tenfold in price by 2032, a firm can do twice the work and still see revenue halve. Some markets will expand as work gets cheaper. Others will not, because a company only needs one annual audit. The design needs an answer, and the only good ones I know are to move up into bearing risk and to price on outcomes.

**6. The firm should carry risk on its balance sheet: warranties, guarantees and, in time, its own insurer.** [conjecture] This is the boldest move the draft misses, and step 1 points straight at it. The firm with the best error data in its niche can do more than buy insurance. It can sell outcome guarantees ("if this filing is rejected, we pay"), price them better than anyone else, and in time hold that risk in a captive insurer. By 2035 the margin in many services will sit in assurance and underwriting, not production. A firm that keeps the risk becomes something closer to a specialist insurer that happens to do the work. That is a different and more valuable business.

**7. The frontier labs, and the platforms clients already use, are the 2035 competitor. Not the incumbent firm.** [conjecture] Labs are already building forward-deployed teams and vertical products. Accounting, payroll and banking platforms already own the client relationship and the data. "Rent the intelligence" is right for cost and speed. But the draft never asks what happens when the landlord moves into the house, and that is the central strategic risk for a firm in this category. Two answers. First, own what the labs cannot easily get: licences, accountability, outcome data and liability. Second, be the regulated, insured layer that sits on top of other people's agents, so the firm assures work done by the client's own agents as well as doing work itself.

**8. Expert judgement is a product in its own right.** [experience on the market; conjecture on the firm] Companies that supply expert-graded training data to labs grew very quickly in 2025. A services firm whose experts write test suites and grade edge cases every day is producing that asset as a by-product. Client data cannot be sold. But anonymised evaluation sets, synthetic cases and expert grading capacity can be, subject to consent. That is a second revenue line and a way to make the labs partners rather than threats.

**9. Distribution.** [experience] It is the hardest problem in the category and the draft spends one line on it. Landscape C, where clients buy through their own agents, is a distribution thesis and should be the main go-to-market idea, not a row marked "conjecture on timing". Until then, the winners I see sit inside someone else's channel: banks, payroll platforms, investors' portfolios, marketplaces.

## Where it is too generic

Most of sections 6 and 7 could appear in any 2026 operating deck: write it down, stop the line, burden of proof on complexity, high-agency generalists, few meetings. None of it is wrong, and none of it is a reason the firm wins. "Agents on the organisation chart" is a nice image but has nothing to do with what the firm is worth. I would cut those sections to half a page and spend the space on pricing, capital and risk. The ten design choices also contain no number, no named market and no stated trade-off. Any one of them could be signed by a rival.

## The strongest ideas, to keep

- **The three landscapes as a stress test.** Very few founders do this. Add the price-collapse landscape and keep the method.
- **The promise as the specification.** One public, measurable promise that defines the engine, the marketing and the quality bar. This is the best go-to-market idea in the draft.
- **Autonomy as a dial, set case type by case type, with the right to turn it back down.** Investors should ask for it in diligence.
- **Quality reporting to the board, not to the person who runs the pods.** This is the right governance, and it doubles as underwriting evidence for insurers.
- **The model-upgrade harness** and "rent the intelligence; own the learning". A new model release should be a good day for the firm, not a frightening one.
- **The back office as the engine's first client.** Cheap, safe, and a working demonstration.
- **Being easy for machines to read** (published service levels, outcomes that can be verified, an interface agents can call). This is the right bet for 2035.

## What I would do differently if I were backing it

1. **Lead with economics.** Set target gross margins and revenue per head for 2028, 2030 and 2035 under each landscape, and report them by case type every month.
2. **Choose the wedge for its route to risk, not only for client pain.** Prefer work where outcomes can be verified, where good error data is valuable to an insurer, and where expansion moves into higher-margin, slow-to-check layers.
3. **Make the greenfield, then buy, choice in 2027.** Raise for it explicitly, and structure the entity so engineers and experts can hold equity.
4. **Put pricing and risk alongside the pods as a core function from day one.** It sets fixed prices, triggers for changes in scope, guarantees and, later, the captive insurer.
5. **Write a strategy for the labs and the platforms**: what the firm will never depend on them for, and where it will partner.
6. **Treat evaluation assets as a second product line** from 2028, subject to consent and licensing.
7. **Halve the operating principles** and replace them with three to five bets that rivals would not make.

The draft builds a firm that would be excellent to work at. My job is to ask whether it would be worth ten times its revenue in 2035. As written, I cannot tell. With the economics, the risk layer and a strategy for the labs added, I think it could be.
