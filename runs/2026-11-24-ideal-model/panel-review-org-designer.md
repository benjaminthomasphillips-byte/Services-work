# Panel review: the organisational designer

*Role: organisational designer and researcher. I study how firms are structured and run: lean and Toyota, professional-service partnerships, and the newer work on AI-native organisations (Garicano on knowledge hierarchies, Puranam on the division of labour between people and algorithms, Kim and Koning on start-up shape). Throughout, I mark **[experience]** for what I know from studying and advising firms, and **[conjecture]** for where I am reasoning forward.*

## My verdict in a paragraph

This draft has a sound core. The separation of powers between maker and evaluator, autonomy as a dial set case type by case type, and the back office as the engine's first client are all well chosen and should be kept. As an organisation, though, it is underdesigned in exactly the places where firms fail. It describes the unit (the pod) and lists the parts of the centre, but it does not say how pods are joined together, how the expert is protected from being used up, who pays for training once there is no pyramid, or what legal and ownership form the firm takes. Those four choices matter more in 2035 than any of the ten listed. Much of section 7 could be the handbook of any well-run software company. The client asked for boldness. The boldest organisational question here is who owns the firm and who carries its risk, and the draft does not ask it.

## Where it is wrong or naive

**1. The pod is a unit with no theory of the joins.** [experience] Every structure built around autonomous small teams (Spotify's squads, Amazon's two-pizza teams, holacracy's circles) runs into trouble at the seams, not inside the teams. Here the seams are sharp. A client buys from three pods but wants one relationship. The platform team and the pods will disagree about who decides the shape of the case system. Conway's law then makes the engine look like the org chart. The draft gives the centre "anything that crosses pods" and leaves it there. That is not a design, it is a dumping ground. I would set out decision rights explicitly: who owns the client across pods, who can fork a playbook, who can refuse a platform change. And I would accept that the client lead, rather than the case type, may be the stronger organising axis once a client buys more than one thing.

**2. Garicano's hierarchy is the design, and the draft does not draw it.** [experience for the theory; conjecture for the numbers] Garicano's knowledge-hierarchy model says that routine problems are solved at the bottom and exceptions are passed up to people whose knowledge is costlier. AI makes knowledge cheaper to acquire at the bottom and communication cheaper going up. Both widen spans of control. In this firm the agents are the bottom layer, and the exception desk and the principal are the layers above. Step 1 already admits that the human work "concentrates": what reaches people is the hardest residue. So the principal's span is the binding constraint on the whole firm. By 2030 one principal may be accountable for thousands of cases a week, and the exceptions they see will be harder each year. The draft needs an explicit escalation hierarchy with tiers, response times and limits on span. The "ladder of judgement" needs rungs with names, and it needs a rule for when a pod splits because its principal is saturated, not because its headcount is.

**3. Apprentices without leverage: who pays?** [experience] The partnership pyramid was never mainly a teaching device. It was a financing device: clients paid for juniors' hours while they learned. Take away the billable junior and training becomes a pure cost, borne by a firm whose competitors will happily poach the finished product. The draft's apprentice programme has no economics. My view is to treat it as a residency, as medicine does. Make it a funded cost centre with a fixed cohort and a two-to-three-year bond or clawback. Make simulation the main teaching tool rather than an extra. Better still, consider whether training can be a business in itself, licensed to the profession [conjecture]. By 2032 the shortage of people who can sign for a system may be the industry's real constraint, and the firm that trains them owns the supply.

**4. The "contribution" pay component will become political.** [experience] Peer-judged credit for reusable knowledge has been tried many times, in the big consultancies' knowledge-management drives of the 1990s and 2000s and in internal "kudos" systems. It decays into lobbying and reciprocal back-scratching, or it becomes a box-ticking exercise. The deeper problem is one the draft names in section 10 and then waves away: experts are being asked to encode the knowledge that makes them replaceable. That is a hold-up problem, and bonuses do not solve hold-up. Ownership does. Partnerships solved it with a residual claim and a tenure promise. I would give principals something closer to a royalty: a vesting share of the margin of the case types they built and still govern, which falls away if they stop governing. Then teaching the system is a capital decision and not a favour.

**5. "Agents are staff" is the wrong metaphor, and it will cost you.** [experience on accountability; conjecture on the cost] Identities, permissions, budgets and logs are right. Org-chart membership, onboarding and "performance reviews" for agents are theatre, and theatre has a price. It blurs accountability. When something goes wrong, the regulator, the insurer and the court will ask which named human owned the decision. An agent is a versioned configuration, closer to plant and equipment than to an employee. I would model each one as a *position* held by an accountable person, and record every agent change in a change log, not in an HR file. The agent itself should carry no seat of its own. This matters more, not less, in landscape A.

**6. Andon without a team leader is alarm fatigue.** [experience] Toyota's stop-the-line works because a team leader, typically with about five operators, responds within seconds, and because the system is designed so that stopping is rare and cheap. Let a fleet of agents pull the cord and you will get either constant false halts or a cord everyone learns to ignore. The design needs a named responder for each case type, a response-time standard, and a measured false-stop rate. Toyota also pairs kaizen with *hoshin kanri*, where strategy is cascaded so that local improvement points the same way. Pods improving their own work each day with no shared direction will optimise locally.

**7. Buying books of clients is presented as a volume play. It is an integration play.** [experience] Roll-ups of professional firms fail on culture and retention far more than on systems. "Moving them straight onto the system" means moving their people onto a model that makes many of them redundant and the rest junior to an engine. The design needs an integration doctrine: who is kept, how acquired principals become evaluator owners, and what happens to the acquired brand.

## What is missing that the firm must decide

- **Legal form and ownership.** In regulated work this is the first organisational decision, not a detail. Alternative business structures in England and Wales, the Arizona and Utah regimes, and the management-services-company workaround elsewhere determine who may own the firm, who may sign, and how outside capital and engineers share in profits. An investor-backed firm whose principals are employees has different incentives from a partnership with a technology company bolted on. I would set this out as an explicit choice with options.
- **Who carries the risk.** [conjecture] By the 2030s I expect the decisive advantage to be the willingness to *warrant* outcomes: fixed price plus a guarantee, backed by the firm's own error data and perhaps a captive insurer. That changes the organisation. Underwriting and actuarial skill become core, quality becomes a pricing function as well as a policing one, and the balance sheet becomes part of the operating model.
- **Integration roles and culture at small scale.** A firm of 40 people and thousands of agents has very few people to carry its culture. Too many of them are senior and scarce, and too few are juniors who absorb norms by watching. What holds it together? I would design rituals for the people and deliberately rotate them between pods, the quality function and the platform.
- **Governance of the shared record.** "Coordination through shared state" is right. But shared state rots without an owner. [experience] Wikis and handbooks decay within a year unless someone's job is to curate them. Someone must own the firm's world model as a product.
- **Adversarial agents.** Landscape C is framed as clients' agents reading the firm. Counterparties' agents will also be negotiating against it, probing its playbooks and learning its thresholds. That calls for a red-team function and rules on what the firm publishes.
- **The labs as competitors.** [conjecture] If model providers or the large software platforms move down into services, a firm that rents its intelligence must decide early what it will never let a supplier see.

## Where it is too cautious or generic

The ten principles, the operating rhythm and "write it down" are good practice, but they are commodity. The end forms in section 9 (operator, platform, supplier) are deferred to 2033. I would decide the platform question much earlier. If the engine and the evaluation function are the fixed-cost asset, as step 1 argues, then letting independent experts work on the firm's engine under its licence from around 2029 is the fastest way to spread that cost and solve the talent shortage at the same time. That makes the firm a federation from early on, not as a fallback. I would also be bolder on headcount. Rather than "headcount barely grows", I would set an explicit target, such as revenue per accountable person, and design against it.

## The strongest ideas, to keep

- The evaluator is separate from the maker, and quality reports to the board. This is the best idea in the draft. [Experience: it works when quality people rotate back into live work. It decays into compliance if they never do.]
- Autonomy as a dial per case type, where lowering it is normal and no disgrace. This is the cultural keystone.
- Nobody is paid on volume they also check.
- The back office as the engine's first client.
- The public promise used as the specification, the marketing and the quality bar all at once.
- A burden of proof on adding complexity.
- Three landscapes as a stress test, rather than a forecast.

## What I would do differently if backing it

I would make five changes. Draw the Garicano hierarchy and design to the principal's span. Pick a legal and ownership form that gives principals a residual claim on what they build. Fund apprenticeship as a residency, with bonds. Treat agents as positions held by named people. And go into warranties and a federated platform years earlier than 2033. The pod is a good cell. The firm still needs its skeleton.
