# Panel review: an investor in AI-enabled roll-ups and holding companies

Review of "Step 3. How we would build an AI-native services firm", draft of 24 November 2026.

I back companies that buy services businesses and change how they work with AI, in the creation-company style that General Catalyst, Thrive and others have made familiar since 2024. My bias is plain: in services, distribution and market choice decide most outcomes, and buying a client base often beats earning it one client at a time. I mark what I know from deals as **[experience]** and what I am guessing about 2027 to 2035 as **[conjecture]**.

## My verdict in one paragraph

This is a good operating design and a weak strategy. Inside the firm it is thoughtful: the independent quality function, the model-upgrade harness, autonomy as a dial set per case type, and the back office as the engine's first client are all things I would want to see in diligence. But it answers "how should the firm be organised?" and leaves out "where does the firm play, how does it get clients, and how is it financed?" Those three questions decide whether it ever reaches the scale where the organisation matters. It assumes a start-up founding: a wedge, design partners, and growth earned through a better experience. In most professional services that is the slowest and most expensive way to reach scale. Buying appears once, as "possibly", in 2028 to 2030. I would put it at the centre of the design from the start, or at least make it a choice the draft argues for or against rather than a footnote.

## Where it is wrong or naive

**1. "Win on experience, not price" works for only a thin slice of buyers.** [experience] Owner-managed businesses rarely leave their accountant, broker or solicitor. Client retention in small-practice accountancy and commercial broking is routinely in the low-to-mid 90s per cent a year, and it barely moves when a rival offers a better portal. People switch at moments of change: a business is founded, funds are raised, an adviser retires, a crisis hits. Crosby chose fast-growing companies precisely because they are always in a moment of change. The draft takes that as one wedge criterion among seven. It should be the first. Outside that slice you either win clients at switching moments or you buy the relationship. The draft admits the risk in section 10 but has no answer to it.

**2. Customer acquisition cost is not in the design at all.** [experience] The founders of from-scratch AI-native firms that I meet do not usually fail on the engine. They fail because each new SME client costs more to win than two or three years of its fees, and the sales cycle runs on trust they do not yet have. Section 4's table of client experience is a product spec, not a go-to-market plan. Nothing in the draft covers channels (banks, payroll and accounting software, insurers, vertical SaaS, landlords' platforms), referral economics, or the role of the existing professional's personal relationship. Step 2 had the key insight: books from the squeezed middle should be priced as customer acquisition, not on profit multiples. Step 3 dropped it.

**3. It refuses to choose a market, and market choice is most of the answer.** The draft says it "does not assume a particular service" and defers this to a later step. The wedge criteria (digital inputs, patterned work, checkable outcomes) are engine criteria. The criteria I screen on are missing:
- **fragmentation**: thousands of small firms and no dominant brand;
- **succession pressure**: the share of owners over 60 with no successor [experience: this is what makes sellers rational at sensible prices];
- **a shrinking supply of qualified people** (the US shortage of new CPAs is the textbook case);
- **recurring, contracted revenue**;
- **ownership rules**: who is allowed to own the firm.

That last one is decisive and absent. The UK allows alternative business structures. In the US, outside Arizona and Utah, a law firm cannot have non-lawyer owners, and accountancy needs the alternative practice structure that splits attest work from the rest. Whether outside capital can own the licensed entity determines whether this design is even legal in a given market.

**4. It assumes incumbents stand still.** The promise is built to be one "that incumbents cannot match without rebuilding themselves". [experience] Incumbents are being rebuilt right now, by people like me. Long Lake has bought dozens of community-association managers and runs them on one platform. Titan is doing the same in IT services, and accounting roll-ups backed by private equity are putting the same models into thousands of practices. By 2028 the AI-native start-up's real competitor is not a sleepy firm billing by the hour. It is a well-funded holding company with 50,000 clients, the same frontier models and an integration team. The draft needs a view on why the from-scratch firm beats that competitor, or why it should become one.

**5. The pod is expensive, and it is built for a greenfield culture.** A licensed principal plus an engineer for each type of work is a premium cost base. Engineers who like messy domains are the scarcest hire in this market [experience]. More importantly, the design assumes everyone was hired into it. In an acquired firm, 70 per cent of the people did not choose this way of working, and the clients' engagement letters may not even allow their files to be used to train the engine. Integration is the hard part of what I do, and the draft has nothing on it: change management, which staff are kept, data rights on acquisition, and migrating clients off old systems. I would make integration itself a case type on the engine, with a playbook, a test suite and measured outcomes. A firm that can absorb a 40-person practice in 90 days has a moat that a better portal cannot match.

## Where it is too generic

Sections 6 and 7 (the operating rhythm and the principles) could be pasted into any well-run software company's handbook. "Write it down", "anyone can stop the line" and "add complexity only with proof" are true, but they do not set this firm apart. The ten design choices are about how to organise, not where to win. The client has rightly complained about vanilla work, and these sections are where it shows. I would cut them to half a page and spend the space on markets, distribution and capital.

## What it fails to anticipate, 2027 to 2035 [conjecture]

- **Capital structure as strategy.** Venture money, a permanent-capital holding company and a buyout fund with debt each produce a different firm. The selling point of an AI roll-up is margin expansion on acquired revenue, financed partly with debt. Whether 2030 lenders will lend against AI-driven margins, before those margins are proven, is the key financing question. The draft leaves the end form until 2033 to 2035. By then the capital raised in 2027 will already have chosen it.
- **The firm as the balance sheet.** Step 2 argued that carrying risk while controlling the work earns more than either alone. Taken to 2035, the winner warrants its outcomes: it owns or partners with a managing general agent (an insurance underwriting agency), prices its own error rate, and sells "guaranteed compliant" rather than "reviewed". That is a bolder end form than "large operator or platform", and the draft does not name it.
- **Distribution is embedded, not bought.** In landscape C, clients' agents will mostly be supplied by banks, accounting software, payroll providers and the model companies. Whoever sets the default supplier in those agents controls demand. The bold move is to become the default service behind a platform with millions of SMEs, through a partnership or by being acquired by it. That matters more than publishing an API.
- **The model companies as competitors or acquirers.** By 2030 at least one frontier lab or hyperscaler may offer regulated services itself, or buy a firm that does. The draft treats models as a utility to rent. It should also consider them as a possible rival and a likely exit.
- **Regulators as a moat.** The firm whose audit trail regulators trust will be the one permitted to raise autonomy first. Garfield's approval by the Solicitors Regulation Authority shows the gate exists. Aim to be the template the regulator writes its rules around, not merely compliant with them.
- **Licences as inventory.** If landscape A arrives, the scarce input is a person allowed to sign, not a person able to do the work. Buying firms buys signers. That favours buying over building as the decade goes on.

## The strongest ideas, to keep

- An **independent quality function reporting to the board**, which holds the holdout cases. This is what lenders, insurers and acquirers will diligence, and it is rare.
- A **model-upgrade harness that promotes or holds each case type** separately. This turns model releases into planned upgrades rather than shocks.
- **Autonomy as a dial**, earned and lowered by evidence for each case type. It survives both landscape A and landscape B.
- **The back office as the engine's first client**: no client risk, and a live demonstration.
- **The single public promise** as spec, marketing and quality bar at once.
- **Contribution pay judged by peers rather than by usage counts**, and the rule that no one is paid on volume they also check.
- **Legibility to machines** (landscape C) as a design requirement from day one.

## What I would do differently

I would run it as **build, then buy**. Spend 12 to 18 months building the engine in one wedge, with design partners chosen at moments of change, until the diligence test from step 1 passes on real cases. Then buy 3 to 5 sub-scale firms in the same wedge, priced as customer acquisition, and use them to prove the integration playbook. Choose the market first, using the ownership rules and the succession and fragmentation screens above, before designing a single pod. Pick the capital structure deliberately in 2027, with the end form in mind. And give the 2035 thesis an edge: an operator that warrants its own outcomes and is the default supplier behind the platforms SMEs already use. Without those choices the design is a fine way to run a firm that may never reach scale.
