# Panel synthesis: step 3, building the firm

Five reviewers read the draft of 24 November 2026: a design-led product builder, the co-founder of an AI-native firm, an investor in AI applications, an investor in AI roll-ups, and an organisational designer. Each marked what they know from practice as **[experience]** and what they are projecting as **[conjecture]**. This synthesis keeps that split. Where a point rests on a reviewer's practice, it says so. Where it is a view about 2030 to 2035, it says that too.

The overall verdict is the same from all five: **a good operating design with no strategy layer.** The draft explains how the firm runs inside. It hardly says where the firm plays, how it makes money, how it finds clients, who owns it or who carries its risk. Every reviewer says the draft is most cautious at exactly the points where 2035 calls for boldness.

---

## 1. Where reviewers agree against the draft

**The firm should carry risk, not just do work (all five).** Every reviewer names warranted outcomes as the biggest missing idea: a fixed price plus a guarantee ("if this filing is rejected, we pay"), priced from the engine's own error data and later held in a captive insurer or a managing general agent. The founder, the AI investor and the roll-up investor all point out that step 1's sharpest idea, that profit moves to the slow, risky layers, has dropped out of step 3. This is **conjecture** in all five reviews. The founder adds **experience** beneath it: insurers are design partners, and getting cover for agent-made work took more founder time than the product did.

**Legal form, ownership and capital are first decisions, not later ones (founder, AI investor, roll-up investor, organisational designer).** All four, from **experience**, say that the entity structure (alternative business structures in England, professional corporations plus management companies in most US states, Arizona and Utah as exceptions) decides who can own equity, who can sign, and which end forms are open. The roll-up investor goes furthest: ownership rules decide whether the design is even legal in a given market.

**The end form cannot wait until 2033 (founder, AI investor, roll-up investor, organisational designer).** The first priced round and the cap table make that choice in 2027. Operator, platform and engine supplier conflict with each other. A firm that sells its engine to rivals arms its competitors.

**Distribution is missing (founder, AI investor, roll-up investor; the product builder in part).** From **experience**, all three say distribution is the hardest problem in the category, and that fast-growing firms sit inside someone else's channel: banks, payroll and accounting software, investors' portfolios, marketplaces. The roll-up investor adds acquisition cost, which is absent from the draft.

**There are no numbers (AI investor, product builder, organisational designer).** No target margins, no revenue per head, no headcount ceiling. "Headcount barely grows" is a hope, not a target.

**Sections 6 and 7 are generic (all five).** The rhythm and principles could sit in any software firm's handbook. Three reviewers would cut them to half a page.

**The real competitor is not the sleepy incumbent (all five, in different forms).** Named rivals: software platforms bundling "done for you" services (product builder), the client's own agent plus a hired signature (founder), frontier labs and the platforms clients already use (AI investor, roll-up investor, organisational designer), and well-funded roll-ups already rebuilding incumbents (roll-up investor and founder, from **experience**).

**Pods break at the joins (product builder, founder, organisational designer).** Pods by type of work give a client several faces and several experiences. The product builder cites Conway's law from **experience** of autonomous squads. The organisational designer says the centre's remit, "anything that crosses pods", is a dumping ground, not a design.

**Agents are not staff (product builder, organisational designer).** Keep the identities, owners, budgets and logs. Drop the org-chart membership and performance reviews. Regulators, insurers and courts will ask which named person owned the decision. Both speak from **experience**.

## 2. Where they disagree, and why

**Experience against price.** The product builder, a craft-first builder, backs winning on experience. The buyers' side disagrees. The AI investor (**experience**): growth comes from a discount into a budget line that already exists. The roll-up investor (**experience**): clients of small practices stay at rates in the low-to-mid 90s per cent and switch only at moments of change. The founder (**experience**): a fixed price makes you an underwriter of scope, and the 8–10% of matters that turn into three matters destroy margin. In short, builders see the product, while investors and operators see switching costs and the tail.

**Build or buy.** The two investors want build, then buy: 12 to 18 months on a greenfield wedge, then acquire firms for clients and licences, priced as customer acquisition. The founder and the organisational designer warn, from **experience**, that books are now a contested auction, clients stay loyal to the old partner, and roll-ups fail on culture. The product builder warns that hiring from billable-hour firms imports habits. In short, capital sees speed to scale and practitioners see integration cost.

**What the pod is organised around.** The product builder wants a central design owner with a veto over everything clients touch. The founder and the organisational designer would organise around the client segment or client lead, with work types as shared capabilities. The roll-up investor thinks the pod is too expensive for acquired firms.

**What is scarce.** The founder: the licensed expert willing to sign. The roll-up investor: engineers who like messy domains. The organisational designer: the principal's span of attention. These are different bottlenecks, and each implies a different hiring plan.

**API or channel.** The product builder wants an API first, with the portal built on top. The roll-up investor says being the default supplier behind a platform with millions of small firms matters more than publishing an API. The founder and the AI investor sit between: being readable by machines is the go-to-market thesis, but it works through channels.

**How much to publish.** The product builder and the founder want to open playbooks and write the standard. The organisational designer warns that counterparties' agents will probe the firm's thresholds, so it needs a red team and rules on what it publishes.

**Pay for contribution.** Four reviewers keep peer-judged contribution pay. The organisational designer says from **experience** that it decays into lobbying, and that hold-up is solved by ownership: a royalty on the margin of the case types a principal built and still governs.

**Hedge or bet.** The founder wants a named primary bet (landscapes A and C together). The AI investor wants a fourth landscape added, not a bet chosen. The rest accept the three landscapes as a stress test.

## 3. Bold ideas missing, ranked by how much they would change the design

All of these are **conjecture** unless marked.

1. **Warranted outcomes and the firm as balance sheet** (all five). This changes the product, pricing, quality (which becomes a pricing function too), capital needs and the end form. By 2035 the firm may look like a specialist insurer that also does the work.
2. **Decide structure, capital and end form in 2027** (four reviewers, **experience**). This turns section 9 from a menu into a commitment and constrains everything else.
3. **Sell assurance over other people's agents** (founder, AI investor). If clients' agents do the checkable work in-house, the firm signs, audits and insures that work. This answers both the client's-own-agent threat and the labs.
4. **Embedded distribution and agent-mediated buying as the main go-to-market** (founder, AI investor, roll-up investor). The goal is to become the default supplier behind banks, payroll and accounting platforms.
5. **Build, then buy, with integration as a case type on the engine** (roll-up investor, AI investor). The aim is to absorb a 40-person practice in 90 days.
6. **A fourth landscape: price collapse** (AI investor). Volume rises and revenue falls. This forces the move up into risk and outcome pricing.
7. **Own the training route for signatories** (founder, organisational designer). Simulation and supervised cases, accredited and licensed to the profession. Whoever trains signers controls the scarcest input.
8. **Design to the principal's span** (organisational designer). Draw Garicano's hierarchy, name the escalation tiers, and split a pod when its principal is saturated, not when its headcount grows.
9. **Write the standard regulators adopt** (founder, roll-up investor, product builder). Be the template, not just compliant.
10. **A federated platform from around 2029, or one firm holding several licences** (organisational designer, product builder, founder).
11. **Smaller but distinctive moves:** evaluation sets as a second product line (AI investor); the invisible, contact-free service and a public changelog (product builder); a published "no" list and a headcount ceiling (product builder); client contracts that grant learning rights from the start; design partners holding equity (founder).

## 4. The strongest ideas to keep

Named by all five:

- **The single public promise** as specification, marketing and quality bar. The founder confirms from **experience**: "our promise became the backlog".
- **Quality independent of the pods, reporting to the board.** It is also underwriting and diligence evidence. The founder would give it the insurer and regulator relationships.
- **The back office as the engine's first client.**
- **Autonomy as a reversible dial for each case type**, where lowering it is no disgrace.

Named by most:

- **Rent the intelligence; own the learning**, with the model-upgrade harness (four).
- **Nobody is paid on volume they also check** (four).
- **Three landscapes as a stress test** (three).
- **Legibility to machines** (three).
- **A burden of proof on complexity** (two).

---

## 5. Ranked changes to the draft

1. **New section before section 2: "Four decisions before the pod chart."** These are entity and licence structure, the insurer partnership, the pricing and risk function, and the distribution channel. Pull "regulation and insurance" out of section 10 and into this section.
2. **The answer in brief and section 4: make warranted outcomes the product.** Add design choice 11, "Sell a guarantee, not work". Recast the experience table so that fixed price and guarantee lead, and restore step 1's argument about profit moving to the slow layers.
3. **Section 1: add landscape D (price collapse)**, name the client's own agent and the labs and platforms as competitors, and state a primary bet with options kept open.
4. **Section 9: choose build, then buy, and the main end form now.** Add an integration doctrine and data rights on acquisition.
5. **New economics block (sections 2 and 6):** target gross margin, revenue per accountable person, review minutes per case, tokens and insurance as a share of revenue, and the share of cases closed with no human touch, under each landscape for 2028, 2030 and 2035.
6. **Section 3, the pod and the centre:** add explicit decision rights at the joins; consider organising by client segment; add a central owner of the client surface; draw the escalation hierarchy and design to the principal's span; add a pricing and risk function.
7. **Section 3, agents:** recast them as versioned deployments or positions held by named people, with changes logged in a change log, not HR files.
8. **Section 3, pay and apprentices:** add a principal royalty and fund the apprenticeship as a residency with bonds. Note the training-route option.
9. **Section 4: build API-first**, with the portal as the first client of the API, and tie it to channel partnerships.
10. **Sections 6 and 7: cut by half.** Replace most of the cadence with a zero-exception-debt rule. Give the andon cord a named responder and a false-stop measure. Add three to five bets rivals would not make, and a "no" list.
11. **Wedge criteria (section 2):** put moments of change first, and add the route to risk, fragmentation, succession pressure and ownership rules.
