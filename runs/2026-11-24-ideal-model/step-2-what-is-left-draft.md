# Step 2. If the engine works, what becomes a commodity, and what is left?

Draft, 24 November 2026, before review.

## The answer in brief

1. **Commoditisation is decided by the market, not the technology.** A fall in the supplier's cost becomes a fall in price only when buyers can see it, compare, switch or do the work themselves. Where they cannot, suppliers have kept the gain, even raised prices. US law firm rates rose while translation prices fell (ledger Q5).
2. **The bigger force is on the buyer's side.** Professional services have long been what economists call credence goods: the buyer cannot judge the quality even after buying, so they pay for trust. Much of the margin rested on that information gap. AI closes it from the buyer's side. A client's own AI can now check the accountant's work, and one in five US accounting clients say they already have (R-WEB-COMMERCIAL-25; a vendor's survey). When the buyer can judge the work, opacity stops protecting the margin. This erodes margins faster and further than supplier costs falling.
3. **Mandated and discretionary services behave differently when prices fall.**
   - **Mandated services:** for compliance work the law requires (filings, statutory inspections), demand barely grows when price falls, so the saving goes to clients and to whoever controls the point of sale.
   - **Discretionary services:** for advice, recovery, coverage and monitoring, much more is bought when it gets cheap. There the low-cost leader captures the expansion.
4. **What is left are things that stay scarce even when both sides have AI:**
   - carrying the consequence (licence and balance sheet);
   - judgement where nobody, human or machine, can verify the answer;
   - moving other parties and the physical world;
   - holding the client's live records;
   - standing with institutions;
   - being the one trusted in a crisis.
5. **The likely shape is a barbell.**
   - The routine tier is priced near cost.
   - The accountable and judgement tier carries a premium.
   - The middle (bundled capacity with modest know-how, sold by the hour) is squeezed.

   Comparable shifts took five to fifteen years, not two, and left a lasting assisted market. In the US, about half of taxpayers still pay a preparer despite thirty years of tax software (general knowledge).
6. **For a 2035 leader:** sell the commodity tier near cost to win the relationship, the live records and the volume of data. Earn the margin on the scarce layers. Choose services where demand grows when price falls, or where the firm can own the point of need.

## 1. What a services firm sells, and what happens to each part

Unit 1 split a service into five parts. Taking each in turn:

| Part | Becomes a commodity? | Why | What is left |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Capacity** (hours of work) | Yes, fastest | Agents supply it at the cost of compute plus checking | Nothing, except where checking stays expensive |
| **Codified know-how** | Yes, as models absorb written practice | Public rules and practice guides are in the models; playbooks are quick to copy (R-LIB-ECONOMICS-33; R-WEB-STACK-23) | Unwritten judgement on hard cases; the firm's own outcome data (step 1, P7, with its legal limits) |
| **Accountability** | No; becomes the core product | A liable party needs a licence and capital; regulators require a named person (R-WEB-LAW-16) | The signature, insurance and the capacity to warrant results |
| **Coordination** | Partly | Agents can chase, schedule and file, but other parties and physical work move at their own speed | A network of trusted doers, standing with counterparties, and performance data on both |
| **Trust** | Changes form | For routine work, trust shifts from the person to a published record that clients' agents can read. In a crisis, it stays personal | Track record as an asset; the person the client calls when it matters |

Two parts that unit 1 did not list matter more by 2035:

- **Ownership of the client's state.** Being where the client's ledger, compliance record or case file lives.
- **Standing.** Licences to act, access to government and registry gateways, panel memberships, recognition by courts and regulators.

## 2. The premises

### Q1. A cost fall becomes a price fall only where buyers can see, compare, switch or insource
**Tier: trend for the pattern; mechanism for the conditions.**

- **Claim.** Four conditions convert a supplier's lower cost into a lower price:
  1. buyers can see that the cost fell;
  2. they can compare like with like;
  3. switching is cheap;
  4. they could do the work themselves with the same tools.
- **Who gains where the conditions are absent:** the supplier. **Where they are present:** the client.
- **Visible today.**
  - **Conditions present, price or volume fell:** translation, content moderation and IT run-work, where buyers can compare and insource (R-WEB-COMMERCIAL-05/07/12). Buyers are acting: an audit fee cut by about 14% (/01), and a bank cutting consultant spend by 24% (/15).
  - **Conditions absent, the supplier kept the gain:** US law rates rose (/13/14), and audit fees did not move in ten years of better tools (R-LIB-ECONOMICS-29).
  - **Insourcing:** AT&T took junior legal work in-house with its own team using AI, and plans to cut its panel of firms (R-LIB-ECONOMICS-15/16).
- **How fast comparable shifts went.** Fixed-price contracts delay pass-through to renewal (R-WEB-COMMERCIAL-04). Machine translation took roughly a decade, from the mid-2010s, to press prices broadly (general knowledge).
- **Strongest counter-argument.** Regulation, inertia and consolidation can hold prices up for a long time. Local consolidators kept gains through price until a competition authority stepped in (R-WEB-OWNERSHIP-25).
- **What follows.** Map each service on the four conditions. Commoditisation is a schedule, set by renewal cycles and buyer behaviour, not a single event.

### Q2. AI collapses the information gap from the buyer's side
**Tier: mechanism; bet on how fast it reaches complex work.**

- **Claim.** Economists class much professional work as credence goods: the buyer cannot tell, even afterwards, whether the work was good or necessary. That gap protected margins and justified the pyramid. The client's own AI narrows it. It can check a return, compare a contract with market terms, judge whether a repair was needed, or read a firm's track record.
- **Mechanism.** When buyers can verify, they pay for verified quality, not for reputation and hours. Premiums based on opacity disappear, and premiums based on demonstrable outcomes survive.
- **Who gains:** clients, and firms with good, provable records. **Who loses:** firms whose margin came from the client not knowing.
- **Visible today.**
  - One in five US accounting clients say they have used AI to check their accountant, and 93% want to be told when AI is used (R-WEB-COMMERCIAL-25; vendor survey, one origin).
  - Partners say clients' AI drafts take longer to fix (R-LIB-ECONOMICS-17; anecdote). That is the friction of a buyer who now has a view.
- **Strongest counter-argument.**
  - For complex matters the client's AI may be confidently wrong, adding work rather than discipline.
  - Most clients won't bother to check.
  - Checking does not remove liability. Someone still has to carry it, so the premium for accountability survives even when the information gap closes.
- **What follows.**
  - Assume that by the early 2030s, routine work is judged by clients' agents against published benchmarks.
  - The firm's track record must be provable.
  - Margin must come from what the client's AI cannot provide: carrying the consequence, and judgement where verification is genuinely impossible.

### Q3. Mandated and discretionary services diverge
**Tier: mechanism.**

- **Claim.** When the price of a service falls:
  - **Mandated services:** for services the law requires (annual filings, statutory inspections, regulatory returns), demand hardly grows. The volume is fixed by the rule. The fall in price goes to clients, or to whoever sits at the point of sale.
  - **Discretionary services:** for advice, debt recovery, monitoring, claims or legal action on small matters, much more is bought. Cases that were uneconomic become viable (A003). A £2 chaser makes a £300 debt worth pursuing (Garfield, R-LIB-DELIVERY-30).
- **Who gains.** In mandated services, clients and distribution owners. In discretionary services, the volume leader, which serves the new demand.
- **Strongest counter-argument.**
  - Mandated services expand in scope when rules tighten. New regimes such as Making Tax Digital, Awaab's Law and AI regulation add work.
  - Discretionary demand may not expand as theory predicts. Many small businesses don't pursue debts for reasons unrelated to cost.
- **What follows.**
  - A market leader built on growth should favour discretionary services with large latent demand.
  - In mandated services, the leader is the lowest-cost operator holding the point of sale. Or it accepts a supplier role behind someone who does.

### Q4. Value moves to whoever owns the point of need
**Tier: mechanism; bet on who that is by 2035.**

- **Claim.** When a service becomes a commodity, the margin moves to whoever the client meets first when the need arises.
  - **Today:** the bank account, the accounting or payroll software, the property portal, the insurer.
  - **By 2035:** possibly also the client's own agent, and whoever supplies it.
- **Mechanism.** These players bundle the commodity service, or choose its supplier and take a share. The pure services firm becomes a white-label supplier.
- **Visible today.** Labs are setting up deployment companies (N055). Software vendors and banks are adding "done for you" tiers (unit 2, call 1; not checked).
- **Strongest counter-argument.**
  - Distribution owners avoid regulated liability and complex cases. They want a partner to carry those, which leaves the accountable firm a bargaining position.
  - Fragmented small clients may stay loyal to a person.
- **What follows.** A firm has three choices:
  - own a point of need in a segment;
  - become the preferred accountable partner to a distribution owner;
  - serve segments the distribution owners can't: multi-entity, regulated, complex.

  Holding the client's live state is the main defence. A firm that is the client's operating system is hard to bundle away.

### Q5. What stays scarce when both sides have AI
**Tier: mechanism for each; the strength varies.**

| Scarce good | Why it stays scarce | Strongest counter-argument |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Carrying the consequence** (licence, insurance, balance sheet) | Liability needs capital and authorisation; regulators insist on a named, accountable person | Insurers or distribution owners take the risk themselves; regulation loosens |
| **Judgement where nobody can verify** | Novel, contested or strategic matters have no checkable answer, even to a machine | The frontier of "novel" shrinks every year; much of today's judgement turns out to be pattern |
| **Moving other parties** | Courts, authorities, contractors and counterparties move at their own pace, and standing and relationships matter | Counterparties automate too, and coordination becomes machine-to-machine |
| **Physical capacity** | Trades, inspectors and clinicians are scarce, and robots are slow to arrive | Physical work is priced separately and the coordination margin thins |
| **The client's live state** | Switching a system of record is painful and risky | Data portability rules and open banking cut switching costs |
| **Standing** (gateways, panels, recognition) | Institutions admit few, slowly | Institutions open access to agents directly |
| **Trust in a crisis** | Death, divorce, insolvency, an enquiry: people want a person | A share of people will accept an agent with a good record; generational change |
| **Advocacy in contests** | When both sides have AI, total spend rises in an arms race (Alloway; R-LIB-ECONOMICS-37) | Institutions cap machine-generated volume or price it out |

### Q6. The middle is squeezed: a barbell, over years rather than months
**Tier: mechanism for direction; general knowledge for speed.**

- **Claim.** Firms that sell bundled capacity plus modest know-how by the hour lose from both ends. The commodity tier undercuts them on price, and the accountable and judgement tier takes the valuable work.
- **What comparable shifts suggest about speed** (general knowledge):
  - High-street travel agents declined over roughly fifteen years after online booking arrived, and survived in complex and luxury travel.
  - Tax software has been widely available for over thirty years, yet about half of US taxpayers still pay a preparer.
  - Translation prices fell over about a decade.

  The pattern: the routine tier goes over five to fifteen years; an assisted, trusted tier persists; and the survivors move to complexity and accountability.
- **Strongest counter-argument.** AI is more general and moves faster than those technologies, so the timescale could be compressed to a few years. That is plausible for informational work. Institutional and buyer behaviour will still set the pace.
- **What follows.** Design for a window of roughly five to ten years in which the routine tier still pays. Use it to build the scarce layers before the margin goes.

## 3. What this means for a 2035 market leader

1. **Price the commodity tier near cost, deliberately.** It wins the relationship, the live records and the volume of cases and outcomes that make the scarce layers priceable (step 1, P3). Competitors that hold its price high get bundled away.
2. **Earn the margin on the scarce layers:** guarantees and warranties, the judgement desk, coordination where the firm holds the network and the standing, and crisis support.
3. **Choose where demand grows when price falls,** or own the point of need. In mandated work, either be the lowest-cost operator holding distribution, or be the accountable partner behind the distribution owner.
4. **Make the record provable.** Clients' agents will judge on outcomes, so the track record must be measurable and published.
5. **Plan for a window, not a moat,** of roughly five to ten years on the routine tier. A leader in 2035 has converted that window into the scarce layers.

## 4. Parameters this hands to step 3

Each service will be placed on these to find its region on the map and the form that wins there:

- How far the buyer can check the work, with AI (the credence level)
- Whether demand is mandated or discretionary, and how elastic it is
- Who owns the point of need
- How much liability the work carries, and whether a licence is required
- The share of coordination and physical work
- Whether the work is adversarial
- How fast outcomes can be checked (from step 1)
- Who holds the client's live state

## 5. Where this is weakest

- **Buyer-side checking of complex work is a bet.** If clients' AI stays unreliable on complex matters, the credence premium survives longer.
- **The barbell timescale rests on unchecked historical comparisons,** and AI may be faster.
- **The claim that accountability stays scarce assumes regulators keep requiring named persons.** If they shift to certifying systems, the signature could commoditise too.
- **Who owns the point of need by 2035 is genuinely uncertain.** Banks, software vendors and the suppliers of clients' agents are all candidates.
