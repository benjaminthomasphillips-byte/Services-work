## The answer in brief

The standard view, which I hold but which is not new: routine work is priced near cost, the accountable and judgement tier carries a premium, the middle is squeezed, and it takes years rather than months. The more useful points are these:

1. **"Professional services" is three different products, and AI does something different to each.**
   - **Routine execution** (filings, bookkeeping, standard documents) falls in price as cost falls and buyers compare. Buyers already find out whether it worked.
   - **Diagnosis** (what is needed, whether the repair or the structure is necessary) is where clients could never check the expert. A client's own AI attacks this first, and the effect shows up as less work being bought, not just lower prices. Revenue that depended on over-servicing goes.
   - **Residual judgement** (genuinely uncertain, contested matters) keeps a premium. That premium is protected by liability, reputation and regulation, not by the client being unable to see.
2. **Trust does not disappear; it moves to whoever supplies the client's checker.** If clients rely on their own AI to judge their advisers, the supplier of that AI, or a ratings layer built on it, becomes a new gatekeeper. That is a point of power the services firm does not hold today.
3. **Carrying risk on its own is a commodity, and it earns roughly the cost of capital, as insurance does.** What earns more is carrying risk *and* controlling the work that creates the risk. Only the party running the engine can drive the error rate down, and so price the risk below anyone else. "Accountability stays scarce" is true only in that bundled form.
4. **In mandated work, the state and the software vendors take much of the value.** For work the law requires, the pool shrinks. The state pre-fills or provides it, and value moves to the software the state's rules require. Making Tax Digital is the UK example. A services firm keeps the complex and accountable part.
5. **The squeezed middle releases experienced professionals just when a builder needs them, and the junior pipeline closes just when they will be scarce.** There is a talent window from about 2027 to 2031: hire experienced people from collapsing mid-sized firms to run the judgement desk and own the evaluator. Build training now for the shortage that follows.
6. **The strategy for the routine tier is a capital choice with three versions,** not "price it near cost". Which one applies depends on whether clients' agents buy services as a bundle or one layer at a time, and on who already holds the client's live records.

## 1. Three products inside "professional services"

| Product | What the buyer can tell | What a client's AI changes | What happens to it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Routine execution:** filings, bookkeeping, payroll, standard documents, routine claims | Whether it worked: the filing was accepted, the payment posted (an "experience good") | Little on judging quality; a lot on comparing price and on doing it in-house | Price falls with cost, at the pace of comparison and renewal |
| **Diagnosis:** what work is needed, whether a repair is necessary, whether a structure suits, whether to litigate | Not even afterwards (a "credence good", where the risk is over-servicing) | It gives the client a second opinion on whether the work is needed at all | Less work is bought. Over-servicing and upselling based on diagnosis shrink |
| **Residual judgement:** novel, contested, high-stakes matters with no checkable answer | Not, and neither can a machine | Little. The client's AI is as unsure as anyone | A premium survives, protected by liability, reputation and regulation |

The first draft treated all of this as credence goods and said premiums based on opacity disappear. That was wrong in two ways:
- Routine execution was never mainly a credence good.
- The judgement premium the draft kept is itself a premium for unverifiable work. What protects it is not opacity but the fact that someone credible carries the consequence.

## 2. The premises

### Q1. A fall in cost becomes a fall in price where buyers can compare, switch or do the work themselves; this is a property of buyer segments
**Tier: mechanism, thinly supported.**

- **Claim.** Lower supplier cost becomes a lower price where buyers can see it, compare like with like, switch cheaply, or bring the work in-house with the same tools. These are properties of the buyer more than of the service. The same audit sees its fee cut when the buyer is KPMG, and not when the buyer is an owner-managed company.
- **Who gains.** Large buyers with procurement capability first; small buyers later, as their agents learn to compare.
- **Visible today.**
  - Buyers with capability act. One cut its audit fee by about 14% (R-WEB-COMMERCIAL-01; one contract). A bank cut consultant spend by 24% (/15). AT&T brought junior legal work in-house (R-LIB-ECONOMICS-15/16).
  - Rising US law rates (/13/14) sit alongside work moving in-house, so higher rates do not mean higher bills.
  - Some suppliers kept their margin by cutting their own costs, passing gains through as more scope for the same price rather than lower prices (R-WEB-COMMERCIAL-04/12).
- **How fast comparable shifts went** (general knowledge):
  - US brokerage commissions were deregulated in 1975 and fell in steps over decades, reaching zero for retail trades around 2019.
  - UK insurance comparison sites spread from the 2000s, yet insurers charged loyal customers more on renewal than new ones ("price walking") until the regulator banned it in 2022.

  Comparison alone does not remove the margin that inertia protects.
- **Strongest counter-argument.** Small clients may never use agents to shop for professional services. Inertia and relationship keep prices for a long time.
- **What follows.** Map buyer segments, not just services. Enterprise work commoditises first; owner-managed clients and households follow at the pace their agents and habits allow.

### Q2. Clients' AI attacks diagnosis first, and the effect is less work, not just lower prices
**Tier: mechanism; bet on speed.**

- **Claim.** Where the expert decides what work is needed, the client has always had to trust them. That gap shows up mainly as work done that did not need doing, not as high margins. A client's AI that gives a second opinion on necessity cuts the volume of billed work. Fewer repairs get ordered, fewer structures get set up, fewer cases go to litigation.
- **Who gains:** clients. **Who loses:** firms whose revenue depends on diagnosis-led upselling. Firms paid for outcomes or by subscription gain, because they never had the incentive to over-service.
- **Visible today.**
  - One in five US accounting clients say they have used AI to check their accountant (R-WEB-COMMERCIAL-25; vendor survey). The same survey says 53% would pay more for faster service, so checking does not simply mean pressure on price.
  - Partners report clients arriving with AI drafts (R-LIB-ECONOMICS-17; anecdote).
- **Strongest counter-argument.**
  - The client's AI checks against rules and typical practice. Step 1 showed that a check against conformance does not establish quality, so on complex matters it may be confidently wrong and add work.
  - Most clients will not bother.
- **What follows.**
  - Prefer business models where the firm gains from doing less work for the same outcome: subscriptions to maintained conditions, outcome fees, warranties.
  - Avoid acquiring firms whose margin depends on diagnosis-led volume.

### Q3. Trust moves to the supplier of the client's checker
**Tier: mechanism for the shift; bet on who wins it.**

- **Claim.** If clients rely on AI to judge their advisers, they must trust that AI. Whoever supplies it becomes the new gatekeeper. That could be:
  - the model provider;
  - the bank or software vendor that embeds it;
  - a ratings or comparison layer built on published outcomes.

  Power that used to sit in the adviser's reputation moves to whoever certifies the adviser.
- **Who gains:** the checker's supplier. **Who loses:** firms that depend on unverifiable reputation.
- **How fast comparable shifts went** (general knowledge). Credit ratings, the Which? product tests and online review platforms show that certification layers capture lasting power. They also carry conflicts of interest and can be gamed.
- **Strongest counter-argument.** Clients may trust a person over any checker, and checkers may fragment so that none gains power.
- **What follows.**
  - A leader must be legible to checkers: publish outcomes in a form that can be verified.
  - It should consider whether it can be, or partner with, the trusted checker in its domain. The conflict of interest is real, so this may have to be a separate entity.

### Q4. Carrying risk alone is a commodity; carrying risk while controlling the work is not
**Tier: mechanism for the economics; bet for how liability law moves after about 2030.**

- **Claim.** "Accountability stays scarce" is too broad. Three things can happen to it:
  1. **It becomes underwriting.** Pure risk-bearing in a competitive market earns about the cost of capital, as insurance does. Title insurance replaced lawyers' title opinions in the US, and its value lies in the search process and distribution more than in the risk (general knowledge).
  2. **It migrates to the makers of systems.** Product liability is being extended to software in the EU. A US regulator cleared an autonomous diagnostic AI in 2018, with its maker carrying responsibility for the output (general knowledge).
  3. **It is compressed.** If one licensed person signs for a whole system (step 1, P6), far fewer signatures are needed, and licences become less scarce.

  What survives is risk carried by the party that also controls the work. Only the operator of the engine can reduce the error rate, so only it can price the risk below a pure insurer and still profit. The margin is in the bundle of risk and operations.
- **Who gains.** Integrated operators with an engine, outcome data and capital. Pure insurers earn the cost of capital. Pure licence-holders lose scarcity.
- **Visible today.** Regulators hold firms and their managers accountable for AI-produced work (R-WEB-LAW-16). That does not require a named person to approve each step.
- **Strongest counter-argument.**
  - Insurers can buy or partner with operators and capture the bundle themselves.
  - Regulators may forbid services firms from warranting outcomes without an insurance licence.
- **What follows.** The design bets on integrated risk and operations: guarantees priced from the firm's own error data, backed by capital or a partner insurer. It does not bet on the licence alone. This links to step 1, P3: the fast engine feeds the slow, risk-bearing business.

### Q5. In mandated work, the state and the software its rules require take much of the value
**Tier: trend in direction; bet on how far it goes by 2035.**

- **Claim.** For work the law requires, two forces shrink the pool available to firms:
  - **The state provides or pre-fills it:** pre-populated tax returns, digital registries, government casework tools.
  - **The state's rules move value to software:** Making Tax Digital requires compatible software, so value moves to the vendors.

  The firm keeps the complex cases, the accountable sign-off, and clients who will not do it themselves.
- **Visible today** (general knowledge, plus unit 2's unchecked call 1):
  - HMRC pre-populates returns and Making Tax Digital is being phased in.
  - The IRS ran a free direct-filing pilot, which was then cut back. That shows how politically contested state provision is: incumbents lobby against it.
- **Strongest counter-argument.**
  - New regulation keeps adding mandated work faster than the state automates it: Making Tax Digital's quarterly updates, Awaab's Law, AI compliance duties.
  - Incumbents lobby to keep their share.
- **What follows.**
  - In mandated work, size the pool after the state and software take their share.
  - The firm's place is the complex and accountable remainder, or partnering with the software that the rules make compulsory.

### Q6. Elasticity sets the size of the pool; competition decides who gets the saving
**Tier: mechanism.**

- **Claim.** The first draft ran these together. They are separate:
  - **How much more gets bought when the price falls.** For mandated work, the revenue pool shrinks as price falls. For discretionary work (advice, recovery, monitoring, small claims) it may grow, as cases that were uneconomic become viable.
  - **Who keeps the saving** is set separately, by competition.
- **Visible today.** Garfield prices a debt chaser at £2 (R-LIB-DELIVERY-30). That shows a price, not that demand grew.
- **Strongest counter-argument.** Discretionary demand may not grow as theory predicts. Many debts go unpursued for reasons unrelated to cost. Institutions may also throttle machine-made volume: tribunals and courts ration or charge (R-LIB-ECONOMICS-37 records a sharp rise in tribunal applications, imported and unverified).
- **What follows.** For a growth strategy, test real elasticity in a pilot before betting on it. Expect institutions to cap volume where it lands on them.

### Q7. Who captures the surplus

My reasoning, by party:

| Party | What it captures | When it wins most |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Clients** | Most of the saving on routine execution; less over-servicing | Where they can compare and switch (Q1, Q2) |
| **Distribution owners** (banks, software vendors, portals) | A share of commodity services bundled into their products | Where they own the point of need and want to avoid liability |
| **Suppliers of clients' checkers** | Power as gatekeepers; perhaps fees | If clients come to trust a checker over a person (Q3) |
| **Model providers** | A slice of every case through usage fees; possibly direct entry into services through deployment companies (N055) | Where the work is mostly informational and unregulated |
| **Insurers** | Premiums for carrying risk, at about the cost of capital | If operators do not bundle risk with operations (Q4) |
| **The state** | Lower administrative costs; takes over some mandated work | In mandated compliance (Q5) |
| **Individual professionals** | High pay for scarce judgement, crisis trust and standing in court | In a shortage, likely in the 2030s (Q8) |
| **Integrated operators** | Margin on bundled risk and operations; growth in discretionary demand | With an engine, outcome data, capital, and a point of need or partner (step 1, P3; Q4) |

### Q8. Scarce goods: what a firm can own, what stays with individuals, what stays with institutions

| Held by the firm | Held by individuals | Held by institutions |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Live records of the client's affairs, where not already held by a vendor | Judgement on genuinely uncertain matters | Gateways and access rights (agent credentials, registries) |
| The engine, its test suites and outcome data | Trust in a crisis; personal relationships | Licences and authorisations |
| Capital and the ability to warrant outcomes | Standing in court and with regulators | Panel memberships and recognition |
| A network of doers with performance data | | Rules that cap or price volume |
| A published, verifiable track record | | |

**The labour market in two phases** (mechanism; my reasoning):

1. **About 2027 to 2031.** Squeezed mid-sized firms release experienced professionals, and their pay softens.
2. **The early to mid 2030s.** With the junior pipeline gone (ledger, Part 7), people with real judgement are scarce, and their pay rises.

- **What follows.**
  - Hire the experienced generation early, to own the evaluator and run the judgement desk.
  - Build training now as a moat against the shortage.
  - Give key individuals equity, because the scarce goods they hold walk out of the door otherwise.

### Q9. Valuation and geography

- **Valuation** (mechanism):
  - **A margin from carrying risk** is valued like an insurer's, nearer to book value.
  - **A margin from owning records, distribution and the engine** is valued like software.
  - **Mid-sized firms selling hours** are the assets whose value is melting.

  So roll-up targets in the squeezed middle should be bought for their clients and priced as customer acquisition, not on profit multiples. This is the argument for buying books of clients from unit 2, restated with its reason.
- **International** (mechanism):
  - AI ends the labour-cost advantage of offshore delivery (Kapoor, discovery sweep), so work that was offshored may come back onshore to AI-native firms.
  - Jurisdiction works both ways. Local law and licences protect a firm, and cap its scale. A 2035 leader in regulated services either holds licences in several jurisdictions or picks a domain where rules converge.

## 3. What this means for a 2035 market leader

There are three strategies for the routine tier. Which applies is a choice of capital and position, not a rule.

| Strategy | What it does | Applies when | Fails when |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **A. Volume and records** | Price routine work near cost to win clients, their live records and outcome data; earn the margin on risk and judgement | The firm can own the point of need, clients buy as a bundle, and the records are not already held by a vendor | Clients' agents buy each layer separately and cherry-pick, or the vendor already holds the records |
| **B. Accountable partner** | Be the regulated, risk-bearing operator behind a bank or software vendor that owns distribution | Distribution owners want to avoid liability and complexity | The partner takes the risk in-house or squeezes the price |
| **C. Harvest the window** | Price routine work at market while it still pays, and use the cash to build the risk, judgement and coordination layers | The routine tier will keep paying for five to ten years in the chosen segment | The window closes faster than expected |

Points that hold under any of the three:

1. **Choose models where doing less work for the same outcome pays:** subscriptions to maintained conditions, outcome fees, warranties.
2. **Bundle risk with control of the work.** Do not rely on the licence alone.
3. **Be legible to clients' checkers.** Publish verifiable outcomes.
4. **Hold the client's live records where they are fragmented across systems.** In multi-entity affairs, or property compliance involving several parties, nobody yet holds the whole picture. A firm that holds the view above the systems is hard to displace.
5. **Use the talent window.** Hire the experienced generation now and build the training route for the shortage.
6. **Size mandated pools after the state's and software's share.** Look for growth in discretionary demand, tested in a pilot.

**A counter to keep in view** (general knowledge): robo-advice was largely captured by incumbents with distribution, such as Vanguard and Schwab, rather than by the start-ups that pioneered it. In services, the incumbent with distribution may adopt the engine and win. A new firm needs a reason the incumbent cannot follow: liability it will not take, a segment it cannot serve, or speed.

## 4. Parameters this hands to step 3

- The mix of routine execution, diagnosis and residual judgement
- Buyer segment: enterprise, owner-managed or household, and how far each compares and insources
- Mandated or discretionary demand, and elasticity
- How far the state, and software required by rules, can take the work
- Who owns the point of need, and who holds the client's live records
- How much liability the work carries, and whether risk can be bundled with operations
- The share of coordination and physical work
- Whether the work is adversarial, and whether institutions cap volume
- How fast outcomes can be checked (step 1)
- Jurisdiction: local, or convergent across countries

## 5. Where this is weakest

- **The three-product split is mine,** and real services mix all three in one engagement. The proportions will be estimates.
- **The labour-market phases are reasoned, not observed.** The glut could be smaller if mid-sized firms adapt.
- **The claim about who gains from accountability depends on liability law,** which could move either way after about 2030.
- **The historical comparisons are unchecked.** A short research pass on brokerage, robo-advice, price walking, title insurance and travel agents would firm them up. Each shows a different mechanism, which is why I use several.
